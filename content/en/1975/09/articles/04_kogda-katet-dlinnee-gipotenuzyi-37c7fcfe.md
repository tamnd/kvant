## When the leg is longer than the hypotenuse...: a conversation with ex-world chess champion Mikhail Tal

Mathematics and chess have many similarities. The forms of thinking of a mathematician and a chess player are quite similar, and it is no coincidence that mathematical abilities are often combined with chess ones. It is enough to mention the names of world chess champions. The first chess king, W. Steinitz, was interested in mathematics. A famous mathematician was his successor, Dr. Em. Lasker. The current FIDE president, ex-world champion M. Euwe, headed the largest computer center in Holland. The first Soviet world champion, Doctor of Technical Sciences M. Botvinnik, has been working on a chess game algorithm for a computer in recent years.

Many prominent mathematicians showed purely scientific interest in the chess topic. As you know, the great mathematician Carl Gauss studied the problem of “arranging eight queens,” and another great mathematician Leonard Euler worked on the problem of “the move of a knight.” Nowadays, mathematicians from different countries work in the field of chess programming (of course, the mathematical methods developed in this case can be used to solve other, more pressing problems). The chessboard, the pieces, and the game itself are often used to illustrate a variety of mathematical concepts and problems.

Thus, the publication in «Kvant» of various articles and problems involving chess is a completely natural phenomenon. We are sure that most readers who are passionate about mathematics and physics also love to play chess. By the way, in the Moscow State University chess championships, students from the Faculty of Mechanics and Mathematics win more often than others, and only recently have they had serious competitors, and those are students from the Faculty of Cybernetics.

Today «Kvant» with great pleasure provides its pages to one of the most popular chess players of our time, ex-world champion Mikhail Tal. Our regular “chess and mathematics” author Evgeniy Gik interviewed the grandmaster.

Meeting Tal was not an easy task. For several months in a row, he moved from one international tournament to another (and took first place in all of them!). They managed to “catch” the grandmaster in Leningrad on the last free day of the 42nd national championship (major league). During the conversation, Tal admitted that for the first time in his life he was giving an interview to a correspondent of a special physics and mathematics journal...

**— Mikhail Nekhemievich, I promise you not to touch the Fischer-Karpov match today. My first question is somewhat related to mathematics. Is it true that you had exceptional math skills in school and constantly won math quizzes?**

- Of course, it’s difficult for me to judge my own abilities. But, as far as I remember, I solved algebraic problems almost instantly.

**—What about geometric ones?**

— Things were worse with geometry. In it, as I was taught, the most important thing is the drawing. In my drawings, no matter how hard I tried, the leg always turned out to be longer than the hypotenuse.

**- It's clear. This means that your calling was algebra, and you had to become an algebraist. How did your mathematical career develop later?**

“The math teacher couldn’t believe that I solved problems so quickly.” She had no doubt that I was copying the answers from the problem book and constantly gave me bad marks. This went on for two years, until finally my parents couldn’t stand it and transferred me to another school. As a result of such upheavals, the love for mathematics cooled noticeably, and the path to chess was completely “cleared.”

**—My first chess question is this. Why do people even play chess, and for many this game becomes the meaning of life?**

- It all starts simple. A child encounters chess for the first time somewhere—at home or at school. He starts playing and at first he just wants to win - against his brother, friend, neighbor. The spirit of competition captivates him. After all, chess is first and foremost a competition. No one thinks about a sports career first. Struggling. Rivalry draws you in and you become infected with the chess germ. Some people tolerate this disease more easily; chess occupies a certain place in their lives - important, but not the most important. Others become chronically ill. A qualitative change is coming. What was fun, a game, a pleasant pastime, becomes a passion - an absolutely important matter.

**— Let chess become, as you say, an absolutely important matter in life for a young man. Should he, in this case, strive for higher education?**

— First of all, it seems to me that at the age of 17 it is still difficult to decide what place chess will take in life. But let us still assume that the decision to become a chess player is final and irrevocable. Should I then spend time on other activities?

I am convinced that chess can and should become professional. The Botvinnik phenomenon is not at all typical for our time. Nowadays grandmasters have to deal with a huge flow of chess information. And if not swallow, then at least taste every particle of this information. In order to achieve great success, you need to play a lot and work on chess. With today's busy calendar, it is simply physically impossible to successfully combine chess with another profession.

⟦figure⟧

But there is no doubt that general development, a general cultural level is absolutely necessary for a chess player. Any major grandmaster has a high culture and broad erudition. Fischer is often cited as an exception. It may be difficult to call him an encyclopedist, but he is not a person, so to speak, with a low intellectual quotient. Suffice it to remember that he is fluent in many languages. And his “strange” actions are more likely related to his character, his view of chess, etc.

Most of my colleagues write a lot and have become professional writers. And, perhaps, it is no coincidence that former MSTU graduate student Yuri Averbakh and talented engineer Alexander Kotov have now become famous chess writers. By the way, I also graduated from the Faculty of Philology at the University of Riga and worked as a teacher of Russian language and literature at school for six months. I won’t lie, I taught with great pleasure, but after my students had “windows” in two quarters for several months, I was forced to leave school.

**—So, you think that the wisest thing to do is to first try to combine chess with another profession and only later make a final decision. How long do you think this combination is possible?**

— I think that, at least up to the master’s level, combining chess with study or work in another field is quite possible.

**— Taking into account the readership of the magazine, let's try to draw a parallel between the professions of a mathematician and a chess player. Both mathematics and chess require constant and intense mental effort. There is an opinion that 8 hours is the daily minimum for doing mathematics. “A truly virtuous person,” as the famous English mathematician J. Littlewood writes with a bit of humor, must find 10 hours of work a day through sleep. Is it conceivable to play chess under such conditions? By the way, some professors avoid accepting mathematicians who play chess well into graduate school.**

— I can only agree that chess is a very dangerous related profession, and, apparently, especially for mathematics.

**—Don’t you think that the profession of a mathematician, so to speak, is “more reliable” than the profession of a chess player? If you do not become a major scientist in the field of “pure” mathematics, you can find yourself in a related profession, becoming an “applied scientist.” In chess, sporting success comes first. And if by the age of thirty a chess player begins to understand that the audience will never burst into applause in response to his moves, and in Palma de Mallorca he is not destined to shine with the sacrifice of a queen, that his youthful dreams have not come true, then what remains for him in the chess world?**

- Naturally, I am biased and cannot objectively evaluate what I have devoted my whole life to. In my opinion, every person who loves chess can find a place for themselves in the chess world. Many of those who do not achieve outstanding results in practical play become coaches, commentators, and theorists.

⟦figure⟧

Of course, in chess, as in any other sport, success and points are valued above all. If you do not show great results, do not fight for the world championship, then in the eyes of others you may look like a loser, and this must be taken into account. Alas, the chess profession cannot yet be classified as prestigious. People who are far from chess often ask grandmasters what they do and what their main profession is. That is why I would not risk recommending the chess path to a young man who has both mathematical and chess abilities and “chooses life.” No prophet can guarantee you high chess achievements, and you can devote yourself entirely to chess only if you love it endlessly and are ready to “sacrifice” public opinion in the name of this love. I also warn you that chess is a very nervous activity, and a chess player’s bread is not always sweet.

**— There is a certain competition in science, as a result of which everyone wins. In chess, only the winner triumphs. Recently, there has even been a theory according to which, in order to win before a game or match, you need to specifically evoke a feeling of anger towards your opponent...**

— The goal of a chess game is to defeat your opponent. A chess player without character, soft, weak-willed, has no chance. A chess player is first and foremost a fighter. And in struggle the spirit of aggression is natural. But still, more often than not, the field of this aggressiveness is limited to the chessboard and is not transferred to the opponent’s personality. Spassky once compared chess players to gladiators, but gladiators with gloves are no longer exactly gladiators. In the end, in chess, after a fight, both sides often remain alive... By the way, Spassky himself is a very gentle person in life and his hostility towards his opponent rather hinders him. He plays excellently with those with whom he has friendship or at least good relations, and plays weaker with those whom he treats with antipathy. For myself, I also can’t say that I “charge up” before the game. And at times, when I like my partner and I know that this game is very important for him for some reason, it is difficult for me to gather myself and play to my full potential. I think this is rather my minus.

Science is not a sport, and ideally, apparently, there should not be such “toughening” as in sports. In our country, even the title of grandmaster does not guarantee a quiet life. Eternal struggle, eternal selection, competition. But I must say that chess players have joys that are not available to people of other professions. Having played one beautiful game, even if not in a very large tournament, you can leave a mark on chess history for many years. Even if a chess player is haunted by sporting failures, he can be proud of individual achievements and live in the beauty of chess. For him, every tournament has a prize “for the most beautiful game” (even if this prize is not officially established).

⟦figure⟧

There is a lot of room in the world of chess for all kinds of people. Kruskop, my first teacher - now they say “coach”, before they used to say “teacher”, and I liked it better - was not an outstanding chess player. He had the first category. But he was just a teacher, an educator, he loved chess and loved children. And it is no coincidence that many of his students devoted their lives to chess in one way or another. I think this man was happy. He showed children the beauty of chess...

**— Mikhail Nekhemievich, already at the age of 23 you entered the golden chronicle of chess. Recognition, fame, admiration - you achieved all this very early. How does a person feel when he reaches the top?**

— A chess player, like, apparently, a mathematician, can never tell himself that he has achieved everything. That would be the end. Indeed, after winning a major tournament, after winning the world title, a moment of emptiness comes, it becomes a little boring. But time passes, and the appetite for the fight awakens, and a chess hunger is felt. New tournaments, new plans - not only sporting, but also creative. Fortunately, the chess hierarchy is changeable. Today you are a world champion, tomorrow you are already an ex-champion. There is always something to fight for.

**—You can’t say that your chess path was strewn only with roses. Has it ever happened that chess bothered you and caused irritation? Have you ever regretted that you gave your whole life to them?**

— Yesterday, during a game with grandmaster Vaganyan, there was a similar moment. The opponent moved his knight to g1, and suddenly it turned out that I no longer had a win... I was incredibly upset. Speaking seriously, you see, chess is my world. Not a house, not a fortress in which I take refuge from life’s anxieties, but rather a world - a world in which I live a full life, in which I express myself... I love the atmosphere of matches, tournaments, chess discussions. I can't imagine myself on a desert island without a partner, unless Friday would have to play chess with me. I don't like playing behind closed doors. I love the public. The noise in the hall does not bother me. When the hall starts buzzing after my move, it inspires me - I know that I played interestingly.

⟦figure⟧

But the chess world is not closed for me. It is connected by many threads to other worlds. Many of my friends, perhaps the majority, do not play chess or play only amateurishly. And I also have a common language with them. Only my other interests - theater, literature, music - could never compete with chess; chess existed, and everything else existed next to it. That’s why I don’t regret anything, despite the defeats, setbacks, and difficulties. I don’t feel sorry for other prospects, unrealized opportunities. I just love playing chess.

**— Let me ask you two more, so to speak, mathematical questions. Do you think math skills can help in practical play?**

- Already when we first get acquainted with the chessboard, we are dealing with numbers - $8\times8=64$! I remember that I was once strongly impressed by a chess proof (using a chessboard) of the Pythagorean theorem. Many endgame positions are purely mathematical in nature. But I don’t find a direct connection between chess strength and mathematical abilities. True, in chess analysis, technical education can be useful. Let’s say, I can’t imagine how Yu. Averbakh would have been able to achieve such a clear systematization in his three-volume work on the endgame if he had not had experience in the exact sciences.

**—The first World Computer Chess Championship took place recently. Are you afraid of competition from machines?**

— True, I am not familiar with the technical side of the matter, but I am skeptical about the chess capabilities of computers. I doubt that she will be able to beat the masters. Probably, a chess machine will be useful for solving other, non-chess problems, but chess is too versatile, complex, and there are no clear answers...

Of course, this will not be said for «Kvant», but I must admit that I associate chess primarily not with mathematics, but with music. By type of reception, by type of experience. There is something very close here. For me, this similarity is so striking that some outstanding chess players are associated with composers. For example, Smyslov is Tchaikovsky: calm, melodious, melodious and occasionally a magnificent explosion that makes a huge impression, Petrosyan is Liszt, absolute virtuosity, Botvinnik, perhaps, is Bach, and Keres is Chopin...

**—And Fischer and Tal himself?**

- Fischer? An unusually talented musician, but unlike anyone else - a magnificent musical machine: after all, now machines not only solve problems, but also compose music. I'm most likely Gershwin, or maybe Kalman...

**— Mikhail Nekhemievich, my last question will still be related not to music or even mathematics, but to chess. You have already talked about the beauty of chess. How do you personally understand the beauty of chess?**

— For some, the beauty of chess is a triumph of logic. A wonderful lot, in their opinion, is a magnificent classical building with impeccable proportions, in which every element, every brick stands in its place. And although I also often “had to” win purely positional games, I am more attracted by the triumph of illogicality, irrationality, absurdity: on the board there is a fierce struggle, subordinated to some idea, a struggle to implement a certain plan, and the outcome of the struggle is decided by an innocent pawn that has nothing to do with the main motive of the drama. In mathematical terms, I like it better when the leg in chess is longer than the hypotenuse.
