## Home task

— I love solving difficult problems. I love looking for new proofs of theorems
and read books about scientists. I study with pleasure, at the school Olympiad
received a prize. I would like to become a scientist. But can I become one? Am I fit to be a scientist? How to test yourself?

Unfortunately, there are no signs by which one could recognize the future
scientist, no. Success in school does not at all guarantee that the same success will be
for a university graduate: the student solves what is given, assimilates other people’s ideas
thoughts; the scientist sets his own tasks. Even if the student is good at conducting
logical reasoning, this does not mean that he is fit to be a scientist. Skill
It is really necessary for a scientist to reason well, except in those cases when
precisely a leap in logical construction - that is, not logic, but guesswork -
provide a solution to the problem. And so on.

But although studying at school is not at all similar to scientific work, some
The qualities necessary for a scientist can be developed even by preparing lessons. Necessary
Just, having solved the next problem, do not rush to move on to the next one. One more time
re-read the condition, look at the answer received - and give free rein to your
imagination... Ask, for example, yourself: what else could it be? Is it possible to solve more difficult problems based on the one you just solved?

However, let’s better listen to the conversation between the two guys. They study in the same class
and today they are preparing lessons together.

**D.** What were we given in algebra?

**O.** Prove that if the numbers are $\dfrac1{b+c}$, $\dfrac1{c+a}$,
$\dfrac1{a+b}$ form an arithmetic progression, then so do the numbers $a^2$,
$b^2$, $c^2$ also form an arithmetic progression.

**D.** In my opinion, the task is not difficult. Each member of the arithmetic
progression is the arithmetic mean of its two neighbors. Means,
$$
\dfrac12\left(\dfrac1{b+c}+\dfrac1{a+b}\right)=\dfrac1{c+a}.\tag1
$$
Let's get rid of fractions in (1), open the brackets, and present similar terms; we get
$$
a^2+c^2=2b^2,
$$
i.e.
$$
\dfrac12(a^2+c^2)=b^2,\tag2
$$
and this was what needed to be proven.

**Oh.** Yes, everything seems to be correct...

**D.** What else is scheduled for tomorrow?

**Oh.** Wait, let me think...

**D.** Over what?

**Oh.** On the problem we just solved.

**D.** What should we think about it if it has already been decided?

**O.** At first, only numbers formed a progression, and in the end - completely
other. What do $\dfrac1{b+c}$ and $a^2$ have in common? Why are they connected?

**D.** You saw why.

**Oh.** I can't really explain it, but something is missing here. What do you think, is the opposite true - what if $a^2$, $b^2$, $c^2$ are progressions,
then $\dfrac1{b+c}$, $\dfrac1{c+a}$, $\dfrac1{a+b}$ - again a progression?

**D.** Absolutely. How could it be otherwise? Do the calculations in the opposite direction
- everything will work out.

**Oh.** Well, it's not pretty. You know, let's try it with numbers.

**D.** How does this work in numbers? You can't prove anything with numbers.

**Oh.** ... let's take, for example, numbers
$$
a^2=1,\quad b^2=4,\quad c^2=7.\tag3
$$
They form a progression. Let's check that $\dfrac1{b+c}$, $\dfrac1{c+a}$,
$\dfrac1{a+b}$ is also a progression.

**D.** Why? It is clear that the test will succeed. I'm all about letters
proved. Nothing new will be added.

**Oh.** Well, I want to see exactly how everything turns out.
So, let there be numbers (3). Let's take only positive roots: $a=1$,
$b=2$, $c=\sqrt7$.

**D.**Calculate:
$$
\dfrac1{b+c}=\dfrac1{2+\sqrt7},\quad\dfrac1{c+a}=\dfrac1{\sqrt7+1},\quad
\dfrac1{a+b}=\dfrac13.
$$
Is this really a progression? However, let's get rid of the irrationality in the denominator:
$$
\dfrac1{b+c}=\dfrac{\sqrt7-2}3,\quad\dfrac1{c+a}=\dfrac{\sqrt7-1}6.
$$

**Oh.** Of course it's a progression. Sum of extreme members
$$
\dfrac{\sqrt7-2}3+\dfrac13=\dfrac{\sqrt7-1}3
$$
twice the average.

**D.** Yes; and the progression difference is equal to
$\dfrac13-\dfrac{\sqrt7-1}6=\dfrac{3-\sqrt7}6$.

**Oh.** Wait. Now I know how to prove what from (2)
follows (1). You just need to sort of get rid of the irrationality in the denominator.

**D.** How is this? After all, there is no irrationality there!

**Oh.** Of course not; but we must act as if it were.
Look:
$$
\dfrac1{b+c}=\dfrac{b-c}{b^2-c^2},\quad
\dfrac1{c+a}=\dfrac{c-a}{c^2-a^2},\quad
\dfrac1{a+b}=\dfrac{a-b}{a^2-b^2}.
$$
By convention, $a^2$, $b^2$, $c^2$ are progressions. So, $a^2-b^2=b^2-c^2$.

Let us denote this difference by $d$. Then $a^2-c^2=2d$. Numbers
$\dfrac{b-c}d$, $\dfrac{a-c}{2d}$, $\dfrac{a-b}d$ obviously form
progression.

**D.** Well, okay, what else did they ask?

**Oh.** Wait, now you understand how progressions are connected
$\dfrac1{b+c}$, $\dfrac1{c+a}$, $\dfrac1{a+b}$ and $a^2$, $b^2$, $c^2$?

**D.** I understood before.

**O.** Let $a$, $b$, $c$ be arbitrary numbers. Let's consider three
$b-c$, $\dfrac{a-c}2$, $a-b$ - this is already a progression. Members of this progression
can be divided by any number $d$. In the problem, $d$ is chosen so that division by it leads to the numbers $\dfrac1{b+c}$, $\dfrac1{c+a}$, $\dfrac1{a+b}$;
That’s why instead of arbitrary $a$, $b$, $c$ we need to take ones such that
$a^2-b^2=b^2-c^2=d$, i.e., so that the numbers $a^2$, $b^2$, $c^2$ make up
progression. But here the question arises again: can all three numbers $a$, $b$,
$c$ be whole?

**D.** Does it really matter whether they can or cannot?

**Oh.** So this is interesting! What if there is such a triple of integers
$a$, $b$, $c$, which $a^2$, $b^2$, $c^2$ is an arithmetic progression. And then the numbers $\dfrac1{b+c}$, $\dfrac1{c+a}$, $\dfrac1{a+b}$ will also turn out to be
progression. This is beautiful - two progressions and both from integers...

**D.** The second one will be a fraction, because if $a$ and $b$ are integers, then $\dfrac1{a+b}$ is a fraction.

**Oh.** Never mind. I want no roots to emerge. Let
$a$, $b$, $c$ will not even be integers, but rational ones - it’s still beautiful. But how to choose three rational numbers $a$, $b$, $c$ so that $a^2-b^2$ is
equal to $b^2-c^2$? After all, this is not an equation of the first degree, the roots may not be extracted... Wait, but we have an expression with the first degrees! This is (1).

**D.** I don’t understand.

**Oh.** Look. Let $\dfrac1{b+c}$, $\dfrac1{c+a}$, $\dfrac1{a+b}$ —
known progression. For example, 1, 3, 5. Or 100, 101, 102. From any
rational numbers. This means that we have a system of three equations of the first
degrees with three unknowns $a$, $b$, $c$. You can
decide. Decisions are rational again
numbers. And then $a^2$, $b^2$, $c^2$ will automatically form a rational
progression.

**D.** Come on, come on, let's try. Let, for example, there be a progression
1, 3, 5.

**O.** No, let's go straight to the general case: let's look at the progression
$\alpha-\beta$, $\alpha$, $\alpha+\beta$, where $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are
arbitrary numbers. To determine the numbers $a$, $b$, $c$ we get the following
system of equations:
$$
\begin{align*}
b+c&=\dfrac1{\alpha-\beta},\tag4\\
c+a&=\dfrac1\alpha,\tag5\\
a+b&=\dfrac1{\alpha+\beta}.\tag6
\end{align*}
$$

**D.** Let's add all the equations and divide the sum by two; we get
$$
a+b+c=\dfrac{3\alpha^2-\beta^2}{2\alpha(\alpha^2-\beta^2)}.\tag7
$$
Subtract from (7) sequentially (4), (5), (6); we get:
$$
a=\dfrac{\alpha^2-\beta^2-2\alpha\beta}{2\alpha(\alpha^2-\beta^2)},\quad
b=\dfrac{\alpha^2+\beta^2}{2\alpha(\alpha^2-\beta^2)},\quad
c=\dfrac{\alpha^2-\beta^2+2\alpha\beta}{2\alpha(\alpha^2-\beta^2)}.
$$

This means that no matter what $\alpha$ and $\beta$ we take, the numbers $a^2$, $b^2$, $c^2$, where $a$, $b$ and $c$ are calculated by these formulas, must form a progression. Let's check it out.

**Oh.** Now. Here's what I thought. For the numbers $a$, $b$ and $c$ we now have fractional expressions. Let's discard their denominators; then $a$, $b$,
$c$ will increase by the same number of times and become integers if,
of course, take $\alpha$ and $\beta$ whole. In this case, the numbers $a^2$, $b^2$, $c^2$
will still form a progression.

**D.** Of course. If 1, 3, 5 is a progression, then so are 10, 30, 50.

**O.** So, we have derived formulas for progressions $a^2$, $b^2$, $c^2$, for which $a$, $b$, $c$ are integers. Namely:
$$
\begin{align*}
a&=\alpha^2-\beta^2-2\alpha\beta,\tag8\\
b&=\alpha^2+\beta^2,\tag9\\
c&=\alpha^2-\beta^2+2\alpha\beta,\tag{10}
\end{align*}
$$
where $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are any integers.

**D.** Let's check. Let $\alpha=2$, $\beta=1$; Then
$$
\alpha^2-\beta^2=3,\quad2\alpha\beta=4,\quad\alpha^2+\beta^2=5.
\tag{11}
$$

**Oh.** Look, this is the Egyptian triangle...

**D.** This is random. Let's count further:
$$
a=3-4=-1,\quad b=5,\quad c=3+4=7.
$$
Indeed, 1, 25, 49 is a progression.

**Oh.** So you and I have learned to solve in whole numbers
indefinite equation
$$
a^2+c^2=2b^2;\tag{12}
$$
solutions are written by formulas (8)–(10).

**D.** Where does this equation come from?

**O.** So, after all, it is a short record of the condition that the numbers $a^2$, $b^2$ and $c^2$ form a progression.

**D.** Oh, I understand. It just hasn’t been proven that formulas (8)–(10) will give all the solutions to this equation.

**Oh.** Why? After all, this is clear; any solution to equation (12) is such that the numbers $\dfrac1{b+c}$, $\dfrac1{c+a}$, $\dfrac1{a+b}$ form a progression; and any progression can be represented as $\alpha-\beta$, $\alpha$,
$\alpha+\beta$. Having sorted through all possible pairs $(\alpha,\beta)$, we obtain all solutions to equation (12). It's clear?

**D.** I think so.

**Oh.** But that's not the point. The method by which we found it is curious.
solution: instead of solving second order equation (12), we solved
its equivalent system (4)–(6) of linear equations with an arbitrary right
part. I wonder if there are still undefined second order equations,
which, if solved in integers, reduce to systems of equations
first order?

**D.** Maybe we can rest? Shall we listen to the tape recorder?

**Oh.** Perhaps... (*Start the tape recorder.* **Oh.** *again
reviews the solution. Exclamation of surprise.*)

**D.** What?

**O.** (*points to line* (11)). Look, these are formulas
for Pythagorean triangles! They give all integer rectangular
triangles with legs $u$, $v$ and hypotenuse $w$:
$$
u=\alpha^2-\beta^2,\quad v=2\alpha\beta,\quad w=\alpha^2+\beta^2
$$
— you can check that $u^2+v^2=w^2$. So, the Egyptian triangle (11) -
not an accident. You can take any Pythagorean triangle with legs $u$, $v$
and hypotenuse $w$ and form the solution of equation (12) from the lengths of its sides:
$$
a=u\pm v,\quad c=u\mp v,\quad b=w.
$$

**D.** Curious. Let's check. Let the lengths of the sides of the rectangular
triangles are 5, 12 and 13. Then $12+5=17$, $12-5=7$. Getting the numbers
$7^2$, $13^2$, $17^2$. Indeed, $7^2+17^2=2\cdot13^2$.

**O.** How come I didn’t guess right away from the look of equation (12)
represent the numbers $a$ and $c$ as the sum and difference of two numbers $u$ and $v$?
After all, then this equation reduces to the Pythagorean theorem; Look:
$$
(u+v)^2+(u-v)^2=2(u^2+v^2),
$$
and therefore $b=u^2+v^2$.

And solutions in integers to such an equation are known. What a beautiful thing!
After all, $(u+v)^2$ is the area of ​​a square built on the sum of the legs, and $(u-v)^2$ is the area of ​​the square built on the difference of the legs. This means that we have proven that the areas of squares built on the difference of legs, the hypotenuse
and the sum of the legs of any right triangle form a progression.

**D.** Just think, a discovery! The whole proof fits into one
line.

**O.** The discovery is small, but of its own. We didn't know this before.

**D.** Anyway, it’s written somewhere in some book.

**Oh.** And let it be! And I'm without a book...

**D.** (*and curiosity finally awoke in him*) Why
must we take the sum and difference of the sides $u$ and $v$? Let's put, for example,
$a=2u+v$, $c=u-2v$; Then
$$
a^2+c^2=5(u^2+v^2)=5w^2.
$$
This means that the solutions in integers to the indefinite equation
$$
a^2+c^2=5w^2\tag{13}
$$
can also be obtained from Pythagorean triangles $u$, $v$, $w$ ($w$ -
hypotenuse) according to the formulas
$$
a=2u+v,\quad c=u-2v,\quad b=w.
$$

**Oh.** That's right. It is clear that in this way you can solve in integers
any indeterminate equation of the form $a^2+c^2=(m^2+n^2)b^2$, where $m$ and $n$ are
whole.

**D.** How to solve the equation
$$
a^2+c^2=3b^2?\tag{14}
$$

**Oh.** I don't know. I'll have to think about it. There is one more question we haven't answered:
which of the indefinite equations of higher degrees can be reduced to the system
linear? Equation (12), for example, we reduced to system (4)–(6). But then we didn’t know if there were any other examples of such equations. Now I understand that they can be
come up with as many as you like. Now dictate to me some kind of equality similar to
to (1), but with different coefficients. For example, start like this:
$\dfrac2{3a+2b}$.

**D.** Please (*dictates*):
$$
\dfrac2{3a+2b}-3\dfrac{c-a}=\dfrac1{b+4c}.\tag{15}
$$

**Oh.** Okay. The result is an equation connecting three unknowns $a$,
$b$, $c$. It is equivalent to the following second-order equation:
$$
3a^2-6b^2+8c^2-9ab-47ac-24bc=0.\tag{16}
$$

We want to find rational solutions to this equation. If $a$, $b$, $c$
are rational, then the fractions in (15) are rational. Let us denote the first by
$2\alpha$, the second - through $3\beta$; then the third is equal to $2\alpha-3\beta$
($\alpha$ and $\beta$ are rational). We get the system
$$
3a+2b=\dfrac1\alpha,\quad c-a=\dfrac1\beta,\quad b+4c=\dfrac1{2\alpha-3\beta}.
\tag{17}
$$
Having solved it, we will find
$$
a=\dfrac{-16\alpha^2+24\alpha\beta+3\beta^2}{5\alpha\beta(2\alpha-3\beta)},
\quad
b=\dfrac{24\alpha^2-31\alpha\beta-12\beta^2}{5\alpha\beta(2\alpha-3\beta)},
\quad
c=\dfrac{-6\alpha^2+9\alpha\beta+3\beta^2}{5\alpha\beta(2\alpha-3\beta)}.
\tag{18}
$$

These are the desired rational solutions to equation (16). The numerators of expressions (18) give its integer solutions if $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are integers. If
If we took not three unknowns $a$, $b$, $c$, but more, then by writing instead
(15) there are as many fractions on the right and left sides as there are unknowns, we would get an indefinite equation of a higher degree. In general, the more fractions there are, the higher the degree of the resulting indefinite equation; and even more so through a larger number of *arbitrary parameters* $\alpha$, $\beta$,
$\ldots$ expresses the decision.

**D.** But you don’t know in advance what kind of equation you’ll get.
And not every equation of the highest degree is equivalent to an equation that includes
sum of fractions. And if they are equivalent, then how to find these fractions?

**Oh.** That's the thing. I'll have to think about it some more...

At this point we will interrupt the imaginary conversation between two friends. They have different
characters: **D.** always wants to get rid of the task as quickly as possible, and **O.** wants to fully understand it. As a result, instead of one task,
assigned to the house, he comes up with several others, it is unknown whether they can be solved. At first glance, he behaves unreasonably, wasting the time allotted for lessons. But it is in **O.** that the qualities inherent in a scientist are more clearly expressed.
The search for the root causes of observed facts, the search for connections between problems is the driving force that makes a scientist work - it is called
curiosity, inquisitiveness, tendency to generalize. However, it is not enough to be able
ask questions, and you must answer them. It would be funny if **O.**, instead of solving a specific task assigned to homework,
listed all the problems associated with it, without mastering any of them. In searching for solutions to newly emerging problems—often unexpected ones—it is important
intuition. Intuition and insight are fruitful breaks in logic;
thanks to her, unexpected steps are taken in an unpredictable direction; and the scope of these steps measures the talent of a person.

We just have to repeat: take your time when completing your homework,
close the textbook and notebook. Look at the tasks again: what did they tell you that was new? whether the private fact lies in them or whether it allows
generalization? what problems were solved in a similar way? Is it possible without changing the method?
solutions, complicate the task? what happens if you get rid of some of the conditions?
tasks? or replace them with their opposite? increase task requirements?
add new restrictions? All of this is a great way to get things moving.
imagination, ingenuity and curiosity, perhaps dormant in you.

### Exercises

1. Find at what coefficients $a_1$, $b_1$, $\ldots$, $c_3$ the equation
$$
\dfrac1{a_1x+b_1y+c_1}+\dfrac1{a_2x+b_2y+c_2}+\dfrac1{a_3x+b_3y+c_3}=0
$$
takes the form $Ax^2+By^2+Cz^2=0$.
2. Solve the equation $6x^2=2y^2+z^2$ in integers. Try to find
other equations of type $Ax^2+By^2+Cz^2=0$, solvable in integers, as well as
ways to solve them.
3. Try to find equations like $Ax^2+By^2+Cz^2=0$ that cannot be solved in integers
numbers (not counting the zero solution).
4. Solve the equation $xyz=yzv+xzv+xyv$ in integers.
5. Come up with a generalization of the following problem: “It is known that for any
values ​​$x$
$$
a_1x^2+b_1x+c_1\gt a_2x^2+b_2x+c_2.
$$
Prove that $a_1\ge a_2$, $c_1\ge c_2$".
6. Come up with a generalization of the following problem: “Find the sum of all irreducible fractions
with a denominator of 3, enclosed between the positive integers $m$ and $n\gt m$.”
7. The same for the problem: “Find an arithmetic progression whose mean
the arithmetic $n$ of the first terms for any $n$ is equal to their number.”
