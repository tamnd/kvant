Or, in other words, a single electromagnetic field is subdivided into electric and magnetic fields in different ways in different cutoff systems.

The relative theory makes it possible to reduce the number of hypotheses that underlie electrodynamics (an example with the induction emf for a moving conductor). Now let's consider another, no less interesting example related to the question of forces acting on charges. We will try to show that in order to find the force with which a magnetic field acts on a moving electric charge - the Lorentz force - it is enough to know how the electrostatic field acts on a stationary charge.

Let us imagine that there is a charging of the body, which will end in the cutoff system $K'$, since the action of the electric field is balanced by some other force, for example, the elastic force $F$. Let us assume for definition that the electric field in the $K'$ system is directed along the $X'$ axis (that is, $E'_y = E'_z = 0$), and the magnetic field is directed along the $Y'$ ($B'_x = B'_z = 0$) axis. The force acting on a stationary electric charge $e$ is equal to the product of the electric field strength and the charge. Since the charging of the body ends, the sum of the forces acting on it

$$F_{\pi} = -e E_z = \frac{ev B'_y}{\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}}.$$

This force compensates for the action of the electric field along the $Z$ axis. What is the product of force $F_{\pi}$? According to one of the equations (8), in the system $K$ there is also a magnetic field, the induction of which

$$B = B_y = \frac{B'_y}{\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}}.$$

Comparing (9) and (10), we find that $F_{\pi} = ev B$.

Therefore, $F_{\pi}$ is also the Lorentz force.
