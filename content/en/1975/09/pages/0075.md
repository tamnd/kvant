⟦folio 73⟧

How?
And if it's impossible,
then why?

The adventures described below were served on the continent of Mathematics.
Participants in these adventures claim that everything happened exactly according to their stories. We invite you to investigate these messages and understand the confusion and questions the storytellers may have.

Eternal Wanderer
This is how the first narrator bitterly called himself and showed us the “route map” of his wanderings (Fig. 1). His house (D) is located in the central triangle of the sketched “terrain plan”. The numbers placed in the remaining triangles instruct the traveler leaving the triangle to take as many steps as indicated in this triangle (a step is called

Fig. 1.

transition to any adjacent triangle through its side, but not through its vertex). So, having entered the triangle with the number 4 (at the top of the “terrain plan”), the traveler must take 4 steps - moving, for example, through triangles 7, 8, 1 into the triangle with the number 2; from here take 2 steps (for example, into triangles 1, 6) and so on.

“No matter which of the 24 colored triangles I start the route from,” the narrator complains, “I still can’t get into the house and stay there.” Why?

The algorithm is stronger than chance

The main character in the next mathematical adventure is the controller. Yesterday, he divided 90 good-quality parts into 9 boxes equally and 10 defective parts into a separate box. These 10 parts turned out to be defective because each of them weighs 0.9 g, and any good one weighs 1 g. He did not mark the boxes and today he cannot remember which of them contained the defective parts.

You'll have to weigh it, and more quickly - you'll have to send the details later. But weighing one part from each randomly selected box is in case: if you are unlucky, you will have to do 9 weighings. The controller got by with just one weighing. How?
