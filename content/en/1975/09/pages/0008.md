This question has become especially relevant in connection with the emergence of magnetic hydrodynamics, a branch of science that studies the laws of motion of a conducting medium (liquid or gas) in a magnetic field. Such a medium could be, for example, liquid metal or plasma moving in outer space.

According to the principle of relative in the reporting system, moving along with the conductor, Ohm's law, established for a stationary conductor, is fair. Since different parts of the medium can move at different speeds, we will consider a fairly small volume of it and write Ohm’s law in the form

$$j' = \lambda E'$$

where $j'$ is the current density, $E'$ is the voltage of the electric field conductor, and $\lambda$ is the electrical conductivity.

Let (as is explained and happen) the medium considered moves at a speed small compared to the speed of light, so that $v^2/c^2 \ll 1$.

We will assume that the medium as a whole is not charged (this is also most often true); then the electric current density $j$ in the stationary reporting system ($K$) is equal to $j'$. For a charged medium this would, of course, not be the case, since a moving charge is equivalent to a current. In order to obtain Ohm's law, that is, to establish the connection between $j$ and $E$, for the movement of the conductor, we only need to know the voltage of the electric field $E'$ through its value $E$ in the stationary reporting system. This can be done using formulas (2) and (3). However, we have not yet said anything about the direction of movement of the conductor.

If the direction of movement of the conductor (along the $X$ axis) coincides with the direction of the current, and therefore the electric field, then, according to (2), $E' = E_x' = E_x = E$. In this case, Ohm's law has the same form as for stationary conductors.

Let us now consider another partial case. Let there be a magnetic field $B$ directed in a stationary reporting system along the $Z$ axis (that is, $B_z = B$, $B_x = B_y = 0$), let the conductor move parallel to the $X$ axis, and let the current flow along the $Y$ ($E_y = E$) axis. Then in the moving conductor there are $E_x' = 0$, $E_z' = 0$ and $E' = E_y'$. Provided that $v^2/c^2 \ll 1$, $E' = E - vB$ and

$$j = \lambda (E - vB).$$

In this case, Ohm's law for the movement of a conductor from that of a stationary one: the current depends not only on the strength of the electric field, but also on the speed of movement of the conductor, as well as on the induction of the magnetic field.
