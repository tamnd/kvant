Do you think the opposite is true - that if $a^2, b^2, c^2$ is a progression, then $\frac{1}{b+c}, \frac{1}{c+a}, \frac{1}{a+b}$ is again a progression?

D. Absolutely. How could it be otherwise? Do the calculations in the opposite direction - everything will work out.

A. Well, it's not nice. You know, let's try the best ones with numbers.

D. How does this work in numbers? You can't prove anything with numbers.

Oh... let's take, for example, the numbers $a^2=1, b^2=4, c^2=7$. (3)

They form a progression. Let's check that $\frac{1}{b+c}, \frac{1}{c+a}, \frac{1}{a+b}$ is also a progression.

D. Why? It is clear that the test will succeed. I proved everything in letters. It won't add anything new.

A. Well, I want to see exactly how everything turns out. So, there are empty numbers (3). Let's take only positive roots: $a=1, b=2, c=\sqrt{7}$.

D. Calculate:

$$\frac{1}{b+c} = \frac{1}{2+\sqrt{7}}, \quad \frac{1}{c+a} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{7}+1}, \quad \frac{1}{a+b} = \frac{1}{3}.$$

Is this a progression? However, let's get rid of the irrationality in the denominator:

$$\frac{1}{b+c} = \frac{\sqrt{7}-2}{3}, \quad \frac{1}{c+a} = \frac{\sqrt{7}-1}{6}.$$

A. Of course, this is a progression. Sum of extreme members

$$\frac{\sqrt{7}-2}{3} + \frac{1}{3} = \frac{\sqrt{7}-1}{3}$$

twice the average.

D. Yes; and the progression difference is $\frac{1}{3} - \frac{\sqrt{7}-1}{6} = \frac{3-\sqrt{7}}{6}$.

O. Wait. Now I know; how to prove that from (2) follows (1). You just need to get rid of the irrationality in the denominator.

D. How is it? After all, there is no irrationality there!

A. Of course not; but we must act as if it were. Look:

$$\frac{1}{b+c} = \frac{b-c}{b^2-c^2}, \quad \frac{1}{c+a} = \frac{a-c}{a^2-c^2}, \quad \frac{1}{a+b} = \frac{a-b}{a^2-b^2}.$$

According to the conditions of this difference through $d$. Then $a^2-c^2=2d$. The numbers $\frac{b-c}{d}, \frac{a-c}{2d}, \frac{a-b}{d}$ obviously form a progression.

D. Well, good... what else did they ask?

A. Wait, now do you understand how the progressions $\frac{1}{b+c}, \frac{1}{c+a}, \frac{1}{a+b}$ and $a^2, b^2, c^2$ are related?

D. I understood before.

A. Let $a, b, c$ be arbitrary numbers. Let's consider the triple $b-c, \frac{a-c}{2}, a-b$ this is already a progression. The terms of this progression can be divided by any number $d$. In the problem, $d$ is chosen so that division by it leads to the numbers $\frac{1}{b+c}, \frac{1}{c+a}, \frac{1}{a+b}$; That is why, together with arbitrary $a, b, c$, we need to take such that $a^2-b^2=b^2-c^2=d$, that is, so that the numbers $a^2, b^2, c^2$ form a progression. But here again the question arises: can all three numbers $a, b, c$ be integers?

D. Does it really matter whether they can or cannot?
