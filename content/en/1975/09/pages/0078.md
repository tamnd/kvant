ANSWERS, INSTRUCTIONS, SOLUTIONS

## To the article “Electrodynamics of moving media”

1. Let the electric field be directed along the $Y$ axis, and the magnetic field along the $Z$ axis ($E_y = E$, $E_x = E_z = 0$, $B_z = B$, $B_y = B_x = 0$). In order for the electric field in a moving reference frame to become zero, according to formulas (3) of the article, it is required that the speed of motion of the system be equal to $v = \dfrac{E}{B}$ and directed along the axis $X$.

Let us consider the motion of a particle in this reference frame. Since there is no electric field, its movement is easy to determine: the particle will rotate around the magnetic field lines, that is, in the $X\ Y$ plane, the radius of rotation will be $\dfrac{mu}{eB}$, where $u$ is the initial velocity, $m$ is the mass, and $e$ is the charge of the particle. In this case, the direction of rotation of a positively charged particle will form a left-hand screw with the direction of the magnetic field induction, and a negatively charged particle (for example, an electron) will form a right-hand screw.

In a stationary reference frame, this motion is superimposed by a translational motion with a speed of $v$. If $v \ll c$, then you can use the usual law of addition of velocities. As a result, we obtain that the particle moves in the $XY$ plane along one of the three trajectories shown in

⟦figure⟧
Fig. 1.

figure 1. Case $a$) corresponds to a large initial velocity of the particle, when $u > v$. In this case, the particle moves part of the time in the direction opposite to the direction of velocity $v$, making a loop-like motion. If $u < v$, there is no return movement (case $б$)). Finally, for $u = v$ the trajectory turns out to be a cycloid (case $в$)).

Note that the sign of the velocity $v$ does not depend on the sign of the charge, therefore both positive and negative particles have the same translational motion in crossed fields. This movement is called drift. As a result of drift, a neutral medium consisting of an equal number of freely moving positive and negative particles (for example, plasma) moves in a direction perpendicular to both the electric and magnetic fields.

2. Let the capacitor plates be located in the $XZ$ plane, the distance between the plates is $d$ and the speed $v$ is directed along the $X$ axis. In the reference frame associated with the capacitor, there is only an electric field $E'_y$ directed along the $Y'$ axis, and the potential difference between the capacitor plates is $\Delta\varphi_0 = -E'_y d$.

⟦column⟧

In a stationary reference frame, we also have two parallel charged plates, the distance between which is equal to $d$ (this distance does not shorten, since it is measured in the direction perpendicular to the motion of the system). Thus, for the electric field in a stationary reference frame, we can write $\Delta\varphi = -E_y d$, where $\Delta\varphi$ and $E_y$ are the potential difference and the electric field strength, respectively. Using the connection between $E_y$ and $E'_y$ (see formulas (7) in the article), we find that

$$\Delta\varphi = \frac{-E'_y d}{\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}} = \frac{\Delta\varphi_0}{\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}}.$$

Note that in the reference frame associated with the capacitor, the magnetic field is zero. This is not the case in a moving reference frame, in which there is a non-zero current due to the movement of the capacitor charges. Therefore, when using inverse field transformations (see formulas (3)), it is impossible to obtain an answer in which $\Delta\varphi$ and $\Delta\varphi_0$ simply swap places.

3. We will assume that the electron is a point charge and place it at the origin of the moving reference frame $K'$. The magnetic field in the reference frame $K'$ will be considered equal to zero (although this is not true for a real electron, since it has a non-zero magnetic moment). The electric field strength created by an electron at a point with radius vector $\mathbf{r}$ is $\mathbf{E} = -\dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\dfrac{e}{r^3}\mathbf{r}$
