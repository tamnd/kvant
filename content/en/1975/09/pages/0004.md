“It is known that Maxwell’s electrodynamics in its modern form leads, when applied to moving bodies, to an asymmetry that is apparently not inherent in the phenomena themselves.” This is how Albert Einstein’s famous work “On the Electrodynamics of Moving Bodies”* begins. Further, Einstein considers as an example not some new, just discovered phenomenon, but the phenomenon of electromagnetic induction: with the relative movement of a conductor and a magnet, an electric current arises in the conductor. This was established by Faraday back in the first half of the century. What attracted the attention of the young, at that time still unknown case of his patents in the Swiss city of Bern to such a not at all new and, it would seem, long ago explained fact? What disturbed Einstein was not that this phenomenon could not be explained, but that it could be explained equally well in two completely different ways.

In fact, if we assume that the magnet is moving and the conductor remains at rest, then an electric current arises in it because the changing magnetic field causes the appearance of an electric field, which sets the charges in motion

*) This work was published in the German physics journal “Annals of Physics” in 1905, that is, exactly 70 years ago. In it, Einstein formulated the basic principles of relative responsibility.

On the other hand, if we assume that the conductor moves and the magnet ends (Fig. 1), then the appearance of the current is explained by the Lorentz force acting from the magnetic field on the free electrons that are in the conductor and move with it in the magnetic field. If $B$ is the magnetic field induction, $l$ is the length of the moving conductor, $v$ is its speed, then the work of the Lorentz force per unit positive charge, that is, the electromotive force of induction $\mathcal{E}$, is equal

$$\mathcal{E} = -vBl$$

(the minus sign is explained by Lenz’s rule for the direction of induction current).

However, Einstein argues, we are dealing here with the same phenomenon: the relative motion of a magnet and a conductor; So why do we propose two different principles to explain one phenomenon? It's a shame when a phenomenon has no explanation, but has two explanations that are not much better, or who can guarantee us that they do not actually contradict each other? No, all is not well in the majestic edifice of electrodynamics. And Einstein takes this as a starting point for his criticism of contemporary science, a criticism that led to a radical restructuring of our ideas about space and time.
