⟦folio 24⟧

Homework task
G. I. Kopylov

— I love solving difficult problems. I like to look for new proofs of theorems and read books about scientists. I study with satisfaction; I won a prize at the school competition. I would like to become a scientist. But can I become one? Am I fit to be a scientist? How to test yourself?

Unfortunately, there are no signs by which one could recognize a future scientist. Success at school does not at all guarantee that those who graduate from university will have the same success: a schoolchild solves what is given, assimilates other people’s thoughts; the scientist sets his own tasks. Further, the student is good at carrying out logical reasoning, this does not mean that he is fit to be a scientist. The ability to reason well is really necessary for a scientist, except in those cases when it is a fairy tale in a logical construction - that is, not logic, but logic - that provides a solution to the problem. And so on.

But although studying at school is not at all similar to scientific work, some of the qualities necessary for a scientist can be developed, even while preparing lessons. Just after solving the next problem, do not rush to move on to the next one. Read the terms again, look at the answer received - and give free rein to your imagination... Ask, for example, yourself: what else could it be? Is it possible to solve more difficult problems based on the one you just solved?

However, let’s better listen to the conversation between the two guys. They study in the same class and are preparing their homework together today.

D. What were we given in algebra?
O. Prove that if the numbers $\frac{1}{b+c}$, $\frac{1}{c+a}$, $\frac{1}{a+b}$ make up the argument of Scruto programming, then the numbers $a^2$, $b^2$, $c^2$ also make up the argument of programming.

D. In my opinion, the task is not difficult. Each argumentative program is the average argumentation of its two neighbors. Means,
$$\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{b+c}+\frac{1}{a+b}\right)=\frac{1}{c+a}.$$

Let's get rid of breakdowns in (1), open the brackets, and present similar terms; we get
$$a^2+c^2=2b^2,$$
that is
$$\frac{1}{2}(a^2+c^2)=b^2,$$
and this was what needed to be proven.
O. Yes, everything seems to be correct...
D. What else is scheduled for tomorrow?
O. Wait, let me think...
D. Over what?
A. On the problem we just solved.
D. What to think about it if it has already been decided?
