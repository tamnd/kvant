new equation
$$a^2 + c^2 = 2b^2;$$
(12)
solutions are written by formulas (8)–(10).

D. Where does this equation come from?
A. So, after all, it is a short record of the condition that the numbers $a^2, b^2$ and $c^2$ form a progression.
D. Ah, I understand. But it has not been proven that formulas (8)–(10) provide all solutions to this equation.
O. Why? After all, this is clear; any solution to equation (12) is such that the numbers $\frac{1}{b+c}, \frac{1}{c+a}, \frac{1}{a+b}$ form a progression; and any progression can be represented as $\alpha - \beta, \alpha, \alpha + \beta$. Having sorted through all possible pairs $(\alpha, \beta)$, we obtain all solutions to equation (12). It's clear?
D. Every one, yes.
A. But that’s not the point. The very method by which we found the solution is curious: instead of solving second-order equation (12), we solved its equivalent system (4)-(6) of linear equations with an arbitrary right-hand side. I wonder if there are still undefined second-order equations that, if solved in integers, have properties similar to the first-order equation system?
D. Maybe we should move away? Shall we listen to the tape recorder?
O. Please... (They start the tape recorder. O. looks at the solution again. He looks surprised.)
D. What?
O. (point to line (11)). Look, these are formulas for Pythagorean triangles! These give all integer right triangles with legs $u, v$ and hypotenuse $w$:
$$u = \alpha^2 - \beta^2, v = 2\alpha\beta, w = \alpha^2 + \beta^2, \text{— можно проверить, что } u^2 + v^2 = w^2.$$
This means that the Egyptian triangle (11) is not an accident. You can take any Pythagorean triangle with legs $u, v$ and hypotenuse $w$ and form a solution to equation (12) from the lengths of its sides:
$$a = u \pm v, c = u \mp v, b = w.$$
D. Curious. Let's check. Let the lengths of the sides of a right triangle be 5, 12 and 13. Then $12 + 5 = 17, 12 - 5 = 7$. We get the numbers $7^2, 13^2, 17^2$. Indeed, $7^2 + 17^2 = 2 \cdot 13^2$.
O. How is it that I, by the form of equation (12), did not immediately prove the numbers $a$ and $c$ as the sum and difference of two numbers $u$ and $v$? After all, then this is an equation of properties to the Pythagorean theorem; Look:
$$(u + v)^2 + (u - v)^2 = 2(u^2 + v^2),$$
and therefore $b^2 = u^2 + v^2$.
And solutions in integers to such an equation are known. What a beautiful thing! After all, $(u + v)^2$ is the area of ​​a square built on the sum of the legs, and $(u - v)^2$ is the area of ​​the square built on the difference of the legs. This means that we have proven that the areas of squares built on the difference of the legs, the hypotenuse and the sum of the legs of any right triangle form a progression.
D. Just think, a discovery! The entire proof fits into one line.
A. The discovery is not great, but its own. We didn't know this before.
D. Anyway, it’s written somewhere in some book.
O. And so be it! And I'm without a book...
D. (his curiosity finally awoke) Why is it necessary to be the sum and difference of the legs $u$ and $v$? Let's put, for example, $a = 2u + v, c = u - 2v$; Then
$$a^2 + c^2 = 5(u^2 + v^2) = 5w^2.$$
This means that solutions in integers to the indeterminate equation
