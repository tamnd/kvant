a sphere with handles on which this node can be placed so that it breaks the entire surface into two “pieces”. Let’s take our knot and “pull” a double-sided surface over it (like a soap film over a wire frame); one of the methods of such “pulling” is described in the 9th chapter of the already mentioned book by M. Gardner (see skoku on p. 9). How to do this, for example, for a green leaf, can be seen from figure 16, b-g.

Remark e. A surface whose edge is a certain node will not always be double-sided; Moebus sheet at figure 16, a - an example of a one-sided surface stretched over a trival knot!

We will assume that the film that we “stretched” onto the knot is double (in the sense in which the expression “double sheet of paper” is used), so glued together at the edges, that is, something similar to a deflated balloon. Let us now inflate this “balloon”; we will get a sphere with handles on which our node is “drawn”. If you cut the “ball” along the knot, it will split into two films from which it was glued together.

Prove that if a knot cuts a sphere with the smallest air number of handles, then this knot can be implemented as an edging for our sphere (an illustration can be figure 16, e-g).
