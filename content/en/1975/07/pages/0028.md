For all liquids, both measurement methods give the same result. However, there is one exception to this rule, and that exception is HeII. When HeII flows through narrow capillaries, the viscosity turns out to be zero, that is, superfluidity is detected. One would expect that the oscillations of a disk lowered into HeII would be undamped. However, with the second method of determining viscosity, HeII behaves like an ordinary liquid—the vibrations of the disk are damped.

These amazing properties of helium found their explanation in the theory of superfluidity by Academician L. D. Landau, constructed by him in 1941. According to this theory, HeII is characterized by two types of motion - normal and superfluid. You can clearly imagine it as consisting of two components. One of them is a normal liquid, and the other is a superfluid. The oscillations of the disk are damped due to its friction against the normal component. In an experiment with helium flowing through a narrow capillary, a superfluid component is detected. At absolute zero there is no normal component.

**Rotating helium**

Let us study the properties of HeII poured into a cylindrical vessel rotating around an axis (see Fig. 4). We will start from the two-fluid model described above and try to imagine the picture of such rotation. The normal component, due to friction against the cylinder walls, must be involved in the movement of the wall. As for the superfluid component, at first glance, it seems that it should remain motionless.

What observable effect should this peculiar picture lead to? As is known, when a liquid poured into a vessel rotates, the free surface of the liquid takes the shape of a paraboloid. Since in our experiment not all helium rotates, but only its normal component, we should expect that when HeII rotates, this surface should be less curved than in the case when HeI is in the cylinder.

What does the experiment show? The shape of the surface of rotating HeII turns out to be the same as in a normal liquid.

How is this phenomenon explained? Doesn't the picture of rotating helium contradict the theory of superfluidity?
