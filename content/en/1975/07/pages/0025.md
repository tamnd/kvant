⟦folio 23⟧

Bohr retained the planetary model of the atom and came to the conclusion that in the microcosm there are laws that are radically different from the laws established by physicists when studying the ordinary macrocosm around us.

Let us now return to the story about a superconducting ring in which an undamped electric current circulates. In this case, the emission of electromagnetic waves does not occur for the same reason that an electron does not emit when moving around an atomic nucleus. In both cases, the laws of quantum physics play a major role. But if, when studying the properties of an atom, we encounter quantization in the microcosm, which we cannot observe directly, then superconductivity provides an example of quantization of a macroscopic quantity that is directly observable by us - current strength.

It turns out that the current strength in a superconducting ring cannot take on any numerical value and change continuously. For the entire electronic collective moving in such a ring, a giant “Bohr” orbit arises. Just as in the planetary model of the atom, the allowed orbits of an electron are characterized by certain values ​​of angular momentum and energy, giant orbits of electrons in superconducting rings are characterized by well-defined values ​​of current strength.

Since the superconducting current is closely related to the magnetic field (see Fig. 1), the quantization of the current leads to the fact that the induction $B$ of the field can only take on a discrete series of values. Consequently, the magnetic flux $\Phi = \pi r^2 B$ through the cross section of the superconducting ring will also be quantized. In other words, the magnetic flux $\Phi$ can be a multiple of a certain minimum portion - the magnetic flux quantum $\Phi_0$, that is, $\Phi = N\Phi_0$, where $N$ is an integer. Magnetic flux is a macroscopic quantity, and the possibility of its quantization means the transition to giant

An experiment in which the quantization of magnetic flux was observed was carried out by American physicists B. Deaver and W. Fairbank in 1961. A thin layer of tin was deposited over the entire surface of a copper wire with a diameter of $1{,}3 \cdot 10^{-3}$ cm. Thus, a “hollow” cylinder of tin was obtained, and a superconducting current was excited in this cylinder at temperatures below $T_к = 3{,}8°$ K (at $T < 3{,}8°$ K tin becomes a superconductor). (Copper is not a superconductor, and therefore it played the role of a core necessary for preparing a superconducting cylinder. In what follows, we will only talk about a tin cylinder. A continuous current circulated in it.) To measure the magnetic flux in the cavity surrounded by this current, an induction coil was placed at one end of the cylinder. The cylinder was set into oscillatory motion along its axis, the oscillation frequency reaching 100 Hz. At the same time, an induction current arose in the coils, allowing one to judge the magnitude of the magnetic flux located inside the cylinder. Experience has shown that the flow equal to $\Phi = \pi r^2 B$ ($r$ is the radius of the cylinder) takes on a discrete series of values:

$$\Phi = N\Phi_0,$$

$N$ is an integer ($N = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$), and $\Phi_0$ is a magnetic flux quantum.
