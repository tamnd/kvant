Fig. 7. Knots in front of the mirror.

Figures 6, a, b? And are they curves? Without going into details *), we must recognize these strange structures as having the right to be called curves, and, consequently, nodes. But with “wild” knots, as opposed to “manual” knots, similar to ordinary rope knots.

A *hand knot* can be reduced to a closed spatial polyline with a finite number of links and without self-intersection points.

All nodes shown in Figures 2 and 4 can be considered manual: each of them is divided into a finite number of “almost straight” segments. On the other hand, it is obvious that the nodes at figure 6 are not tame, such nodes are called *wild*.

Wild nodes may have unexpected properties; for example, the knot shown in figure 6, b **) is untied, but only after an infinite number of successive operations: first the loop $A_1$ must be removed from the loop $A_2$, then the loop $A_2$ from $A_3$ and so on. It is impossible to untie this knot in the usual sense.

§ 5. Types of nodes can be defined in different ways

We would like our knot theory to preserve the naive idea of ​​“equally tied” knots that we all have. This gives rise to the first of two (different) definitions of the *type* of a node.

First definition: Two nodes are said to be *isotopic*, or have the same *isotopic type*, if one node can be “tied” into another without cutting it or allowing self-intersections.

In practice, the isotopicity of two nodes can sometimes be proven either using a rope ring or a series of figure-diagrams. For example, as we already know, the top node is isotopic to Jacob's ladder and both of them are isotopic to the circle. Here's another example: a *single* knot (see Fig. 4, a, b) is isotopic (check it out!) to the simplest knot after a circle, shown in figure 7, a. Due to its resemblance to the outline of a clover leaf, this knot is called *cloverleaf*, or *shamrock*. Draw the “*double bow*” knot yourself; already at the age of two or three years you were able to prove (by pulling the free end of the string) that “*bow*” (see Fig. 4, c, d) and “double bow” are isotopic to a single knot, and therefore to a clover leaf.
