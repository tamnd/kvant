Liquid helium rotates completely calmly (this corresponds to laminar flow) only at very low rotation speeds (usually less than $\sim 10^{-2}\ см/с$). As the speed increases, a picture similar to a turbulent regime appears. Imagine a moving fluid containing a large number of funnels - areas in which the movement of the fluid is similar to the movement of air in a tornado. Rotating helium turns out to be permeated with such areas. They are called vortices. The superfluid component located in the region of the vortex rotates around its axis. The rotation speed increases as one approaches the vortex axis, and at a certain distance from the axis (on the order of interatomic distances) a “breakdown” of superfluidity occurs; that is, the property of superfluidity is maintained up to a certain critical speed ($\sim 60\ м/с$). Therefore, a region containing normal liquid appears near the vortex axis. The appearance of funnels associated with vortices leads to the curvature of the free surface of the rotating HeII.

Thus, rotating superfluid helium turns out to be permeated with vortices; the vortex trunk represents the normal component. Helium atoms rotate around the axis of the vortex, just as electrons rotate in a superconducting ring *). The motion of these atoms is also subject to a quantization rule, which is similar to Bohr's quantization rule for the orbits of electrons in an atom.

If $p$ is the momentum of the helium atom (in the superfluid component), $r$ is the radius of the vortex barrel, then

$$pr = N\hbar, \text{ или } m_{\mathrm{He}} v \cdot r = N\hbar \quad (***)$$
$$(N = 1, 2, 3, \ldots)$$

($m_{\mathrm{He}}$ is the mass of a helium atom). In other words, in the case of vortices in helium, the orbital momentum of the helium atom is quantized. The magnitude of the orbital momentum quantum is $\hbar$.

The radius of vortices in superfluid helium is a value that can take on any value, up to the size of the vessel into which liquid helium is poured. Typically, in experiments, the vessel has a radius of 1 cm, that is, the quantization scale increases by approximately $10^{8}$ times compared to the atomic one (recall that the size of the atomic orbit is $\sim 10^{-8}\ см$).

From (***) it is clear that the speed of rotation of helium atoms around the barrel depends on their distance to the vortex axis - $v = N\dfrac{\hbar}{m_{\mathrm{He}} r}$ - and for the same value $r$ can take on a discrete series of values. To characterize motion along a closed contour of radius $r$, use the value $vr$, which is called circulation. As can be seen from formula (***), during the formation of vortices in superfluid helium, the circulation of the liquid can only take on a discrete series of values, that is, it is a quantized quantity:

$$vr = N\frac{\hbar}{m_{\mathrm{He}}}\ (N = 1, 2, 3, \ldots).$$

The quantization law (***) was experimentally studied in the work of the English physicist Vainen. The experimental results showed that the quantity $m_{\mathrm{He}} vr$ actually takes on a discrete series of values, and the orbital momentum quantum is equal to $\hbar$.

Thus, in superfluid helium, as well as in superconductors, the effect of macroscopic quantization is observed.
