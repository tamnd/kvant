⟦folio 21⟧

Quantum theory and low-temperature physics emerged almost simultaneously, at the beginning of this century. At first they developed independently of each other, but later a deep connection between these “peers” was revealed. First of all, it became clear that without quantum physics it is impossible to explain the phenomena observed at low temperatures. Subsequently, and especially in recent years, low-temperature physics itself has made important contributions to quantum theory.

Quantum is a fundamental concept of modern physics. The importance of this concept is supported by the name of the magazine you are reading at the moment. It is one of the main provisions of quantum physics that the purpose of a number of physical quantities, such as energy, momentum, etc., in the definitions of quantizing conditions, that is, they can only apply a discrete series of values. But in non-Advenan times it was believed that quantization concerns only the microcosm, that is, it is characteristic of processes occurring in atoms, atomic nuclei, etc. Indeed, when studying macroscopic objects we are dealing with a huge number of particles. And although the behavior of each individual particle is described by the laws of quantum physics, in general, in a macrosoptic sample, the quantization of energy, momentum and other physical quantities does not manifest itself due to the chaotic thermal motion of the particles. Quantum efficient ones seem to be successfully “fading away.” Thus, the thermal movement of atoms massages quantum laws. However, the study of some macroscopic objects, the sizes of which are hundreds of thousands of times larger than atomic ones. Near absolute zero, when thermal motion does not play a significant role, direct observation of quantum laws becomes possible.

The two phenomena of macroscopic quantization are discussed further in this article.

Magnetic flux quantization

First we turn to the phenomenon of superconductivity. Superconductors, as is known, are remarkable in that at a certain temperature (it is called critical and denoted $T_k$) electrical resistance disappears. This phenomenon was discovered in 1911 by the Dutch scientist Kamerlingh Onnes. But the nature of superconductivity became clear almost half a century later, in 1957, when the theory of superconductivity was finally constructed.

In this article we will not dwell on the explanation of superconductivity.
