## Appendix I

"Vortices" in superconductors

This application describes how a magnetic field penetrates certain superconducting substances. This process is quantum in nature: the field penetrates the substance in separate portions. Before moving directly to the description of this phenomenon, let us briefly dwell on the issue of using superconductors to create strong magnetic fields.

At first glance, it may seem that superconductors are ideal for creating arbitrarily strong magnetic fields. In fact, when using electromagnets one has to take into account heat losses. An increase in current strength in a conventional solenoid also entails an increase in Joule losses. And if we require very strong magnetic fields, then, along with large expenditures of power, complex cooling systems are needed to maintain the current to prevent the wires from overheating and even melting. When using a superconducting current that flows without any resistance, these difficulties are completely absent.

However, the matter is not as simple as it might seem at first glance. Let us assume that an ordinary superconductor is in an external magnetic field. It cannot penetrate inside, into the thickness of the superconductor. The fact is that there is an inextricable connection between the magnetic field

and superconducting current. Exposure to an external magnetic field leads to the appearance of a superconducting current in the surface layer of the metal. This surface current creates its own magnetic field, which is opposite to the applied field. The theory of superconductivity proves that the magnetic field inside the superconducting substance is completely compensated (this phenomenon is called the Meissner effect).

If we create a superconducting current in a metal, then a magnetic field will arise in the surrounding space. It cannot penetrate a superconducting sample. However, such a picture is observed as long as the current and the corresponding field are not very large ($\sim 3 \cdot 10^{-3}$ $тл$). When the field reaches a certain value $B_\mathrm{к}$ (critical value), it penetrates into the superconductor and, “bursting” into it, destroys the superconducting state. Therefore, for a long time it seemed that obtaining strong magnetic fields (large $B_\mathrm{к}$) using superconductors was impossible.

Later it turned out, however, that there are superconductors with the help of which very large (superstrong) magnetic fields can be obtained. These superconductors - they are called type II superconductors - can be in the so-called mixed state, the theory of which was developed by the Soviet theoretical physicist A. A. Abrikosov.

If a type II superconductor through which current flows is in a magnetic field, then at a certain value of induction $B_{\mathrm{к}1}$ (this value is called the lower critical field), the field begins to partially penetrate into the thickness of the superconducting sample. Electrons in a superconductor, under the influence of the Lorentz force, begin to move in circles (if their speed is perpendicular to the field lines). The picture that arises is similar to the picture of rotating He II: vortices appear in the superconductor.
