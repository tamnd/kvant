I was traveling around Leningrad on a tram with my nephew Misha. Having deposited 6 kopecks into the cash register, I tore off two tickets.

- This ticket is mine! - said Misha.

- Please take any of them. They are exactly the same; with any of them you can travel the entire route.

- The same, but not quite. This ticket is the most ordinary, with the number 286 357 on it. And the next ticket with the number 286 358 is “lucky”, the sum of the first three digits coincides with the sum of the last three.

Then I remembered that I had heard more than once about a widespread belief: a ticket with the same sums of numbers brings happiness. In this case, Misha got ticket 286 358, in which 2+8+6=3+5+8.

— And how often do you come across “lucky” tickets? - I asked.

- No, very rarely. About once a month. And since I go to the institute and back every day, except weekends, it means that on average one “lucky” ticket comes for every 50 regular ones.

“Nonsense,” said one of our companions. — I got on at the previous stop and at the same ticket office I also pulled out a “lucky” ticket - 286,349. And even now someone is tearing off a ticket 286,367, also “lucky”; will appear soon - 286,376, then 286,385. So in every ten tickets there is one “lucky” one.

“This is not true,” objected the new passenger, who tore off the “lucky” ticket 286 367. “Your example does not prove anything.” In the next ten there will be another “lucky” ticket - 286,394. And then there will be no “lucky” tickets for a long time, until the number 286,439, so here there will be an interval of 45 tickets between two “lucky” tickets. Many such examples can be given. In the same roll of tickets, the initial numbers of which are 286, between tickets 286,097 and 286,169, that is, among 71 tickets, there is not a single “lucky” one.

“So I’m saying,” Misha picked up, “on average, one “lucky” ticket ends up on the canvas.”

“This is also a rash statement,” I remarked. — To answer this question correctly, you need to research it. But first you need to formulate it precisely. Let's put it this way: how many “lucky” six-digit numbers are there from 000,000 to 999,999, that is, numbers that have equal sums of the first three and last three digits?
