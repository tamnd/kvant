Comparing formulas (2) and (3), we arrive at the following expression for the electron momentum:

$$p = \frac{\Phi e}{2\pi r}$$

(4)

An essential feature of superconductors is that they split electrons into bonded pairs. Therefore, it makes sense to talk about the momentum of an electron pair as a whole, which is equal to twice the momentum of the electron:

$$p = \frac{\Phi e}{\pi r}$$

(5)

Formula (5) is directly related to the electron pulse in a superconductor with the magnetic flux created by the superconducting current. Substituting this expression into the quantization law (*), we obtain

$$r \frac{\Phi e}{\pi r} = N\hbar (N=1, 2, 3, \dots).$$

From here

$$\Phi = N \frac{\hbar \pi}{e} = N \frac{h}{2e},$$

that is

$$\Phi = N\Phi_0,$$ where $\Phi_0 = \frac{h}{2e}$.

Thus, the magnetic flux quantum turns out to be equal to $\frac{h}{2e}$. The magnetic flux of an arbitrary superconducting ring can only take values ​​that are multiples of $\Phi_0$. The quantum value is easily calculated by substituting into (**) the numerical values ​​of Planck’s constant $h$ and the electron charge $e$:

$$\Phi_0 = 2 \cdot 10^{-15} \text{ e}\sigma.$$

In the experiment of Deaver and Fairbank, which we described above, it was discovered that the magnetic flux in the cylinder takes values ​​that are multiples of exactly this value.

"Vortices" in superfluid helinium

Another example of quantization, which occurs on a huge scale compared to the atomic scale, is also observed at low temperatures, when another “superphenomenon” is studied - the superfluidity of liquid gel.

It is known that at a temperature of $T = 4,2^\circ\text{K}$ and normal pressure, helium passes from a gaseous state into a liquid state and, with a further increase in temperature down to absolute zero, does not solidify (only under a pressure of 25 amperes at a temperature close to absolute zero can helium be converted into a solid state). But this does not exhaust the changes in the properties of this substance. At $T = 2,17^\circ\text{K}$ helium goes into a special state - superfluid. The phenomenon of superfluidity, discovered by academician P. L. Kapitsa in 1937, consists in the ability of liquid gel at a temperature less than $T = 2,17^\circ\text{K}$ to flow in narrow capillaries without any friction. Helinium at a temperature less than $2,17^\circ\text{K}$ is called HeII - unlike HeI ($4,2^\circ\text{K} > T > 2,17^\circ\text{K}$), it is an ordinary liquid.

The phenomena of superfluidity and superconductivity are similar to each other. We can say that superconductivity is the superfluidity of electrons. The analogy between superconductivity and superfluidity turns out to be profound, and in part, it manifests itself in the possibility of observing macroscopic quantum phenomena both in superconductors and in superfluid gelini.
