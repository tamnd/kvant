§ 9. About simple knots

The existence of simple knots was proven more than twenty years after the simple ones were collected and systematized; in 1926, American mathematicians Alexaan Holder (one of the founders of knot theory) and Briggs published a table of knot diagrams (at that time they were not yet called simple), similar to tables of prime numbers*) (p. 17). The place of a node in the table is determined, first of all, by its minimum number of crossings, and among nodes with the same number - first of all, by the “confectionery number” of the node, and then by other invariants that are still unfamiliar to us.

Exercise 11. Find all the simple nodes in Figures 2-8 and their diagrams in the table. Calculate the values ​​of the invariants M, I, K, and P for these nodes.

The proof of both the existence of simple nodes and the decomposability of any non-trivial node into a finite number of simple ones is based on the substitution property of the genus of nodes: the genus of the composition of two nodes is equal to the sum of the genus of “factors”, that is:

$$P(A \# B) = P(A) + P(B).$$

We talked about this property in § 10, and now we will use it twice.

1°. So, consider the non-trivial node $A$. If it is simple, then the existence of simple nodes has already been proven, as well as the decomposability of the node $A$ into a finite number of simple ones. Otherwise, we decompose $A$ into a composition of two nodes, then, if possible, we decompose which of the “factor” nodes, and so on. In no more than $(P(A) - 1)$ steps we will obtain the expansion we need - the genus of each of the (nontrivial!) “factors” is at least 1, and the sum of them all is $P(A)$.

2°. Let us now give an extremely short proof of the statement “no non-trivial knot can be untied by tying another one next to it” (see § 8, property 3). Indeed, if $A \# B = E$, then $P(A \# B) = P(A) + P(B) = 0$, so $P(A) = P(B) = 0$, and therefore $A = B = E$.
