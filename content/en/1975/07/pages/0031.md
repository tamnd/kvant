⟦folio 29⟧

“breakdown” of superconductivity - magnetic induction inside the exit from zero. The output “barrel” is in normal condition.

Thus, the superconducting sample turns out to be made up of threads, which represent ordinary non-superconducting regions. The vortices in a superconductor form a triangular lattice (see Fig. 5). The exit trunks are oriented in the direction of the confluent magnetic field lines. In the space between the outputs, the properties of superconductivity are preserved. The magnetic flux, an arbitrary cross-section of the output barrel, turns out to be the same for all outputs and equal in magnitude to the magnetic flux quantum $\Phi_0$. That is, each output carries one flux quantum, and in the form of such separate portions the magnetic field penetrates inside the superconductor.

As the field increases ($B > B_{K1}$), the number of normal regions increases, the output filaments move closer together, and at a certain field value $B_{K2}$ (this value is called the upper cremic field), when the distance between the output filaments becomes $\sim 10^{-6}$ m, superconductivity is completely destroyed and the substance passes into the normal state.

This peculiar pattern of penetration of a magnetic field into a type II superconductor makes it possible to use them to obtain merged magnetic fields. The value of the upper critical field $B_{K2}$ can be very large. For example, for the alloy Nb$_3$Sn $B_{K2} \approx 20$ ml. (Type II superconductors are alloys with a high impurity content.)

When using superconductors of the second type to obtain confluent magnetic fields, the energy costs are hundreds to thousands of times less than when using electromagnets. For example, to obtain a 10 ml field using a conventional solenoid requires a power supply of 5000 kW and, in addition, requires a complex installation for cooling the solenoid winding. When using superconductors, only energy is required to obtain a liquid gel (at the temperature of the liquid gel, the solenoid metal is in a superconducting state). But for this in the example given, a power of only 5 kW is required. That is, energy costs are reduced by 1000 times.

Appendix II

Let us present the derivation of the formula for the energy possessed by the current flowing through the conductor.

Let us consider the circuit along which the current $I$ is projected. The magnetic flux $\Phi$, which created this current, returns with an increase in $I$ according to the law $\Phi = LI$, where $L$ is the inductance of the circuit. Let us assume that at the initial time $t_0 = 0$ the current in the circuit is $I_0$, and, starting from this moment, the current strength uniformly returns equal to $I_t$. It is clear that an increase in current strength is accompanied by an increase in the energy of the magnetic field that created the current.

During time $t$, charge $q$ moved along the conductor. The charge of motion is eradicated, since the current strength returns (the current density is $j = nev$; at a constant $n$, the increase in $j$ is due to the increase in $v$). In order to obtain a formula for the current energy, we calculate the work of electric forces to move the charge $q$ along the entire closed circuit. The work is expressed by the formula $A = qE$, where $E$ is the electromotive force, equal to $E = IR + \frac{\Delta \Phi}{\Delta t}$ in an ordinary conductor.

The first term on the right side of this equality is associated with the total ohmic losses, and the second is due to the presence of effective self-induction, which weakens the current. It is clear that $q = I_{cp} \cdot t$ ($I_{cp}$ is the average current strength, equal to $I_{cp} = \frac{I_0 + I_t}{2}$ for a uniform increase in current). Thus, equal turns out to be equal

$$A = \frac{I_0 + I_t}{2} t E.$$

In superconductors $R = 0$ and $E = \frac{\Delta \Phi}{\Delta t}$. Since the formula is $\Phi = LI$, then $E = L \frac{\Delta I}{\Delta t}$. In our case, the change in point $\Delta I$ is equal to $\Delta I = I_t - I_0$, and the time interval is $\Delta t = t$. Then $E = L \frac{I_t - I_0}{t}$. Substituting this value for $E$ into the formula to work, we arrive at the following expression:

$$A = \frac{I_0 + I_t}{2} t \cdot L \frac{I_t - I_0}{t}.$$

The work thus turns out to be equal

$$A = \frac{LI^2}{2} - \frac{LI_0^2}{2}.$$

It can be seen that the amount of work is described by a change in the value $\frac{LI^2}{2}$. It is natural to consider it equal to the current energy, so

$$E = \frac{LI^2}{2}.$$

(The attentive reader will discover an analogy between the above derivation of the formula for current energy and the derivation of the expression for kinetic energy in ordinary mechanics.)
