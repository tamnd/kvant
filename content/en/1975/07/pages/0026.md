In the next section we will calculate the value of the magnetic flux quantum $\Phi_0$. It turns out to be equal to $2 \cdot 10^{-15} \text{вб}$. This is the value of the minimum portion of magnetic flux. The value of the magnetic induction quantum, as can be seen from formula $\Phi = \pi r^2 B$, depends on the radius of the cylinder. In the experiment of Deaver and Fairbank it was equal to $1.3 \cdot 10^{-3} \text{см}$, and the magnetic flux quantum corresponded to the field $B \approx 5 \cdot 10^{-6} \text{мл}$. This is the maximum value. It is always 10 times less than the Earth's magnetic field induction.

Magnetic flux quantum

We will return to the experiment in which the quantization of magnetic flux was discovered, and now we will move on to the question: how to calculate the value of the magnetic flux quantum $\Phi_0$? To answer this question, we will use the analogy with the planetary model of atoma and accept the representations of Bohr's theory to the current circulating in the flask (or cylinder).

The electrons moving in a superconducting flask are similar to atomic electrons located in the Bohr orbit. Let us write down the conditions for quantizing the angular momentum of these electrons:

$$mvr = N\hbar.$$

Now, $r$ should be understood not as the radius of the electron orbit, but as the radius of the bulb in which the superconducting current circulates. Since the radius of the bulb is a quantity determined, condition (*) can be considered as a condition for quantizing the electron momentum $p = mv$. Pulse quantization means that the current in the bulb can only take on a discrete series of values. And this in turn leads to quantization of the magnetic flux. Let's find how $p$ and $\Phi$ are related to each other.

The energy possessed by the current $I$ flowing through a circuit with inductance $L$ is equal to $E = \frac{LI^2}{2}$. The same formula is valid for the case of a superconducting circuit *).

Since the magnetic flux is equal to $\Phi = LI$, the expression for the energy can be written as

$$E = \frac{I\Phi}{2}.$$

The strength of the current created in the flask $n$ by electrons moving at a speed $v$ is very high. Substituting this expression into (1), we arrive at the following formula for energy:

$$E = \frac{\Phi ne}{2\pi r} \cdot \frac{v}{2}.$$

The energy of a particle moving at a speed of $v$ is known to be equal to $E = \frac{mv^2}{2} = \frac{pv}{2}$, where $p$ is the momentum of the particle. Consequently, the energy of $n$ electrons moving at a speed of $v$ in a superconducting column is equal to

$$E = \frac{npv}{2}.$$

*) “Appendix II” at the end of the article shows the choice of formula $E = \frac{LI^2}{2}$.
