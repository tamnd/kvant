About diffusion in crystals

“This book is not a textbook, not a reference book, not a monograph, but a story about one of the aspects of the life of crystals” - this is how Professor Ya. E. Geguzin begins his book *). More precisely, this is not a story, but 80 short stories about one of the types of thermal motion of atoms in crystals - about the wandering of atoms around a crystal. Behind every cold poke of crystals lies the active movement of atoms, diverse and incessant. Atoms not only vibrate, that is, they seem to trample around a number of places. They can change places and wander around the crystal. If the crystal is free from various imperfections and no external forces act on it, then the wandering of atoms around the crystal occurs freely, randomly. But if a small sign of “disorder” appears in the crystal - a scratch, a mash, if the crystal is sloped or stretched, if the crystal is given an unusual shape, if the number of impurity atoms in one part of the crystal is greater than in another - in a word, if the state of the crystal is nonequilibrium, now the flows of atoms become directed - diffusion begins. For example, the author widely uses a figurative analogy: “...I imagine a mountain, from the top of which stones are rolling down to the foot... Each stone has excess energy, which will be released when the stone rolls down to the foot of the mountain... There are many stones, they roll, collide,..., after which one moves to the foot even more rapidly, and others begin to move upward. The fate of each stone can be different and variable. However, they all ultimately strive for the foot of the mountain.” The author likens a crystal with many signs of disequilibrium to a mountain and a stone at its top. Each sign of disequilibrium of a crystalline body is a stone on the top of a mountain, and the process of directed movement of atoms - the process of a diffuse arrangement of disequilibrium - is similar to the rolling of a stone to the foot. The stone rolls down to the foot of the mountain - the excess energy that it possesses at the top is released. The crystal is freed from disequilibrium - excess energy is released, the crystal moves from an unstable state to a more stable one. “And it may turn out,” writes Ya. E. Geguzin, “that the crystal considers it expedient to aggravate one of the signs of disequilibrium in order for other disequilibrium to be smoothed out faster and more completely.”

Essays by Ya. E. Geguzin tell about many free phenomena that accompany diffusion or are explained by diffusion. “Diffusion requires emptiness” is the title of one of the essays. It turns out that in the structure of the crystal there are, as it were, specially prepared empty spaces for wandering atoms - so-called vacancies, “atoms of emptiness”: in some place in the structure of the crystal there is not enough atom.

How do these vacancies behave? Imagine an empty seat in a row of spectators at a movie, somewhere in the middle of the row. You want to keep him busy. You can, of course, walk along the entire row, disturbing other spectators, or you can do it differently: ask them to move - everyone will move to the next place, and as a result, the empty, vacant place will seem to spill out to you from the middle of the row, as if the empty place itself is missing along the row. This is how vacancies travel in a crystal. And don't think that this happens rarely. Ya. E.
