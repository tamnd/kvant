Second definition. Two nodes are said to be equivalent, or of the same type, if they are isotopic or if one of them is isotopic to the mirror image of the other.

According to this definition, every node is equivalent to its mirror image. But figure eight is even isotopic!

Exercise 3. Prove this by “tying” the knot at figure 7, b (the “left” eight) into a “right” eight.

Nodes that, like the figure eight, are isotopic to their mirror image are called mirror nodes. Are there non-mirror nodes? Yes: in 1914, the German mathematician Dehn, one of the founders of the theory of knots, proved the non-mirror nature of the clover leaf (see figure 7, a). In other words, equivalent nodes are not always isotopic (isotopic nodes are equivalent by definition). Although the square knot and granny knot are very similar (Fig. 8, a and 8, b), they are not only non-isotopic, but also nonequivalent. Strict proof of this

§ 6. Knot invariants

By what criteria are nodes distinguished? The mere feeling that two knots are “similar” or “dissimilar” is not enough: even such simple knots as a clover leaf and a figure eight can be depicted and tied in different ways (we have already compared figure 4, b, d and 2, a with Figures 7, a and 7, b). For us, only those characteristics and properties of nodes are important that do not depend on the method of their implementation or representation and which therefore coincide for all nodes of the same type.
