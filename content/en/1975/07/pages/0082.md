9. Let's replace the condition according to which the knights should not retreat with another - the permutation consists of a minimum number of moves. Let us now use figure 13. Here the letters in the circles correspond to the verticals of the board, and if two verticals are connected by a knight’s move, then the corresponding circles are connected by a segment.

By rearranging the knights in a circle (counterclockwise), we will eventually place them on the desired verticals. However, such a rearrangement would require too many moves. To reduce their number, you should use internal segments whenever possible. In this case, the number of moves will be 19; these moves are: de, fd, gf, eg, ce, bc, db, fd, hf, gh, eg, ce, ac, ba, db, fd, ef, ce, dc (try to prove that this number of moves is minimal, try to prove it yourself). It is easy to see that in this decision neither the white nor the black knights retreat. Thus, we not only solved Loyd's problem, but also found the shortest permutation.

Note that the method we used is similar to the well-known “buttons and threads” method by G. Dudeney. He also solves, for example, the problem of rearranging two white and two black knights standing in the corners of the board $3 \times 3$ (see «Kvant», 1971, No. 9, p. 55). A number of similar problems can be found in «Kvant», 1973, No. 1, p. 63.
