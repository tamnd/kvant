Basically, every “lucky” ticket is “beautiful”. However, not every “beautiful” ticket will be “lucky”. For example, ticket 100,748 will be “beautiful”, but will not be “lucky”. So, if we denote the number of “lucky” tickets by $C$, then we can write the inequality

$$C<111\,112.$$

“But this is still not a complete solution to the problem,” said Misha. “We get that there are less than 111,112 “lucky” tickets, but we don’t know how much.” Is it possible to show that there are more than a certain number of them? I've heard this called "bottom estimating."

“We can give an estimate from below,” answered Georgy Vladimirovich, “I’m only afraid that it will be quite rough.” Let's call “excellent” tickets those whose number consists of two completely identical halves, for example 287 287. There are exactly 1000 such tickets, namely 000 000, then 001 001, 002 002, ... up to 999 999. Naturally, there are fewer “excellent” tickets than “lucky” ones, so we can write down the following estimate:

$$1000<C<111\,112.$$

Here the upper estimate is more than 100 times higher than the lower estimate, so this result can hardly be considered a solution to the problem.

“Perhaps the estimate above can be improved somewhat,” I said, “by using the criterion of divisibility by 11.”

-What is this sign? - Misha asked. “We didn’t go through it.”

- It's very simple. Let's add up all the numbers in the odd places, then separately add the numbers in the even places. So, if the difference of the resulting sums is divisible by 11, then the entire number is divisible by 11, and vice versa, any number divisible by 11 has this property.

“What does this sign have to do with “lucky” tickets?” Misha asked in surprise.

— The most direct thing, but tell me first, have you heard of “lucky Moscow style” tickets?

- Oh, yes! Muscovites consider a ticket “lucky” if the sum of the numbers in even places is equal to the sum of the numbers in odd places. These are the weirdos!

- Firstly, you are an eccentric if you believe that “lucky” tickets can bring good luck, and secondly, Muscovites call the same tickets “lucky” as Leningraders, and the tickets that we call “lucky in Moscow” they call “lucky in Leningrad.” This is how “roller coasters” are called “Russian” in America. But that's not the point. It’s quite easy to check that the ticket numbers that you call “lucky in Moscow” are divisible by 11. Right?

“That’s right,” answered Misha.

- And there are no more of these tickets than numbers from 0 to 999,999 divisible by 11.

— That is, no more than 90,910! - Georgy Vladimirovich exclaimed.

“What kind of tickets are there more,” asked Misha, ““lucky” or “happy in Moscow”?

“It’s not at all difficult to establish that there are as many of them as others,” I answered.

“You will also say, “not difficult,” Misha chuckled, “we don’t know how many of both there are.”

Thus, we have established a one-to-one correspondence between those and other tickets. And it follows that there are the same number of them. Right?

— What will be the sum of the digits of the number if the last three digits in the “lucky” ticket are replaced by the difference between 9 and these digits? - asked Georgy Vladimirovich.

“Now,” Misha thought, “sooo... three times nine is twenty-seven... minus... plus... That turns out to be 27!” But again we get a one-to-one correspondence! Georgy Vladimirovich, it follows that there are as many “lucky” tickets as tickets with a sum of digits of 27.

“That’s right,” he answered.
