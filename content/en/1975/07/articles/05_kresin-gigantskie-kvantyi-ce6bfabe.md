Quantum theory and low-temperature physics emerged almost simultaneously, at the beginning of this century. At first they developed independently of each other, but later a deep connection between these “peers” was revealed. First of all, it became clear that without quantum physics it is impossible to explain the phenomena observed at low temperatures. Subsequently, and especially in recent years, low-temperature physics itself has made important contributions to quantum theory.

Quantum is a fundamental concept of modern physics. The importance of this concept is supported by the name of the magazine you are reading at the moment. It is one of the main provisions of quantum physics that the purpose of a number of physical quantities, such as energy, momentum, etc., in the definitions of quantizing conditions, that is, they can only apply a discrete series of values. But in non-Advenan times it was believed that quantization concerns only the microcosm, that is, it is characteristic of processes occurring in atoms, atomic nuclei, etc. Indeed, when studying macroscopic objects we are dealing with a huge number of particles. And although the behavior of each individual particle is described by the laws of quantum physics, in general, in a macrosoptic sample, the quantization of energy, momentum and other physical quantities does not manifest itself due to the chaotic thermal motion of the particles. Quantum efficient ones seem to be successfully “fading away.” Thus, the thermal movement of atoms massages quantum laws. However, the study of some macroscopic objects, the sizes of which are hundreds of thousands of times larger than atomic ones. Near absolute zero, when thermal motion does not play a significant role, direct observation of quantum laws becomes possible.

The two phenomena of macroscopic quantization are discussed further in this article.

Magnetic flux quantization

First we turn to the phenomenon of superconductivity. Superconductors, as is known, are remarkable in that at a certain temperature (it is called critical and denoted $T_k$) electrical resistance disappears. This phenomenon was discovered in 1911 by the Dutch scientist Kamerlingh Onnes. But the nature of superconductivity became clear almost half a century later, in 1957, when the theory of superconductivity was finally constructed.

In this article we will not dwell on the explanation of superconductivity.

Let's talk about some of the phenomena observed in the world of superconductors, phenomena in which, perhaps, the laws of quantum physics are most clearly manifested.

Let's start with the following experiment. Let us excite an electric current in a metal ring (for example, using electromagnetic induction). At room temperature, the current in the ring will quickly decay. The flow of current is accompanied by thermal losses, and these losses lead to current attenuation.

A completely different picture is observed in the case when the temperature is close to absolute zero and the metal is a superconductor. Under these conditions, the once energized current becomes undamped. In one of the optics, the current circulated in the superconducting ring for 2.5 years, and if the experiment had not been stopped, it could have circulated indefinitely.

At first glance, the described picture of undamped current in a superconducting ring seems easy to explain. In fact, in superconductors, current flows without any resistance. There are no joyle losses in them. Therefore, electrons that do not lose their energy properties can move around the ring for as long as desired.

However, the situation is much more complicated. Of course, there are no thermal losses in a superconductor. Nevertheless, the picture remains mysterious and completely inexplicable from the point of view of classical physics.

Let's consider this issue in more detail. The movement of electrons forming a current in the ring is not uniform. Electrons move in a circle and, therefore, accelerate. But the acceleration of the movement of an electric charge is always accompanied by the emission of electromagnetic waves *). Must emit electricity

A problem has already arisen in the history of physics.

In 1911, Rutherford, based on his classical experiments on α-particle scattering, created a planetary model of the atom. He proposed (we have now reduced ourselves to such a planetary model that the word “suggested” even sounds strange) that an atom consists of a nucleus and electrons rotating around it in circular orbits.

The main difficulty of the planetary model of the atom was associated with the acceleration of the motion of electrons around the nucleus. An electron moving with centripetal acceleration should emit electromagnetic waves, while its energy should decrease, and this should lead to its fall onto the nucleus. In other words, from the basic principles of the physics of electromagnetic phenomena, the conclusion follows that the planetary model of the atom is unstable. But in reality, atoms are stable.

The study of this fundamental difficulty standing in the way of the recognition of the planetary model of the atom led to the creation of Bohr's quantum theory. Bohr retained the planetary model of the atom and came to the conclusion that in the microcosm there are laws that are radically different from the laws established by physicists when studying the ordinary macrocosm around us.

Let us now return to the story about a superconducting ring in which an undamped electric current circulates. In this case, the emission of electromagnetic waves does not occur for the same reason that an electron does not emit when moving around an atomic nucleus. In both cases, the laws of quantum physics play a major role. But if, when studying the properties of an atom, we encounter quantization in the microcosm, which we cannot observe directly, then superconductivity provides an example of quantization of a macroscopic quantity that is directly observable by us - current strength.

It turns out that the current strength in a superconducting ring cannot take on any numerical value and change continuously. For the entire electronic collective moving in such a ring, a giant “Bohr” orbit arises. Just as in the planetary model of the atom, the allowed orbits of an electron are characterized by certain values ​​of angular momentum and energy, giant orbits of electrons in superconducting rings are characterized by well-defined values ​​of current strength.

Since the superconducting current is closely related to the magnetic field (see Fig. 1), the quantization of the current leads to the fact that the induction $B$ of the field can only take on a discrete series of values. Consequently, the magnetic flux $\Phi = \pi r^2 B$ through the cross section of the superconducting ring will also be quantized. In other words, the magnetic flux $\Phi$ can be a multiple of a certain minimum portion - the magnetic flux quantum $\Phi_0$, that is, $\Phi = N\Phi_0$, where $N$ is an integer. Magnetic flux is a macroscopic quantity, and the possibility of its quantization means the transition to giant

An experiment in which the quantization of magnetic flux was observed was carried out by American physicists B. Deaver and W. Fairbank in 1961. A thin layer of tin was deposited over the entire surface of a copper wire with a diameter of $1{,}3 \cdot 10^{-3}$ cm. Thus, a “hollow” cylinder of tin was obtained, and a superconducting current was excited in this cylinder at temperatures below $T_к = 3{,}8°$ K (at $T < 3{,}8°$ K tin becomes a superconductor). (Copper is not a superconductor, and therefore it played the role of a core necessary for preparing a superconducting cylinder. In what follows, we will only talk about a tin cylinder. A continuous current circulated in it.) To measure the magnetic flux in the cavity surrounded by this current, an induction coil was placed at one end of the cylinder. The cylinder was set into oscillatory motion along its axis, the oscillation frequency reaching 100 Hz. At the same time, an induction current arose in the coils, allowing one to judge the magnitude of the magnetic flux located inside the cylinder. Experience has shown that the flow equal to $\Phi = \pi r^2 B$ ($r$ is the radius of the cylinder) takes on a discrete series of values:

$$\Phi = N\Phi_0,$$

$N$ is an integer ($N = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$), and $\Phi_0$ is a magnetic flux quantum.

$$mvr = N\hbar.$$

"Vortices" in superfluid helinium

Another example of quantization, which occurs on a huge scale compared to the atomic scale, is also observed at low temperatures, when another “superphenomenon” is studied - the superfluidity of liquid gel.

It is known that at a temperature of $T = 4,2^\circ\text{K}$ and normal pressure, helium passes from a gaseous state into a liquid state and, with a further increase in temperature down to absolute zero, does not solidify (only under a pressure of 25 amperes at a temperature close to absolute zero can helium be converted into a solid state). But this does not exhaust the changes in the properties of this substance. At $T = 2,17^\circ\text{K}$ helium goes into a special state - superfluid. The phenomenon of superfluidity, discovered by academician P. L. Kapitsa in 1937, consists in the ability of liquid gel at a temperature less than $T = 2,17^\circ\text{K}$ to flow in narrow capillaries without any friction. Helinium at a temperature less than $2,17^\circ\text{K}$ is called HeII - unlike HeI ($4,2^\circ\text{K} > T > 2,17^\circ\text{K}$), it is an ordinary liquid.

The phenomena of superfluidity and superconductivity are similar to each other. We can say that superconductivity is the superfluidity of electrons. The analogy between superconductivity and superfluidity turns out to be profound, and in part, it manifests itself in the possibility of observing macroscopic quantum phenomena both in superconductors and in superfluid gelini.

**Rotating helium**

Let us study the properties of HeII poured into a cylindrical vessel rotating around an axis (see Fig. 4). We will start from the two-fluid model described above and try to imagine the picture of such rotation. The normal component, due to friction against the cylinder walls, must be involved in the movement of the wall. As for the superfluid component, at first glance, it seems that it should remain motionless.

What observable effect should this peculiar picture lead to? As is known, when a liquid poured into a vessel rotates, the free surface of the liquid takes the shape of a paraboloid. Since in our experiment not all helium rotates, but only its normal component, we should expect that when HeII rotates, this surface should be less curved than in the case when HeI is in the cylinder.

What does the experiment show? The shape of the surface of rotating HeII turns out to be the same as in a normal liquid.

Liquid helium rotates completely calmly (this corresponds to laminar flow) only at very low rotation speeds (usually less than $\sim 10^{-2}\ см/с$). ## Appendix I

"Vortices" in superconductors

This application describes how a magnetic field penetrates certain superconducting substances. This process is quantum in nature: the field penetrates the substance in separate portions. Before moving directly to the description of this phenomenon, let us briefly dwell on the issue of using superconductors to create strong magnetic fields.

At first glance, it may seem that superconductors are ideal for creating arbitrarily strong magnetic fields. In fact, when using electromagnets one has to take into account heat losses. An increase in current strength in a conventional solenoid also entails an increase in Joule losses. And if we require very strong magnetic fields, then, along with large expenditures of power, complex cooling systems are needed to maintain the current to prevent the wires from overheating and even melting. When using a superconducting current that flows without any resistance, these difficulties are completely absent.

However, the matter is not as simple as it might seem at first glance. Let us assume that an ordinary superconductor is in an external magnetic field. It cannot penetrate inside, into the thickness of the superconductor. The fact is that there is an inextricable connection between the magnetic field

⟦column⟧

and superconducting current. Exposure to an external magnetic field leads to the appearance of a superconducting current in the surface layer of the metal. This surface current creates its own magnetic field, which is opposite to the applied field. The theory of superconductivity proves that the magnetic field inside the superconducting substance is completely compensated (this phenomenon is called the Meissner effect).

If we create a superconducting current in a metal, then a magnetic field will arise in the surrounding space. It cannot penetrate a superconducting sample. However, such a picture is observed as long as the current and the corresponding field are not very large ($\sim 3 \cdot 10^{-3}$ $тл$). When the field reaches a certain value $B_\mathrm{к}$ (critical value), it penetrates into the superconductor and, “bursting” into it, destroys the superconducting state. Therefore, for a long time it seemed that obtaining strong magnetic fields (large $B_\mathrm{к}$) using superconductors was impossible.

Later it turned out, however, that there are superconductors with the help of which very large (superstrong) magnetic fields can be obtained. These superconductors - they are called type II superconductors - can be in the so-called mixed state, the theory of which was developed by the Soviet theoretical physicist A. A. Abrikosov.

If a type II superconductor through which current flows is in a magnetic field, then at a certain value of induction $B_{\mathrm{к}1}$ (this value is called the lower critical field), the field begins to partially penetrate into the thickness of the superconducting sample. Electrons in a superconductor, under the influence of the Lorentz force, begin to move in circles (if their speed is perpendicular to the field lines). The picture that arises is similar to the picture of rotating He II: vortices appear in the superconductor. Superconducting electrons rotate around the vortex axis; the rotation speed increases as it approaches the axis, and at some distance from it it occurs

That is, each output carries one flux quantum, and in the form of such separate portions the magnetic field penetrates inside the superconductor.

As the field increases ($B > B_{K1}$), the number of normal regions increases, the output filaments move closer together, and at a certain field value $B_{K2}$ (this value is called the upper cremic field), when the distance between the output filaments becomes $\sim 10^{-6}$ m, superconductivity is completely destroyed and the substance passes into the normal state.

This peculiar pattern of penetration of a magnetic field into a type II superconductor makes it possible to use them to obtain merged magnetic fields. The value of the upper critical field $B_{K2}$ can be very large. For example, for the alloy Nb$_3$Sn $B_{K2} \approx 20$ ml. (Type II superconductors are alloys with a high impurity content.)

When using superconductors of the second type to obtain confluent magnetic fields, the energy costs are hundreds to thousands of times less than when using electromagnets. For example, to obtain a 10 ml field using a conventional solenoid requires a power supply of 5000 kW and, in addition, requires a complex installation for cooling the solenoid winding. When using superconductors, only energy is required to obtain a liquid gel (at the temperature of the liquid gel, the solenoid metal is in a superconducting state). But for this in the example given, a power of only 5 kW is required. That is, energy costs are reduced by 1000 times.

Appendix II

Let us present the derivation of the formula for the energy possessed by the current flowing through the conductor.

Let us consider the circuit along which the current $I$ is projected. The magnetic flux $\Phi$, which created this current, returns with an increase in $I$ according to the law $\Phi = LI$, where $L$ is the inductance of the circuit. Let us assume that at the initial time $t_0 = 0$ the current in the circuit is $I_0$, and, starting from this moment, the current strength uniformly returns equal to $I_t$. It is clear that an increase in current strength is accompanied by an increase in the energy of the magnetic field that created the current.

During time $t$, charge $q$ moved along the conductor. The charge of motion is eradicated, since the current strength returns (the current density is $j = nev$; at a constant $n$, the increase in $j$ is due to the increase in $v$). In order to obtain a formula for the current energy, we calculate the work of electric forces to move the charge $q$ along the entire closed circuit. The work is expressed by the formula $A = qE$, where $E$ is the electromotive force, equal to $E = IR + \frac{\Delta \Phi}{\Delta t}$ in an ordinary conductor.

The first term on the right side of this equality is associated with the total ohmic losses, and the second is due to the presence of effective self-induction, which weakens the current. It is clear that $q = I_{cp} \cdot t$ ($I_{cp}$ is the average current strength, equal to $I_{cp} = \frac{I_0 + I_t}{2}$ for a uniform increase in current). Thus, equal turns out to be equal

$$A = \frac{I_0 + I_t}{2} t E.$$

In superconductors $R = 0$ and $E = \frac{\Delta \Phi}{\Delta t}$. Since the formula is $\Phi = LI$, then $E = L \frac{\Delta I}{\Delta t}$. In our case, the change in point $\Delta I$ is equal to $\Delta I = I_t - I_0$, and the time interval is $\Delta t = t$. Then $E = L \frac{I_t - I_0}{t}$. Substituting this value for $E$ into the formula to work, we arrive at the following expression:

$$A = \frac{I_0 + I_t}{2} t \cdot L \frac{I_t - I_0}{t}.$$

The work thus turns out to be equal

$$A = \frac{LI^2}{2} - \frac{LI_0^2}{2}.$$

It can be seen that the amount of work is described by a change in the value $\frac{LI^2}{2}$. It is natural to consider it equal to the current energy, so

$$E = \frac{LI^2}{2}.$$

(The attentive reader will discover an analogy between the above derivation of the formula for current energy and the derivation of the expression for kinetic energy in ordinary mechanics.)
