§ 1. Could Alexander the Great discover the theory of knots?

The books of ancient historians have preserved for us a legend about how the Macedonian king Alexander, walking with his army during his victorious campaign into the depths of Asia through the Persian city, visited the temple and saw one of the attractions of Percy - a knot woven by the sage Gordius; legend said that the one who unties this knot will become the king of the universe. The young king cut the knot with a sword.

Should we admire Alexander or keep in mind that with the same blow of the sword he deprived himself, through guilt, of the opportunity to become the founder of the theory of knots?

Moreover, this problem was solved only in our time, about fifteen years ago, when a method was invented that made it possible to determine by the type or description of a knot whether a given knot was untied or not.

§ 5. Types of nodes can be defined in different ways

We would like our knot theory to preserve the naive idea of ​​“equally tied” knots that we all have. This gives rise to the first of two (different) definitions of the *type* of a node.

First definition: Two nodes are said to be *isotopic*, or have the same *isotopic type*, if one node can be “tied” into another without cutting it or allowing self-intersections.

In practice, the isotopicity of two nodes can sometimes be proven either using a rope ring or a series of figure-diagrams. For example, as we already know, the top node is isotopic to Jacob's ladder and both of them are isotopic to the circle. Here's another example: a *single* knot (see Fig. 4, a, b) is isotopic (check it out!) to the simplest knot after a circle, shown in figure 7, a. Due to its resemblance to the outline of a clover leaf, this knot is called *cloverleaf*, or *shamrock*. Draw the “*double bow*” knot yourself; already at the age of two or three years you were able to prove (by pulling the free end of the string) that “*bow*” (see Fig. 4, c, d) and “double bow” are isotopic to a single knot, and therefore to a clover leaf.

§ 6. Knot invariants

By what criteria are nodes distinguished? The mere feeling that two knots are “similar” or “dissimilar” is not enough: even such simple knots as a clover leaf and a figure eight can be depicted and tied in different ways (we have already compared figure 4, b, d and 2, a with Figures 7, a and 7, b). For us, only those characteristics and properties of nodes are important that do not depend on the method of their implementation or representation and which therefore coincide for all nodes of the same type.

Such features are called knot invariants *).

Most of the known invariants are quite difficult to define and calculate.

The most studied and convenient of algebraic invariants - the knot group - is calculated according to precisely described rules, which, in principle, can be memorized by any high school student; however, to understand both these rules and (which, of course, is more important) the very definition of node groups, you need to know the beginning of topology and algebra calculations. Using the node group, one usually proves that a node is non-trivial or that two nodes are not equivalent.

§ 8. Arithmetic of knots

If two knots are tied side by side on the same rope, then the resulting knot is called the product or composition of these two knots.

The operation of node composition is very similar to the operation of multiplying integers.

1. The role of the “unit” in the “multiplication” of two knots is obviously played by the trupal knot—the circle. Denoting it by the letter E, and the composition of two nodes A and B by A # B, we obtain A = E # A = A # E (see Fig. 14, a).

2. Composition is a commutative operation:

A # B = B # A.

This is easy to prove by running one knot “through” another along the rope (see, for example, Fig. 14, b-e).

§ 9. About simple knots

The existence of simple knots was proven more than twenty years after the simple ones were collected and systematized; in 1926, American mathematicians Alexaan Holder (one of the founders of knot theory) and Briggs published a table of knot diagrams (at that time they were not yet called simple), similar to tables of prime numbers*) (p. 17). The place of a node in the table is determined, first of all, by its minimum number of crossings, and among nodes with the same number - first of all, by the “confectionery number” of the node, and then by other invariants that are still unfamiliar to us.

Exercise 11. Find all the simple nodes in Figures 2-8 and their diagrams in the table. Calculate the values ​​of the invariants M, I, K, and P for these nodes.

The proof of both the existence of simple nodes and the decomposability of any non-trivial node into a finite number of simple ones is based on the substitution property of the genus of nodes: the genus of the composition of two nodes is equal to the sum of the genus of “factors”, that is:

$$P(A \# B) = P(A) + P(B).$$

We talked about this property in § 10, and now we will use it twice.

1°. So, consider the non-trivial node $A$. If it is simple, then the existence of simple nodes has already been proven, as well as the decomposability of the node $A$ into a finite number of simple ones. Otherwise, we decompose $A$ into a composition of two nodes, then, if possible, we decompose which of the “factor” nodes, and so on. In no more than $(P(A) - 1)$ steps we will obtain the expansion we need - the genus of each of the (nontrivial!) “factors” is at least 1, and the sum of them all is $P(A)$.

2°. Let us now give an extremely short proof of the statement “no non-trivial knot can be untied by tying another one next to it” (see § 8, property 3). Indeed, if $A \# B = E$, then $P(A \# B) = P(A) + P(B) = 0$, so $P(A) = P(B) = 0$, and therefore $A = B = E$.
