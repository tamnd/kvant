Henri Lebesgue
(on the centenary of his birth)

Henri Lebesgue was born years ago, on June 28, 1875, in the small French town of Beauvais. After successfully graduating from school, Lebesgue entered the Paris Ecole Normale Supérieure, where he attended lectures by J. Tannery, C. Jordan and other outstanding mathematicians. After graduating, he worked as a mathematics teacher at the Lyceum in Nancy, continuing the scientific work begun in Paris. Having completed his doctoral dissertation in 1902, Lebesgue moved to the department of mathematics at the provincial university in Rennes, from where he moved to Poitiers, and only in 1910 he returned to Paris, where he became a professor at the Sorbonne, and from 1921 at the Collec de France.

For his scientific merits, Lebesgue was elected a member of the French Academy of Sciences in 1922, an honorary member of the London Mathematical Society in 1924, and a member of the Royal Society (English Academy of Sciences) in 1930. He received academic prizes four times, wrote several books and many scientific papers. After a serious illness, Lebesgue died on July 26, 1941.

The quiet, leisurely life of an armchair scientist! But no matter how unpretentious the external events of Lebet’s life were, his scientific achievements turned out to be so bright and profound. If you randomly open any page of any book on the theory of functions of a real (or, as they also say, real) variable, on functional analysis or on the theory of probability, you will almost certainly come across the words “Lebet measure”, “Lebet point”, “Lebet integral”, or the concepts introduced by him “summable function”, “almost everywhere”, and sometimes Lebet’s name turns out to be an encrypted symbol $L^p$: after all $L$ is the first letter of his surname Lebesque, and $L^p$ is the space of functions whose $p$th power is Lebes summable. Almost all the concepts related to modern measure and integral theory go back to the work of Lebet, and the introduction of these concepts in some sense turned out to be a turning point in the transition from 19th-century mathematics to 20th-century science.

In the development of science there are long periods, reminiscent of the rapid flow of a large river. During these periods, based on a ready-made stock of concepts and ideas, scientists obtain new scientific results and expand the scope of application of already known methods. But little by little, scientific problems are accumulating, the solutions of which are impossible within the framework of existing scientific ideas.

The head of the French mathematical scale, Charles Hermite, expressed himself even more harshly on this topic. In his letter to the prominent Dutch mathematician T. But young people were drawn to new areas of science, not paying attention to the questioning mathematicians of the previous generation. In France, they were inspired by the lectures of Jules Tannery and Camille Jordan, in which mathematical analysis was built on the creative basis of precise definitions, impeccable proofs, and iron logic. They stressed at these lectures that although discontinuous functions were not encountered in the applications that existed at that time, they should be studied on an equal basis with continuous ones, since this is required by a correct understanding of the intersymes of mathematics; they learned that the definitions of lengths, areas and volumes, suitable only for smooth lines and surfaces, are too narrow, and that more general definitions must be sought. These ideas accumulated, turned into convictions, and became an incentive for scientific work. In 1898, the young French scientist R. Barashchit wrote a dissertation in which he gave a deep classification of discontinuous functions. In the same year, a book by one of the brightest leaders of youth, the twenty-seven-legged mathematician Emile Borel, appeared, dedicated to the new theory of functions. Anira Lebesgue, who was beginning his scientific career at that time, joined this scientific school.

One of Lebesgue's first works was a note “On non-film developable surfaces.” This work angered classical mathematicians. One of the leaders of geometric time, Gaston Darboux, was especially indignant. The very title of the work seemed counterintuitive to him. The fact is that developable surfaces are called surfaces, a sufficiently small one of which can be obtained by bending a piece of the plane. But all developing surfaces known by that time - cylinders, cones and others - were made up of rectilinear generatrices. They were, as they say, linear. Therefore, talking about non-film developing surfaces seemed completely absurd. But the whole point was that the young author understood developing surfaces differently than the classical geometers. He considered this not only the surfaces obtained by carefully bending a sheet of paper, but also the surfaces that are obtained if this sheet is crumpled (explaining this work to his friends, he said: “Imagine a crumpled handkerchief”). It turned out that it is possible to “crumple” a piece of a plane in such a way that after this there is not a single straight segment left on it. Of course, the resulting developing surface did not have a tangent plane; it was always made of folds and kinks. And it is clear why geometers missed it when classifying developing surfaces - they only dealt with smooth surfaces.

Lebesgue's interest in developable surfaces was not accidental. He was interested in a general question posed by Camille Jordan: how to determine the area of ​​a surface without assuming its smoothness. It is easy to find the area of ​​a crumpled developing surface - just unfold it onto a plane. But it is impossible to calculate the area of ​​such a surface using formulas, we offer classical mathematics - they include arbitrary ones, and they exist only for smooth surfaces.

Solving this particular problem, Lebesgue came to general ideas about what the measure of a set is, how to measure the lengths, areas and volumes of the most bizarre figures. Many mathematicians before him were interested in this question. Jordan's definition turned out to be the most successful.

Jordan's main idea was as follows. There are figures that obviously have an area, for example, polygons. Now suppose that the figure $\Phi$ has the following property: *whatever small positive number $\varepsilon$ we take,*

In another way, the same definition can be formulated as follows: a figure $\Phi$ is *Jordan measurable* if for any $\varepsilon > 0$ it can be represented as the union of the polygonal region $\Phi_\varepsilon$ and the remainder, which is covered by a polygonal region having an area smaller than $\varepsilon$. The measure $m\,(\Phi)$ of such a figure is contained between numbers of the form $m\,(\Phi_\varepsilon)$ and numbers of the form $m\,(\Phi_\varepsilon) + \varepsilon$, where $m\,(\Phi_\varepsilon)$ is the area of ​​$\Phi_\varepsilon$.

This definition corresponds to the usual process of measuring the areas of figures. We resort to it in school when measuring the area of ​​a circle - regular inscribed polygons act as internal polygons, and the remainder consists of segments of the circle cut off by the sides of this polygon. These segments can be enclosed in triangles formed by chords and tangents (Fig. 3) and it can be shown that with a sufficiently large number of sides of the polygon, the total area of ​​these triangles is arbitrarily small.

The Jordan measure has many good properties, in particular, as it should be, the union of a finite number of pairwise disjoint Jordan measurable sets is measurable, and its measure is equal to the sum of the measures of the parts. The first of these principles is a far-reaching generalization of the ancient position: the measure of the whole is equal to the sum of the measures of its part; the second follows from the same position. Having obtained his principles, Borel found the measures of all the sets with which mathematicians of his time dealt. For example, from his principles it followed that any countable set of points has zero measure: after all, each of the points has zero measure, and then the union of these points has zero measure.

If the numbers $y_k$ and $y_{k+1}$ are given, then by $E_k$ we denote the set of those values ​​$x$ for which the inequality $y_k \leq f(x) < y_{k+1}$ holds. The Lebesgue integral sum is the sum $\sum_{k} y_k m(E_k)$.

Going to the preposition with an unlimited change in the segments of the partition, we obtain a number that is equal to the Lebesgue integral $\int_a^b f(x) \, dx$. Visually speaking, the essence of Lebesgue’s ideas is that for discontinuous functions, the “curvilinear trapezoid” had to be cut not parallel to the ordinat axis, but parallel to the abscissa axis.

Nowadays, the Lebesgue process of calculating the integral seems to be something that mathematicians take for granted. But at the beginning of the century he seemed to shake the very foundations of mathematics. And then Lebesgue came up with a new justification for the fact that his method of calculating integrals was natural. Perhaps he was influenced by the example of Hilbert, who gave the axiomat of geometry a little earlier. Similarly, Lebesgue formulated a system of requirements that an integral must satisfy, and proved that the only definition of an integral that satisfies all these requirements is the one he proposed.

In 1902, thanks to the support of C. Jordan and E. Picard, it was possible to overcome the prejudice of opponents of the new mathematical scale, and Lebesgue successfully completed his doctoral dissertation.

The creation of the Lebesgue measure and integral made it possible to overcome many difficulties that had accumulated by that time in mathematical analysis. Lebesgue’s idea turned out to be especially useful: it is not necessary to require that some property of a function be satisfied for all values ​​of the argument, but it is enough to study properties that are satisfied almost everywhere, that is, everywhere except for the set of points of zero measure. It turned out, for example, that if the function $y = f(x)$ is bounded and interpretable according to Lebesgue, then the function $F(x) = \int_a^x f(t) \, dt$ for almost all values ​​of $x$ is arbitrary, coinciding with $f(x)$. The Lebesgue integral is extremely useful in the theory of trigonometric series, that is, series of the form

$$\frac{a_n}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} (a_n \cos nx + b_n \sin nx).$$

Lebesgue himself occupied himself with this theory a lot and with enthusiasm.

At first glance, it seemed that the Lebesgue integral was too complex and subtle a tool to be used in applications; in practice, “wild” functions and sets also do not occur, and all practical problems of servicing by general integers. But in those years, the theory of infinite-dimensional spaces began to be developed, necessary for the study and solution of integral equations (equations containing the desired function under the integral sign). Such equations have many properties that concern mathematical physics, and methods for solving them were invented, based on constructing a sequence of functions that, in a certain sense, approaches the desired solution. It was not possible to prove, while remaining within the framework of classical mathematics, that such a sequence is somehow approaching. The state of affairs here does not resemble an attempt to solve the equation $x^2 = 2$, trying only with rational numbers. Of course, it is possible to construct a sequence of rational numbers that approaches the desired root, but the root itself is integral, and the sequence has no limit within the set of rational numbers. The triumph of the ideas of Lebesgue, Baer and Borel led to the fact that even Darboux changed his mind about the new mathematics, and, speaking in 1908 at the Mathematical Congress in Rome, he spoke about the fiery and inquisitive spirit of mathematics of the 20th century, about a science conducting its research in a completely new field with unknown prospects. He emphasized that 20th-century science is not afraid to attack the foundations of constructs that for so long seemed unshakable.

Later, the ideas that led to the creation of the Lebesgue measure and integral allowed A. N. Kolmogorov to construct the axiomatics of probability theory and N. Wiener to define the concept of measure for function spaces. Wherever ideas of measure penetrated, constructions and concepts dating back to Lebesgue were used. They turned out to be very useful in statistical physics, allowing A. Ya. Khinchin and J. von Neumann to substantiate some of the ideas of the American physicist Gibbs.

Lebesgue devoted the last twenty years of his life to problems of history and teaching mathematics. Naturally, he paid most attention to the theory of measuring quantities in high school. In a series of articles, later collected in the book “On the Measurement of Quantities” (it was also translated into Russian), he wittily criticized common misconceptions in the teaching of these issues. In his book, he showed the inconsistency of common definitions of length, area and volume, and criticized textbooks for striving for rigor in some places while making gross logical mistakes in others.

75 years have passed since Lebesgue created his theory of measure and integration. These concepts have forever entered mathematics - without them it is impossible to think of probability theory, functional analysis, statistical physics, or topological algebra.
