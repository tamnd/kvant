What are longitude and latitude?

The position of a point on the Earth's surface is determined by two coordinates: geographic latitude and geographic longitude. Do you know where these names came from? In the second century AD. e. Greek astronomer and geographer Claudius Ptolemy introduced the concept of length and width in relation to the measurement of countries, applications to the Mediterranean Sea, stretched from west to east. He called the results of measurements along the sea length, and across them - width. When translated into Russian, instead of these words, which can be attributed to any objects, special terms were introduced to characterize the position of the body on the earth's surface - longitude and latitude. Although these concepts, clearly illustrated on the globe, are widely known, not everyone knows their exact definition.

It is often said that latitude φ is the angular distance of a given point from the earth's equator, and longitude λ is the dihedral angle between the plane of a given meridiana and the plane of a conventionally selected initial meridiana. Consequently, the network of parallels and meridians plotted on the globe allows us to indicate the geographic coordinates of any point on the globe. Naturally, the coordinate value is necessary for orientation. But, being at some point on Earth, we ourselves must be able to determine our geographical coordinates. As is known, this is done with the help of astronomical observations. Suppose we have calculated the latitude and longitude of the point where we are located. Can we now, having found a point on the globe with such coordinates, be sure that we are located exactly at this point on the Earth? No! Even if the point on the globe is indicated with extreme accuracy.

The fact is that the Earth is not a sphere, and the globe is only a simplified visual model of the Earth. It is the irregularity (non-spherical) shape of the Earth that leads to discrepancies between the geographical coordinates obtained from astronomical observations and the coordinates determined for a given point on the globe on the map.

Then geographic latitude can be measured by two meridians from the equatorial plane to a given point, and longitude by the arc of the equator from a conditionally selected initial meridian to a given one.

The true shape of the Earth, called a geode, spring is complex, although close to an elliptical rotation, continuous in the direction of the stripes. The complexity of the shape is caused by the uneven distribution of mass within and on the surface of the Earth, in part, by the presence of materials from high mountains and deep basins in the oceans.

Due to the elliptical distance of the Earth, the corresponding line does not pass through the center of the Earth at any point, and geode irregularities also lead to lateral deviations from the outer line, so that it may not even cross the Earth’s axis at all. This gives rise to the so-called plumb deviations, which are expressed in incorrect changes in the direction of the plumb line when moving from point to point. And since in the practical determination of latitudes and longitudes one of the main directions along which astronomical instruments are oriented is a plumb line, the discrepancy between the true geographical coordinates of a point on the Earth and its coordinates on the globe becomes fully understandable.

Let’s formulate a clear definition of latitude and longitude. Latitude is the angle between the response line at a given location and the plane of the equator. It’s clear why we didn’t say “the plane of the earth’s equator.” Due to the deviation of the plumb line, the earth’s equator - a line at all points of which the geographic latitude is zero - deviates from the flat section of the Earth by the magnitude of the deviation of the plumb line, which can reach and even exceed, especially in mountainous areas, 10″, which in a linear measure corresponds to approximately 300 m. Everything said about the earth’s equator also applies to parallels - lines of equal latitude, which for the same reason are not plane curves.

A strict definition of geographic longitude should be as follows: longitude is a dihedral angle between two planes parallel to the Earth’s rotation axis, one of which contains a plumb line at the selected starting point, and the other contains a plumb line at the determined point. From the degree measure of longitude we can move on to time coordinates. This transition can be achieved using simple calculations: a rotation of the Earth around its own axis by 360° corresponds to 24 hours, therefore, 1 hour = 15°, 1 minute of time = 15′ arc, 1 second of time = 15″ arc. Obviously, at points that correspond to different longitudes, noon (and the corresponding midnight) occurs at different times. It is easy to understand that the difference (in time) between the moments of half a day in a given place and at a point, the longitude of which is taken to be zero, is the longitude of this place.

In 1884, by international agreement, the Greenwich Observatory, founded in 1675 in London, was taken as the starting point in determining longitudes. In different countries, various main public observatories were taken as exclusive points: in Russia - Pulkovo near St. Petersburg, in France - the Paris Observatory, etc. In the old days, longitudes were absent from one of the Canary Islands - Ferro, which was the westernmost point of the Old World, i.e. the continents of Europa, Asia and Africa. Therefore, all longitudes were considered in the same direction and of the same sign - east of Ferro.

Due to the deviation of the plumb line, all meridians - lines, all points of which have the same longitude - are also not flat curves obtained during the Earth's flow by planes containing the Earth's axis, but slightly

How accurate are geographic coordinates from astronomical observations? It all depends on the purpose of such definitions. The coordinates of astronomical observatories are more accurately determined, the latitude of which is known with an accuracy of 0°′,1, and longitude - up to 0.01 seconds of time. To estimate these values, it follows that 1″ of meridian arc corresponds to 31 m on the earth’s surface, 0°′.1 seconds of time at the equator is equal to 46 m, and in mid-latitudes it is almost half as much. Thus, the indicated accuracy corresponds to approximately 3 m on Earth. Therefore, for example, it is not enough to say that the latitude of the Paris Observatory is 48°50′11″.2 and the eastern longitude is 0°9′20″.93.

However, before the invention of radio, debt collection was a very difficult task in practice. Indeed, in the absence of direct communication, how can you find out how many hours, minutes and seconds of local time there are at a given moment in Greenwitz (i.e., at the starting point for determining debt), if you are hundreds or thousands of kilometers away? These were, for example, the best eclipses and eclipses of the satellites of Jupter (the use of the latter to determine longitude was pointed out by Galileo, who discovered the satellites of Jupter in 1610). Unfortunately, these phenomena do not occur instantly, but last for several minutes, so they can only be recorded with a corresponding error. In addition, there was the difficulty that eclipses of Jupter’s satellites are almost impossible to observe with the fingers of a rocking ship and these phenomena do not occur every day, and for several months of the year Jupter is not visible at all. Eclipses of the Moon are completely rare phenomena and occur no more than twice a year. Therefore, when traveling, it was necessary to wait several days or even weeks until it was possible to make several (to control and improve accuracy) such observations, and at sea for observation purposes this method of determining longitude was not at all suitable.

In the 16th century, another way was found to determine the local time of the initial meridin - using the so-called lunar distances, rules; its practical application became possible only after the invention of the sextag. The Moon circles the Earth every day, like a clock hand moving across the “dial” - the starry sky. But this hand moves through the clock slowly, 55 times slower than the clock hand of an ordinary clock. It is clear that by the movement of the clock hand the actual time can be determined, but very inaccurately due to the slowness of its movement. Obviously, determining time by the movement of the Moon is even less reliable, but the accuracy and subtlety of the divisions of the “dial”—the precisely known positions of the stars—helps here. True, the position of the Moon relative to these stars must be changed with very great accuracy, well at an angle of about 30°, so changing the lunar distance from the star with an accuracy of 1′ allows you to determine the time with an accuracy of two minutes. In order to use this method, you need to know very well all the features of the movement of the Moon, so that by observing the position of the Moon you can obtain the time of the initial meridian, the difference of which with the local time of this point will give the longitude of the latter. Obviously, this method imposes very high demands on celestial mechanics, which develops theories of the motion of the Moon that are more complex. The lunar distance method was not much superior in accuracy to the previous one, but it could be used at any time when the Moon and stars were visible, which is why it became widespread during travel, especially sea travel.

However, the method of lunar distances did not fully satisfy the sailors (mainly due to the complexity of the calculation and low accuracy). Sailing ships, depending on the strength and direction of the wind, often lasted in places out of sight of the shores, so it was very important to be able to determine the position of the ship (its latitude and longitude). In 1713, the English government, being especially interested in the safety of navigation (at that time England was the country with the most developed navy), awarded a huge prize of 20,000 feet sterling for the development of a reliable method for determining longitude with an accuracy of $1/2$ degrees. The problem of determining longitude received a completely new solution with the invention of the telegraph in the middle of the last century, which made it possible to transmit time signals of the prime meridian and determine the difference in local times of two points with previously unattainable accuracy - up to a fraction of a second. Nevertheless, the laying of submarine cables made it possible to connect different continents with each other. Finally, the universal solution itself arrived with the invention of the wireless telephone. Already in 1921, the New Holland radio station in Petrograd began transmitting so-called rhythmic time signals several times a day, consisting of 61 points per minute, so the intervals between points were less than a second by $1/60$ seconds. True, as a result of errors in the observatory clock and various technical interference, these signals were errors for a split second, but this could be taken into account later, after they were received at the observatory and subsequent astronomical observations. For this purpose, special biological data on the exact moment of broadcasting of past signals were published monthly, which makes it possible to retroactively correct the chronometer readings. This was, of course, necessary only when determining longitude with extreme accuracy; in navigation, where accuracy up to 1 second is more than enough, the published corrections of the transmitted time signals had no meaning. Now, thanks to the invention of quartz and then molecular clocks *), which store time to thousandths of a second for many months, the correction of transmitted radio signals is practically zero. The old problem of determining geographic longitude is obtaining the most accurate solution.

Comment

If you observe the starry sky for several hours, you will notice that the positions of the constellations change relative to the horizon. For example, in the eastern sky, stars rise higher above the horizon and move to the right. In the upper side of the sky, a large star describes a circle throughout the day. We can say that the entire firmament (celestial sphere) rotates around a celestial axis called the axis mundi. The points of intersection of the axis of the world with the celestial sphere are called the stripes of the world - the supernal and southern. The northern stripe is the one from which the rotation of the celestial sphere occurs clockwise (when viewed from the outside). This rotation actually appears, since the Earth actually rotates around its axes (in the opposite direction). Obviously, the axis of the world is parallel to the axis of rotation of the Earth.

In addition to the axis of the world and the stripes of the world, there are also celestial characteristic lines and points on the celestial sphere (Fig. 1). The SWNE plane is the horizon plane, the plane tangent to the Earth's surface at point C, where the observable is located. Line NS is the noon line (at noon the shadow of vertical objects falls along this line). A plumb line drawn at point C intersects the celestial sphere at the zenith point Z. The plane passing through points S, Z, P (northern stripes of the world), N is the plane of the celestial meridian. The celestial equator is the line of intersection of the plane perpendicular to the axis of the world and passing through the observable (point C) with the celestial sphere.

Which luminary crosses the celestial meridian twice per day; The phenomena of the passage of a luminary through the celestial meridian are called culminations. At the upper culmination the luminary is highest above the horizon, at the lower culmination it is lowest (Fig. 2).

For luminaries located near the stripe of the world, both culminations (both upper and lower) can be observed; For rising and setting luminaries, only the upper culmination is observed (the lower culmination occurs below the horizon).

The moment of the upper culmination of the Sun is called true full, and the moment of the lower culmination of the Sun is called true midnight.
