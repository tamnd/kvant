## Pushkin and exact sciences

⟦figure⟧

**"Poor Pushkin"**

Two years ago, while relaxing on a bench in a Leningrad public garden, I found myself an unwitting witness to the following conversation: “Poor Pushkin,” a young woman said to her neighbor, folding up the latest issue of “The Week.” “They left no living space on the man.” Now they are writing “Pushkin and Mineralogy” - about the precious stones in his life.”

... "Pushkin and the exact sciences." Will this title not cause a similar sigh of regret, will it seem too pretentious, paying to some extent a tribute to fashion? After all, we are not talking about the use of new mathematical methods in the study of the poet’s poems (a quite meaningful topic that is being developed by major mathematicians). We’ll just look at how some purely scientific terms are reflected on the pages of Pushkin’s works. The beginning of the last century was marked by remarkable discoveries in physics, chemistry, and mathematics. It is equally interesting if for a poet who strove to go “on par with the times” in everything, such references are rare or frequent.

**Dictionary of Pushkin's language**

Answering the question to what extent the exact sciences found their direct (and to some extent external) reflection in Pushkin’s works turns out to be not a difficult task. The fact is that on the occasion of the 150th anniversary of the poet’s birth, the Institute of Russian Literature (Pushkin House) published the “Dictionary of the Pushkin Language”. This is truly fundamental work! Each of the four volumes of the Dictionary contains 800 pages; here are collected all the words used by Pushkin in his poetic and prose works (including letters). 16,000 words, arranged in alphabetical order, are interpreted in the sense that was given to them in Pushkin’s time. The “frequency” of their use is indicated and a link is given to the volume and page of the Academic edition of the poet’s complete works (prepared and published for the 100th anniversary of the poet’s death, towards the end of the thirties), where the corresponding word appears.

So, having a “Dictionary” of Pushkin’s language, the easiest way to start is to write down words that are directly related to the exact sciences that Pushkin could use, and look for them in it. This is exactly what the author of this article did. And the number of “hits” turned out to be very small.

This situation is in accordance with the “traditional” image of Pushkin, who, according to the recollections of his sister Olga, cried as a child

he wrote: “Bad physics, but what a bold idea!”
Let us give an example to which Puskin’s remark is no less suitable.
In the famous poem “Portrait”, dedicated to the beautiful Zakrevskaya, he wrote:

With your flaming soul,
With your stormy passions
O wives of the North, between you
She appears sometimes
And past all the conditions of the world
Strives until he loses strength,
Like a lawless comet
In the calculated circle of luminaries.

It is obvious, however, that the movement of a comet is as well calculated (regular), as is the movement of the heavenly bodies: there is no greater “legislator” than nature. If it sometimes seems to us that the behavior of its objects is contrary to the laws, it simply means that we do not yet know other more complex laws that they obey!

Let us finally point out Pushkin’s well-known epigram, in which he reproduces a dispute between representatives of various philosophical schools of antiquity concerning the existence of the physical substantive concept of movement. This epigram, written in Mikhailovsky in the fall of 1825, is called “Movement”:

There is no movement, said the sage bradata.
The other fell silent and began to walk in front of him.
He could not have objected more strongly;
Everyone praised the answer to plan.
But, gentlemen, this is a funny incident
Another example comes to mind:
After all, every day the sun walks before us,
However, stubborn Galileo is right.

The “sage of bradata” is the ancient Greek philosopher Zeno of Elea (5th century BC), and his “other” opponent is Antisthenes (born around 440 BC). In eight lines of this epigram, the great pose with amazing capacity and expressiveness managed to convey the essence of the dispute; the final lines are intended to warn those who considered Antisthenes’s “answer to plan” exhaustive: Pushkin recalls the Galilean (Copernican) heliocentric system of the world, according to which the movement of the Sun is apparent, illustrating the relative nature of this movement.

To give an idea of ​​the depth with which Puskin scholars delve into the history of the emergence of Poz’s poems, into the explicit and hidden meaning of each of their lines, I note that an exhaustive analysis of Puskin’s “Movement” in one of the articles by Academician M.P. Alekseev contains 18 pages of dense text.

Concluding remarks

The listed examples are, of course, illustrative in nature and are only a “zero approximation” to the substantive topic “Pushkin and Science.” Pushkin’s deep connections with contemporary science are manifold. Schilling became famous not only for the invention of the “sales telegraph”; His inventions in maritime affairs and his work on electricity are known. The theme “Pushkin and Humboldt**” attracts attention and has also been developed.

However, the breadth of Pushkin’s interests and his understanding of the role of science in the life of society were most clearly manifested in his journalistic activities and were associated with the publication of the Sovremennik magazine - an enterprise on which he placed so much hope and into which he contributed so much effort in the last one and a half to two years of his life.

For Russian journalism 20-30 years. Over the past century, the publication of popular science articles has, one might say, a traditional character. Pushkin’s “Contemporary” was given a new look in this regard too. His publisher did not want to simply reprint popular science reviews from foreign magazines - he involved his correspondent, P. B. Kozlesky, a professional diplomat and a deeply educated person, in writing them. Kozlovsky’s articles were published in two of the four lifetime issues of the Russian Sovremennik. The first of them is “Analysis of the Paris Yearbook”, in modern terms, decisions for this publication, published under the supervision of the Secretary of the French Academy of Sciences, Francois Arago. In his detailed solution, Kozlovsky not only talks about the latest successes of French science, but also ardently advocates for the spread of education in his homeland, in Russia. The task is both noble and courageous, if we remember that in the eyes of the autocracy, enlightenment was synonymous with freedom. Kozlovsky’s next article, published in the 3rd volume of Sovremennik, was called “On Hope.” This was the first popular presentation of the foundations of probability theory in Russian literature. At that time we still did not have the last term, and Kozlovsky proposed calling this theory “convenience theories.” It is interesting to see on the pages of Sovremennik, next to the position of Pushkin, Zhukovsky, Tyuchev, algebraic formulas, Pascal’s triangle and even the graph that Kozlovsky completed his article.

His scientific connections with Russian scientists continued throughout his long life.
