Variants of the Rado problem

The most successful person on T. Rado's problem was another famous Hungarian mathematician, R. Rado. In 1950, 1951, 1968 he published three communities of statistics in English journals: “Several Covering Theorems”, I-III, entirely induced by T. Rado’s problem (so we had reason to publish an article “On Rado’s Problem” without indicating the incisal). We list only some results and unsolved issues related to this problem.

For the problem of a triangular “table” T and similar triangular “napkins” (with correspondingly parallel sides), we managed to obtain some estimates for $\varepsilon_0$; in particular, for covering T with congont (and parallel) triangles (an analogue of Sokolin’s result), one can specify the critical value $\varepsilon_0$ exactly: $\varepsilon_0 = \frac{1}{6}$. (Of course, in this problem we can always talk about a “table” shaped like a regular triangle.)

Neoctor estimates were also obtained for round “napkins” (of the same and different sizes), and for other types of similar “napkins”.

Exercise 7. Prove that if all “napkins” are similar to a given figure Φ (which they cover), then there exists $\varepsilon_0 > 0$ such that from any covering of a “table” of area 1 we can select a subsystem of non-overlapping “napkins” with a total area of ​​at least $\varepsilon_0$ ($\varepsilon_0$, of course, depends on the shape Φ).

Thus, here the critical value $\varepsilon_0$ is always positive, in contrast to the Branlov problem, in which $\varepsilon_0 = 0$ (where the “napkins” are rectangular, not necessarily similar to each other).

Other generalizations of the Rado problem were also considered, for example: what is the maximum total area that the total area of ​​two subsystems can have, in each of which the “napkins” do not overlap each other; what are the estimates for the spatial analogues of the Rado problem - but in all these directions only partial results and rather rough estimates have been obtained.
