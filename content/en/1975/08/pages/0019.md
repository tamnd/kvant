Finally, the universal solution itself arrived with the invention of the wireless telephone. Already in 1921, the New Holland radio station in Petrograd began transmitting so-called rhythmic time signals several times a day, consisting of 61 points per minute, so the intervals between points were less than a second by $1/60$ seconds. By noticing the coincidence of the seconds of the chronometer strikes with the points of the radio signals, it was possible to check your chronometer with universal, i.e., Greenwich, time with an accuracy of hundredths of a second. True, as a result of errors in the observatory clock and various technical interference, these signals were errors for a split second, but this could be taken into account later, after they were received at the observatory and subsequent astronomical observations. For this purpose, special biological data on the exact moment of broadcasting of past signals were published monthly, which makes it possible to retroactively correct the chronometer readings. This was, of course, necessary only when determining longitude with extreme accuracy; in navigation, where accuracy up to 1 second is more than enough, the published corrections of the transmitted time signals had no meaning. Now, thanks to the invention of quartz and then molecular clocks *), which store time to thousandths of a second for many months, the correction of transmitted radio signals is practically zero. The old problem of determining geographic longitude is obtaining the most accurate solution.

Our article is legitimate with such an example. Imagine a person familiar with astronomical methods for determining his location. Let him be taken to any place - to a deserted island, to the desert, to the mountains, anywhere, and he doesn’t know at all where he is, in what country, even in what hemisphere. Let him lose track of time and know neither the place nor the date. But give him an astronomical diary with tables of logarithms, a universal instrument, a chronometer showing arbitrary time, and a radio receiver for time signals (without the right to receive other broadcasts). Allow him to observe the stars one clear night and the Sun the next morning. Having processed the assignments, he will tell the place, and on a geographical map he will indicate where he is and in which direction he needs to go to get to the nearest or specified city. In a word, it is impossible for such a person to forget himself on the globe.

The action of the molecular clock is associated with the natural vibrations of the molecules of certain substances.
