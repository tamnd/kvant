Therefore, all other sets can be covered by squares with a total area of ​​$\left(\frac{4}{9}\right)^n$. But $\lim_{n \to \infty} \left(\frac{4}{9}\right)^n = 0$, which means that for a sufficiently large $n$ the total area of ​​the covering squares is arbitrarily small, and therefore the measure of the entire remaining set is zero.

The fact that the set described above has zero Jordan measure is not surprising, because it is entirely a product of discarded crosses and has no “living space”. The surprising thing is that other tables with holes in the sets turned out to be impossible to change according to Jordan. Such a set can be constructed by throwing out crosses more economically, namely choosing the first cross with area $1/4$, then four crosses with a total area $1/8$, etc. The remaining squares have successive total areas of $3/4$, $5/8$, $9/16$, etc. All these numbers are greater than $1/2$, and therefore any polygon containing the set remaining after an infinite number of discards has an area no less than $1/2$. At the same time, this set does not contain a single whole square, and therefore its area cannot be greater than zero. The resulting contradiction shows that this set does not have Jordan measure. Thus, certain Jordanian measures do not cover many interesting figures.

Of course, such an answer turns out to be an unprivileged weight - there was not a single whole part in the set, and its area was positive. But Borel went even further, changing different sets using two principles:

a) If the set $\Phi$ consists of a countable *) collection of mutable subsets $\{\Phi_n\}$, no two of which have a common point, then

$$m \left( \bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} \Phi_n \right) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} m(\Phi_n).$$

b) The measure of the complement to a variable set $\Phi$ is obtained by subtracting the measure of the subset from the measure of the integer $U$,

$$m(\Phi') = m(U) - m(\Phi).$$

The first of these principles is a far-reaching generalization of the ancient position: the measure of the whole is equal to the sum of the measures of its part; the second follows from the same position. Having obtained his principles, Borel found the measures of all the sets with which mathematicians of his time dealt. For example, from his principles it followed that any countable set of points has zero measure: after all, each of the points has zero measure, and then the union of these points has zero measure.

But Borel's constructions had one drawback - the same flat set $\Phi$ can be constructed from polygons in different ways. And it does not follow that this will yield the same result for the measure. Certain measures had to be given, regardless of the choice of one or another method of constructing the set.
