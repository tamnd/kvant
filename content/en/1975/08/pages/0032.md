⟦folio 30⟧

“A blind blind man,” “Experience is the son of difficult mistakes,” and, finally, “Genius is a friend of paradoxes.” Academician S.I. Vavilov specifically noted the depth of these Pushkin definitions.

Let us point out for the sake of the canvas that for the third time “algebra” is also mentioned in the critical article. Pozt compares the Tatar with the Moorish (in Spain) and writes: “The Tatars were not like the Moors. Having conquered Russia, they did not give it algebra or Aristotle.”

In a critical article related to the work of Küchelbecker, Pushkin writes: “Inspiration is needed in position, as in geometry.” Significant phrase! Professor David Hilbert of the University of Göttingen, one of the greatest mathematicians of our century, once said that his student left mathematics, deciding to commit himself to philosophy. “This doesn’t surprise me,” Gilbert replied, “he felt a lack of imagination!” The inspiration that Pushkin writes about is nothing more akin to Hilbert’s fantasy.

Pushkin's judgment on geometry was not accidental; at about the same time, but on a different occasion, he writes in his notebook: “Inspiration is needed in geometry, as in pose,” swapping position and geometry and, as if maintaining their equality in the face of inspiration. Academician M.P. Alekseev notes that such a comparison is not accidental. In 1826, when these phrases were written down by Pushkin, N. I. Lobachessky was already talking about his new geometry, and the echoes of his speech at Kazan University, delivered on February 24, 1826, could have reached Pushkin.

It is always tempting to trace whether the life destinies of two geniuses who lived at the same time, although they manifested themselves in different areas of human culture, intersected. According to M.P. Alekseev, there is reason to believe that the names Pushkin and Lobachevsky can be combined with respect to mechanical ones. First of all, I. Velikoopolsky, the brother of Lobachevsky’s wife, knew Pushkin well, saw him several times, corresponded with him and even exchanged poems (very caustic ones). There is direct evidence of how Lobachesny loved Pushkin’s poetry: he often recited his poems by heart in the family circle. During a trip to Orenburg at the very beginning of the 30s (without material for the “History of the Pugachev Rebellion”), Pushkin remained in Kazan, the city where Lobachevsky lived all his life. Here Pushkin spent several days in the house of Professor Fuchs, Lobachessky’s colleague at the university. Fuchs and his social wife had many guests every evening; It is not clear that among them in the “Pushkin days” were Lobachevsky. True, documentary evidence could not be found. But I believe that it was precisely these Japanese words that led L. B. Modzalevsky—Pushkinism and the son of Pushkinism—to study the biography of Lobachevsky. As a result, Modzalewski became the author of one of the most informative books about the great Russian mathematician.

Pushkin used the words “physics” and “chemistry” once each. “Chemistry” is in an uninteresting context, but “physics” is very resonant.

In November 1824, he wrote a cycle of poems, “Imitations of the Koran,” a kind of poetic retelling of the ancient book. Pushkin made notes on some lines. So, to the stanza

The earth is motionless—the vaults of the sky,
Creator, held by you,
May they not fall on dry land and water
And they won’t suppress us.—
