It is clear that the smaller $\varepsilon$, the more difficult it is to construct an example. It can happen that for $\varepsilon$, greater than some $\varepsilon_0$, our Statement is true, but for smaller $\varepsilon$ it is no longer true. Let us prove that for the one-dimensional version of our problem this is exactly the case.

At $\varepsilon = 1/3$ The statement is false.

In other words, no napkin system can serve as the required example for $\varepsilon = 1/3$, i.e., any napkin system contains a subsystem of non-overlapping napkins covering a part of the “table” of length $1/3$ (or more than $1/3$).

Let us be given some system of segments $K$ covering a segment o of length 1. Let us choose from $K$ a larger segment and denote it $i_1$. Let us now remove from the system $K$ all segments of the covering that intersect with the displayed segment $i_1$ (they cover a segment of length no more than $3|i_1|$, where $|i|$ is the length of the segment $i$). Of the remaining coverage segments, we again select the largest one (we denote it $i_2$) and discard all those segments that intersect with $i_2$ (they cover a segment of length no more than $3|i_2|$), and so on until we have exhausted all the coverage segments. The chosen subsystem of non-intersecting segments $i_1, i_2, \dots$ is such that

$$3|i_1| + 3|i_2| + \dots \geq 1,$$

since the lengths $3|i_1|, 3|i_2|, \dots$ in total cover only $K$ and, therefore, the entire segment $o$ of length 1. Therefore, the sum of the length $|i_1| + |i_2| + \dots$ is not less than $1/3$.
