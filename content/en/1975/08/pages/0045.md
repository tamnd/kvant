Thus, in the Rado problem (unlike the Brailov problem) there also exists some critical value $\varepsilon_0$ such that

at $\varepsilon > \varepsilon_0$ true

Statement 1. It is possible to indicate an example of a “napkin” system such that any subsystem of non-overlapping “napkins” has a total area less than $\varepsilon$,

and for $\varepsilon < \varepsilon_0$ it is true

Statement 2. From any system of “napkins” one can choose a subsystem of non-overlapping “napkins” with a total area of ​​at least $\varepsilon$.

Of course, we always consider the area of ​​the “table” to be equal to 1.

T. Happy to prove that for the critical value $\varepsilon_0$ the following estimates are true:

$$\frac{1}{9} \leq \varepsilon_0 \leq \frac{1}{4},$$

(1)

but I was sure that in fact it was $\varepsilon_0 = 1/4$.

He proposed to prove this equality in a letter to Waclaw Serpiinski.

However, neither Sierpiinski nor other mathematicians succeeded in doing this, although many took on Rado’s problem. And only 55 years later, in 1973, it became clear why. It turns out that the T. Rado hypothesis is simply wrong!

A sensational note by the Hungarian mathematician M. Ajtai appeared in the “User Bulletin of the Academy of Sciences”, in which it was quite simply shown that with squares of only two different sizes it is possible to cover a rectangle of area 1 in such a way that it is impossible to choose from them a non-overlapping subsystem of area $1/4$ or larger. It is now known that

$$\frac{1}{8,6} < \varepsilon_0 < \frac{1}{4};$$

(2)

the left inequality was the result of successive considerations of inequality (1) by T. Rado, the last of which belongs to the Leningrad geometer V. A. Zalgaler * (apparently, it is also not final, being closer to the “rough” result of Theorem 2 than to the hypothetical estimate of T. Rado).

A special case of the Rado problem is solved much more beautifully and simply - when all the “napkins” are squares of the same size. In this case, the critical value $\varepsilon_0$ turns out to be actually equal to $1/4$. This result was proven back in 1940 by the young Leningrad mathematician A.S. Sokolin (and later, independently of him, by many others, in several different ways).

**Blichfeld's theorem and Sokolin's result**

Let us first prove such a pleasant theorem, one of the variants of which was devoted to the second cover page of «Kvant», 1973, No. 10.

Blichfeld's theorem. If the area of ​​the fidurra located on a sheet of dumpling paper is greater than $n-1$ (e.g. $n$ is an integer; the side of the dumpling is taken as 1), then this fidurma can be folded by parallel transfer into such a position that it covers at least $n$ nodes of the seed. (Nodes are called nodes where lines intersect, dividing the plane into square glues.)

Proof. Let's cut a sheet of dumpling paper into separate cells and collect together, in a stack, all those cells on which at least part of the fidurra fits (from figure 4, and let's move on to figure 4, 6). Since the sum of the areas of the pieces of our fidurra is greater than $n-1$, then some points in the stack will be covered by at least $n$ pieces
