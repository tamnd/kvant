Henri Lebesgue
(on the centenary of his birth)

Henri Lebesgue was born years ago, on June 28, 1875, in the small French town of Beauvais. After successfully graduating from school, Lebesgue entered the Paris Ecole Normale Supérieure, where he attended lectures by J. Tannery, C. Jordan and other outstanding mathematicians. After graduating, he worked as a mathematics teacher at the Lyceum in Nancy, continuing the scientific work begun in Paris. Having completed his doctoral dissertation in 1902, Lebesgue moved to the department of mathematics at the provincial university in Rennes, from where he moved to Poitiers, and only in 1910 he returned to Paris, where he became a professor at the Sorbonne, and from 1921 at the Collec de France.

For his scientific merits, Lebesgue was elected a member of the French Academy of Sciences in 1922, an honorary member of the London Mathematical Society in 1924, and a member of the Royal Society (English Academy of Sciences) in 1930. He received academic prizes four times, wrote several books and many scientific papers. After a serious illness, Lebesgue died on July 26, 1941.

If you randomly open any page of any book on the theory of functions of a real (or, as they also say, real) variable, on functional analysis or on the theory of probability, you will almost certainly come across the words “Lebet measure”, “Lebet point”, “Lebet integral”, or the concepts introduced by him “summable function”, “almost everywhere”, and sometimes Lebet’s name turns out to be an encrypted symbol $L^p$: after all $L$ is the first letter of his surname Lebesque, and $L^p$ is the space of functions whose $p$th power is Lebes summable. Almost all the concepts related to modern measure and integral theory go back to the work of Lebet, and the introduction of these concepts in some sense turned out to be a turning point in the transition from 19th-century mathematics to 20th-century science.

In the development of science there are long periods, reminiscent of the rapid flow of a large river. During these periods, based on a ready-made stock of concepts and ideas, scientists obtain new scientific results and expand the scope of application of already known methods. But little by little, scientific problems are accumulating, the solutions of which are impossible within the framework of existing scientific ideas.
