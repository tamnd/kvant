## Pushkin and exact sciences

⟦figure⟧

**"Poor Pushkin"**

Two years ago, while relaxing on a bench in a Leningrad public garden, I found myself an unwitting witness to the following conversation: “Poor Pushkin,” a young woman said to her neighbor, folding up the latest issue of “The Week.” “They left no living space on the man.” Now they are writing “Pushkin and Mineralogy” - about the precious stones in his life.”

... "Pushkin and the exact sciences." Will this title not cause a similar sigh of regret, will it seem too pretentious, paying to some extent a tribute to fashion? After all, we are not talking about the use of new mathematical methods in the study of the poet’s poems (a quite meaningful topic that is being developed by major mathematicians). We’ll just look at how some purely scientific terms are reflected on the pages of Pushkin’s works. The beginning of the last century was marked by remarkable discoveries in physics, chemistry, and mathematics. It is equally interesting if for a poet who strove to go “on par with the times” in everything, such references are rare or frequent.

**Dictionary of Pushkin's language**

Answering the question to what extent the exact sciences found their direct (and to some extent external) reflection in Pushkin’s works turns out to be not a difficult task. The fact is that on the occasion of the 150th anniversary of the poet’s birth, the Institute of Russian Literature (Pushkin House) published the “Dictionary of the Pushkin Language”. This is truly fundamental work! Each of the four volumes of the Dictionary contains 800 pages; here are collected all the words used by Pushkin in his poetic and prose works (including letters). 16,000 words, arranged in alphabetical order, are interpreted in the sense that was given to them in Pushkin’s time. The “frequency” of their use is indicated and a link is given to the volume and page of the Academic edition of the poet’s complete works (prepared and published for the 100th anniversary of the poet’s death, towards the end of the thirties), where the corresponding word appears.

So, having a “Dictionary” of Pushkin’s language, the easiest way to start is to write down words that are directly related to the exact sciences that Pushkin could use, and look for them in it. This is exactly what the author of this article did. And the number of “hits” turned out to be very small.
