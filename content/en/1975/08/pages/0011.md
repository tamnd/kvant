In the same way, in order to improve the value of the integral from the calculation of a discontinuous function, Lebesgue proposed to make not the domain of change of the argument, but the range of values ​​of the function. If the numbers $y_k$ and $y_{k+1}$ are given, then by $E_k$ we denote the set of those values ​​$x$ for which the inequality $y_k \leq f(x) < y_{k+1}$ holds. The Lebesgue integral sum is the sum $\sum_{k} y_k m(E_k)$.

Going to the preposition with an unlimited change in the segments of the partition, we obtain a number that is equal to the Lebesgue integral $\int_a^b f(x) \, dx$. Visually speaking, the essence of Lebesgue’s ideas is that for discontinuous functions, the “curvilinear trapezoid” had to be cut not parallel to the ordinat axis, but parallel to the abscissa axis.

Nowadays, the Lebesgue process of calculating the integral seems to be something that mathematicians take for granted. But at the beginning of the century he seemed to shake the very foundations of mathematics. And then Lebesgue came up with a new justification for the fact that his method of calculating integrals was natural. Perhaps he was influenced by the example of Hilbert, who gave the axiomat of geometry a little earlier. Similarly, Lebesgue formulated a system of requirements that an integral must satisfy, and proved that the only definition of an integral that satisfies all these requirements is the one he proposed.

In 1902, thanks to the support of C. Jordan and E. Picard, it was possible to overcome the prejudice of opponents of the new mathematical scale, and Lebesgue successfully completed his doctoral dissertation.

The creation of the Lebesgue measure and integral made it possible to overcome many difficulties that had accumulated by that time in mathematical analysis. Lebesgue’s idea turned out to be especially useful: it is not necessary to require that some property of a function be satisfied for all values ​​of the argument, but it is enough to study properties that are satisfied almost everywhere, that is, everywhere except for the set of points of zero measure. It turned out, for example, that if the function $y = f(x)$ is bounded and interpretable according to Lebesgue, then the function $F(x) = \int_a^x f(t) \, dt$ for almost all values ​​of $x$ is arbitrary, coinciding with $f(x)$. The Lebesgue integral is extremely useful in the theory of trigonometric series, that is, series of the form

$$\frac{a_n}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} (a_n \cos nx + b_n \sin nx).$$

Lebesgue himself occupied himself with this theory a lot and with enthusiasm.

At first glance, it seemed that the Lebesgue integral was too complex and subtle a tool to be used in applications; in practice, “wild” functions and sets also do not occur, and all practical problems of servicing by general integers. But in those years, the theory of infinite-dimensional spaces began to be developed, necessary for the study and solution of integral equations (equations containing the desired function under the integral sign). Such equations have many properties that concern mathematical physics, and methods for solving them were invented, based on constructing a sequence of functions that, in a certain sense, approaches the desired solution. It was not possible to prove, while remaining within the framework of classical mathematics, that such a sequence is somehow approaching. The state of affairs here does not resemble an attempt to solve the equation $x^2 = 2$, trying only with rational numbers. Of course, it is possible to construct a sequence of rational numbers that approaches the desired root, but the root itself is integral, and the sequence has no limit within the set of rational numbers.
