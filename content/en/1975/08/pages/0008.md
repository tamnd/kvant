Schwartz proved that in this way it is impossible to find the area of ​​the most ordinary cylinder - a polyhedron with arbitrarily small faces and having an arbitrarily large area can be inscribed into it. Such a polyhedron consists entirely of small folds.

Lebesgue managed, after much thought, to come up with a definition of surface area that did not require drawing tangent planes, but at the same time avoided all the difficulties associated with Schwartz's example. Solving this particular problem, Lebesgue came to general ideas about what the measure of a set is, how to measure the lengths, areas and volumes of the most bizarre figures. Many mathematicians before him were interested in this question. Jordan's definition turned out to be the most successful.

Jordan's main idea was as follows. There are figures that obviously have an area, for example, polygons. Now suppose that the figure $\Phi$ has the following property: *whatever small positive number $\varepsilon$ we take,*

*there are two polygonal areas\*) $\Phi_\varepsilon$ and $\Phi_\varepsilon^*$, such that $\Phi_\varepsilon$ is entirely contained in the figure $\Phi$, and $\Phi_\varepsilon^*$ contains it entirely, and the difference between the areas $\Phi_\varepsilon^*$ and $\Phi_\varepsilon$ is less than $\varepsilon$.* Then the area of the figure $\Phi$ must be taken the number that is enclosed between the areas $\Phi_\varepsilon$ and $\Phi_\varepsilon^*$ for all $\Phi_\varepsilon$ and $\Phi_\varepsilon^*$. In another way, the same definition can be formulated as follows: a figure $\Phi$ is *Jordan measurable* if for any $\varepsilon > 0$ it can be represented as the union of the polygonal region $\Phi_\varepsilon$ and the remainder, which is covered by a polygonal region having an area smaller than $\varepsilon$. The measure $m\,(\Phi)$ of such a figure is contained between numbers of the form $m\,(\Phi_\varepsilon)$ and numbers of the form $m\,(\Phi_\varepsilon) + \varepsilon$, where $m\,(\Phi_\varepsilon)$ is the area of ​​$\Phi_\varepsilon$.

This definition corresponds to the usual process of measuring the areas of figures. We resort to it in school when measuring the area of ​​a circle - regular inscribed polygons act as internal polygons, and the remainder consists of segments of the circle cut off by the sides of this polygon. These segments can be enclosed in triangles formed by chords and tangents (Fig. 3) and it can be shown that with a sufficiently large number of sides of the polygon, the total area of ​​these triangles is arbitrarily small.

The Jordan measure has many good properties, in particular, as it should be, the union of a finite number of pairwise disjoint Jordan measurable sets is measurable, and its measure is equal to the sum of the measures of the parts.
