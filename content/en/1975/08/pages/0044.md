⟦folio 42⟧

Having solved Exercises 1 and 2, you should know the following fact:

Theorem 1. Let the “table” and “napkins” be segments, the length of the “table” is equal to 1.

a) From any system of “napkins” covering a “table”, one can select a subsystem of non-overlapping “napkins” with a total length of at least $1/2$.

b) If $\varepsilon > 1/2$, then it is always possible to specify a system of “napkins”, implying that any of its subsystems of non-overlapping “napkins” has a length less than $\varepsilon$.

In other words, the Statement is true for $\varepsilon > 1/2$ and false for $\varepsilon \leq 1/2$.

Exercise 4. Prove a similar theorem for the “table” - a circle of length 1, and the “napkins” - a friend of this circle.

Square napkins

In 1928, the famous Hungarian mathematician Tibor Rado (T. Rado) reported in a letter to the editor of the leading user of the mathematical journal Fundamenta Mathematicae, where in the user of the mathematical school Vashlav Serginsky, that he could not solve an apparently simple geometric problem related to the theory of functions of several variables. Having presented the reports and results known to us about the purchase of a segment by segments, T. Rado raises the question of obtaining analogous estimates for a “two-dimensional” problem in which the “napkins” are squares (of different sizes), and the “table” is, say, a square (or rectangle) of area 1, and the square is parallel to the edges of the table (Fig. 3).

So, the difference between the Rado problem and the M321 (Brailov) problem is only in the form of “napkins”: in M321 they can be any rectangles, but in Rado they can only be squares. But this “small” difference, as sometimes happens, completely changes the matter.

The method by which we extracted a subsystem of length at least $1/3$ from a system of segments covering a segment of length 1 also works in the two-dimensional case. With his help, T. Rado proved this fact.
