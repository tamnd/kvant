And only the emergence of completely new ideas leads science out of the current dead end.

The second half of the 19th century was such a turning point in the history of the development of mathematics. For almost two hundred years, mathematical analysis developed the ideas of Newton and Leibniz, who created the calculus of infinitesimals and the mathematics of variables. Of course, new concepts were introduced that were unknown to the founders of analysis, but these concepts naturally followed from existing ideas. And if by some miracle Newton or Leibniz had gotten into the hands of a mathematical journal from the mid-19th century, they would have quickly understood the formulation of most problems and methods for solving them. Further designations have not changed much over the years. And most importantly, the basic principle remained unchanged: the value of a scientific result depends on how successful the new method turns out to be in solving practical problems.

But the characteristic feature of black mathematics is that, despite the creation of new methods for solving practical problems, it is interested in the instrument itself created, for each emerging concept it searches for the widest and most natural area of ​​its applicability, for each proven theorem - the most general conditions under which it is valid. And this is not empty exercise in mathematical methods, but a necessity. Only by establishing concepts and theorems in the most general way, freeing them from unnecessary restrictions associated with the specific task from which they arose, can one see the connections between the distant sweets of science and learn to apply the created methods in situations that, at first glance, have nothing promised with the question where they arose.

For two centuries, mathematicians used the concepts of number, geometric figure, function, length, area, volume, etc. without much thought. It was said that when a French general brought his “solution” to the problem of squaring a circle to the Academy of Sciences, he was asked what exactly he understood by the area of ​​a circle. “Areas are not determined, they are calculated!” exclaimed the gallant general. And this point of view was common among most mathematicians. They believed that area is a property of a geometric figure, expressed by a number and having very prominent properties (the area of ​​the whole is equal to the sum of the areas of the parts, congested figures have equal areas, etc.). They did not doubt for one minute that any flat geometric figure has an area (perhaps equal to zero or infinity). However, on other issues, the views of mathematicians of the 17th-18th centuries were distinguished by the same uncriticality - they were convinced that it is possible to draw a tangent to all lines at any point, with the possible exception of a few special points, that all surfaces are smooth, and the function is set aside with the expression by which it is given. But such vagueness of basic definitions began to lead to erroneous results, and already from the beginning of the 19th century, a process of clarifying mathematical concepts began, which lasted for several decades.
