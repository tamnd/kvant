However, Lebesgue considered his main achievement not to be the creation of a new concept of measure, but the application of this concept to a new definition of the integral. If the function $y = f(x)$ is not discontinuous and non-negative on the segment $[a, b]$, then $\int_a^b f(x)dx$ is nothing more than the area of ​​a curved trapezoid (Fig. 4). Therefore, if you know how to find the area of ​​this trapezoid, you can find the value of the integral. But if a function has an infinite number of discontinuity points, for example, a discontinuity at each point, then the corresponding curved trapezoid is not a figure in the classical sense of the word and may not have a total area. Calculating this area using the Lebesgue method, we obtain the value of the integral of such a function.

The definition of the integral as a measure of a curved trapezoid did not seem very convincing to Lebesgue himself - since the time of Cauchy, i.e., since the beginning of the 19th century, mathematicians have been accustomed to defining the integral purely analytically, without resorting to concepts such as area or volume. Lebesgue came up with an analytical definition for his integral. Explaining this definition, he used the following image: if there is a certain amount of money, then an inexperienced person will list it in a row, taking coins and bank notes of various states one after another. An experienced cashier will sort tens into tens, fives into fives, rubles into rubles, etc., and then multiply the number of tens by 10, the number of fives by 5, etc. and add up all the resulting products.
