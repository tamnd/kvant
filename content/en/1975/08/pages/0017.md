These were, for example, the best eclipses and eclipses of the satellites of Jupter (the use of the latter to determine longitude was pointed out by Galileo, who discovered the satellites of Jupter in 1610). Unfortunately, these phenomena do not occur instantly, but last for several minutes, so they can only be recorded with a corresponding error. Meanwhile, one minute of time corresponds to 28 km below the equator, so determining longitude in this way was associated with a possible error of hundreds of kilometers. In addition, there was the difficulty that eclipses of Jupter’s satellites are almost impossible to observe with the fingers of a rocking ship and these phenomena do not occur every day, and for several months of the year Jupter is not visible at all. Eclipses of the Moon are completely rare phenomena and occur no more than twice a year. Therefore, when traveling, it was necessary to wait several days or even weeks until it was possible to make several (to control and improve accuracy) such observations, and at sea for observation purposes this method of determining longitude was not at all suitable.

In the 16th century, another way was found to determine the local time of the initial meridin - using the so-called lunar distances, rules; its practical application became possible only after the invention of the sextag. The Moon circles the Earth every day, like a clock hand moving across the “dial” - the starry sky. But this hand moves through the clock slowly, 55 times slower than the clock hand of an ordinary clock. It is clear that by the movement of the clock hand the actual time can be determined, but very inaccurately due to the slowness of its movement. Obviously, determining time by the movement of the Moon is even less reliable, but the accuracy and subtlety of the divisions of the “dial”—the precisely known positions of the stars—helps here. True, the position of the Moon relative to these stars must be changed with very great accuracy, well at an angle of about 30°, so changing the lunar distance from the star with an accuracy of 1′ allows you to determine the time with an accuracy of two minutes. In order to use this method, you need to know very well all the features of the movement of the Moon, so that by observing the position of the Moon you can obtain the time of the initial meridian, the difference of which with the local time of this point will give the longitude of the latter. Obviously, this method imposes very high demands on celestial mechanics, which develops theories of the motion of the Moon that are more complex. The lunar distance method was not much superior in accuracy to the previous one, but it could be used at any time when the Moon and stars were visible, which is why it became widespread during travel, especially sea travel.

However, the method of lunar distances did not fully satisfy the sailors (mainly due to the complexity of the calculation and low accuracy). Sailing ships, depending on the strength and direction of the wind, often lasted in places out of sight of the shores, so it was very important to be able to determine the position of the ship (its latitude and longitude). In 1713, the English government, being especially interested in the safety of navigation (at that time England was the country with the most developed navy), awarded a huge prize of 20,000 feet sterling for the development of a reliable method for determining longitude with an accuracy of $1/2$ degrees.
