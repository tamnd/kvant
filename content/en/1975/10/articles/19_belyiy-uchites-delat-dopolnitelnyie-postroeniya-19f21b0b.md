Learn to make additional constructions
S. B. Belie

Almost every geometry problem can be solved in several ways. It is always interesting to try to find the simplest, “beautiful” solution - especially since such solutions are highly rated by examiners. Additional constructions often help with this. In one case, these constructions are directed by themselves, in others the dreams are not so obvious and require a lot of experience, ingenuity, and geometric intuition from the decider.

Let's move on to consider specific tasks.

Problem 1. On the sides AC, BC of a right triangle, outside its construction are the squares ACDE and BCKF. From points E and F, the redistributions EM and FN are dropped onto the extension of the hypotenuse. Prove that EM + FN = AB.

In the right triangle ACB we draw the height CL (see Fig. 1).

Obviously, ΔACL = ΔEAM, ΔBCL = ΔFBN. Therefore EM = AL, FN = LB, EM + FN = AL + LB = AB.

Problem 2. (MIPT, 1970). The height of an equilateral boundary is greatest if its diagonals are mutually redistributed and the area of ​​the boundary is equal to S.

Let us draw a line through vertex C parallel to the diagonal BD until it intersects with the continuation of the base of the trapezoid AD at point E (see Fig. 2). Since the trapezoid is equilateral, and its diagonals are mutually redistributed; then triangle ACE is equilateral and right-angled (prove it!). In triangle ACE we draw the height CF and denote its length by h. Clearly AF = CF, but AF is equal to the midline of the trapezoid, so S = h². Therefore, h = √S.

Problem 3. On sides AB and BC of triangle ABC, squares ABDE and BCKF are constructed outside it. Prove that the segment DF is twice the median BP of the triangle.

We extend triangle ABC to a parallelogram, and extend the median BP to its diagonal (see Fig. 3). Let $ABC = \alpha$, then $DBF = 180^\circ - \alpha$. But also $BCQ = 180^\circ - \alpha$, and the sides enclosing these angles are also equal to each other, therefore $\Delta DBF = \Delta QCB$. Therefore, $BQ = 2BP = DF$.

Problem 4. Inside the triangle, find a point such that if you connect line segments with the vertices of the triangle, then the triangle will be divided into three parts, the area of ​​which is in the ratio $m:n:q$.

We divide the base AC of triangle ABC by points D and E into three parts in this ratio $m:n:q$ (Fig. 4). Through point D we draw a line parallel to side AB, and through point E a line parallel to BC. Let us prove that the point O of the intersection of these lines is the desired one. To prove the connection of vertex B with points D and E. $S_{\Delta AOB} = S_{\Delta ABD}$, since these triangles have a common base AB and equal heights. Similar to $S_{\Delta BOC} = S_{\Delta BEC}$, and the next one to $S_{\Delta AOC} = S_{\Delta DBE}$. But the areas of triangles ABD, DBE, EBC are in the ratio $m:n:q$, since they all have the same height. Therefore, $S_{\Delta AOB} : S_{\Delta BOC} = m:n:q$.

Problem 5. Through the intersection points of the medians of triangle ABC, a line l is drawn intersecting the sides AB and AC. Prove that the sum of the distances from line l to vertices B and C is equal to the distance from it to vertex A.

Let O be the point of intersection of the medians of the triangle, AD the median (Fig. 5). Let us drop the perpendiculars BM, DL, CN, AK onto the line l. It is easy to see that DL is the midline of the trapezoid BMNC, $DL = \frac{BM + CN}{2}$, and $\Delta AKO \sim \Delta DLO$, so $\frac{DL}{AK} = \frac{DO}{OA} = \frac{1}{2}$ and $AK = 2DL = BM + CN$.

Problem 6. (MSU, Mechanics and Mathematics, 1973). *In a trapezoid, the diagonals are 3 and 5, and the segment connecting the midpoints of the bases is 2. Find the area of ​​the trapezoid.*

First of all, note that the points $E$ and $F$—the midpoints of the bases $BC$ and $AD$ of the trapezoid $ABCD$ (see Fig. 6)—and the point $O$ of the intersection of the diagonals lie on the same straight line (prove it!). Through point $C$ we draw two straight lines: one parallel to the diagonal $BD$ until it intersects with the continuation of the base $AD$ at point $L$, the other parallel to $EF$. Since $OF$ is the median of triangle $AOD$, then $CK$ is the median of triangle $ACL$. Now we will complete the triangle $ACL$ to a parallelogram, and the median $CK$ to its diagonal. $S_{\square ABCD} = S_{\Delta ACL} = S_{\Delta ACQ}$ (why?). It remains to note that $\Delta ACQ$ is rectangular, $S_{\Delta ACQ} = 6$.

Problem 8 (MIPT, 1973). *Two circles of radii $R$ and $r$ $(R > r)$ have an external tangency at point $A$. Through the point $B$, taken on the larger circle, a straight line is drawn, touching the smaller circle at the point $C$. Find the length of the segment $BC$ if the chord $AB$ is equal to $a$.*

Through the center $O$ of the smaller circle and the point $B$ we draw a straight line (see Fig. 8). Then $BC^2 = OB^2 - r^2$. By the cosine theorem

$$OB^2 = (R + r)^2 + R^2 -$$
$$-2R\,(R + r)\cos\sphericalangle AO_1B,$$

where

$$\cos\sphericalangle AO_1B = \frac{2R^2 - a^2}{2R^2}.$$

Hence,
$$BC^2 = (R+r)^2 + R^2 - 2R\,(R+r)\times$$
$$\times\ \frac{2R^2 - a^2}{2R^2} - r^2 = a^2\left(\frac{R+r}{R}\right),$$

$$BC = a\sqrt{1 + \frac{r}{R}}.$$

Let's draw some conclusions. When starting to solve a geometric problem, you need to keep in mind that a general geometric problem can be solved in several ways. Therefore, if you have an idea for solving a problem, but you see that the path to the solution is quite long, then try to find a different approach to the solution. It should be remembered that additional constructions can provide significant assistance.
