ONE of the most significant cultural centers of Central Asia was the Ograrsky district, located in the Syr-Dary river basin. The main city of this region, Ograr, or Farab, as the Arab conquerors called it, is the birthplace of several outstanding representatives of science and culture throughout the medieval East. The largest of them was the great thinker, encyclopedist, and versatile mathematician Abu Nasr Muhammad al-Farab, born in 870. He was educated first at his home in Ograr and then in Bardad. During his lifetime, al-Farab received the work “al-muallim assaniya” - the second teacher. (The first teacher of “al-muallim al-awzal” was the great thinker of antiquity, Aristotle.)

Al-Farab died in Damsk in 950.

Al-Farab owns about fifty works on various branches of knowledge. Almost all of them are written in Arabic, the language of science of that time. In his scientific activity, physico-mathematical disciplines occupy more of a place. According to historians, al-Farab wrote about 70 works on geometry, astronomy and music alone, most of which have been lost.

Currently, a number of al-Farab treatises have been studied. These works were published in Russian translation with detailed comments *). Al-Farab used arithmetic, geometry, and trigonometry in works on music theory, astronomy, and mathematical geography. Let us briefly dwell on some of al-Farab's achievements in the field of mathematics.

Al-Farab in “Enumeration of Sciences” tries to define in his own way the sections, subject and content of all branches of mathematics of his time (arithmetic, geometry, optics, mathematical astronomy). He was one of the first in the history of mathematics to define the subject of a new branch of mathematics - algebra. “This science,” writes the scientist, “is common to both arithmetic and geometry. It contains various ingenious methods for finding numbers...” He is one of the first to install

Issues of the established tradition al-Farabi includes applied mathematics, as he calls it, “the science of skillful techniques,” among the main mathematical branches.

Much credit goes to al-Farabi in the development of geometry, especially constructive geometry. In the practice of geometric constructions, for the first time in the history of mathematics, he sets out in a systematic form methods for solving numerous problems of constructive geometry, especially important problems for constructing a constructed distance using a compass.

Let us present one of the two constructions of parabolas by points, apparently first proposed by al-Farabi. Al-Farabi's research on trigonometry is of great interest for the history of mathematics. The most important is his introduction of tangent and cotangent lines in the trigonometric circle: these functions were defined only as shadows of the gnomon (pole). He defines tangent and cotangent as tangent segments to a circle. Al-Farabi first formulates the sine theorem for an arbitrary triangle.

According to al-Farabi, “a sine is a chord of a double arc.” He adds new relations to the previously known correspondences between trigonometric functions, including

$$tg\alpha = \frac{1}{\text{ctg}\alpha}.$$

In the “Book of Applications to Alma-Geost” he was one of the first to prove the theorem of sines for a right spherical triangle. Al-Farabi widely uses trigonometry to solve various problems of mathematical astronomy.

In al-Farabi we come across interesting statements about probabilistic concepts. In his Treatise on Right and Wrong in the Nature of the Stars, he assesses the possibility of more and less probable

Al-Farabi calls for investigating phenomena that are “possible in most cases,” believing that “experience is useful in relation to phenomena that are possible in most cases.”

In the “Big Book of Music” and in his adaptations of the “Almagest”, functional dependencies, combinatorial problems, infinitesimal ideas and other issues of mathematical content were widely considered. Of great interest are the functional dependencies considered by al-Farabi in connection with the creation of music theory. Al-Farabi, for example, finds quantitative relationships between the pitch of sound ($y$) in the trumpet of a wind instrument and other physical quantities in the form of the following functional relationship:

$$y = \frac{k\sigma F h}{Dd},$$

where $k$ is the proportionality coefficient, $D$ is the diameter of the pipe, $h$ is the distance of the hole from the mouthpiece, $d$ is the diameter of the hole, $\sigma$ is the smoothness characteristic, $F$ is the injection force of the source. All these relationships are expressed verbally.

Al-Farabi's works on mathematics had a great influence on the mathematical creativity of Abu l-Wafa (940-998), Ibn Sina (980-1037), al-Biruni (973-1050), Omar Khayyam (1048-1131), Nasr ad-Din al-Tusi (1201-1274), Roger Bacon (1214-1294), Leonardo da Vinci (1452-1519) and others.
