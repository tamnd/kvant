## Book about space fireworks

“In the Zhong-Qing period, in the second year, on the 10th moon on the day of Kwei-Hao, an extraordinary star appeared in the middle of Nan-Meng. It was the size of a bamboo abacus and showed five colors in succession. It gradually diminished in brilliance until the 6th moon of the following year, when it disappeared.” Thus, with reference to the lunar calendar and the counting of years according to the reigns of the emperors, the Chinese chronicle tells about the appearance of the guest star. Scientists have determined that this star appeared on December 7, 185 AD. e. and was visible until July 186.

What was happening in the sky at this time? Astronomers are confident that the chronicle speaks of a supernova explosion that became brighter than Venus.

Supernova explosions are very rare occurrences. The last supernova explosion in our Galaxy was observed by Kepler and his contemporaries in 1604. Of course, this does not mean that supernovae have not erupted in the Galaxy since then, since astronomers may have accidentally missed some of them, while others were not visible from the observation post.

But astronomy of the 20th century has stepped far beyond the boundaries of our Galaxy. In the boundless world of galaxies, astronomers regularly discover supernovae: since 1885, the number of supernovae registered in other galaxies has exceeded 400. Patrols have already been established.

But why are supernova explosions so interesting to astronomers? The observed enormous increase in brightness (some supernovae sometimes exceed the brilliance of the galaxy in which they burst!), as well as careful analysis of the spectra of supernovae and the continuously expanding shells ejected by these stars, indicate that supernovae are exploding stars. Supernova explosions are one of the most ambitious phenomena currently occurring in the Universe, second only, perhaps, to the mysterious processes in the nuclei of galaxies and quasars. Astronomers have serious reasons to think that the study of the far from clear processes that cause and accompany supernova explosions will have a significant impact on our ideas about the origin and development of stars, the synthesis of chemical elements in the Universe, the origin of cosmic rays, and the nature of non-thermal optical, X-ray, gamma and radio emissions.

How our knowledge about supernovae and novae gradually developed, literally bit by bit, is described in the book by Moscow astronomer Yu. P. Pskovsky, which is called that way. “Novae and supernovae”\*).

The reader will meet supernovae in the second part of the book, on the pages of which a very “physical”, imaginative and accessible story is told about how astronomers discover and study supernovae.

The discovery of pulsars allowed astronomers to “see” the final stage of the evolution of some stars. Such stars should rapidly shrink at the end of their life. The nuclei of the atoms of the star's substance begin to collapse and break up into neutrons and protons. The resulting protons absorb electrons and, releasing energy, also turn into neutrons. The formation of a neutron star is a catastrophic explosive process with a rapid release of energy. The explosion produces a shock wave that ejects the outer layers of the star and creates an expanding shell of gas. We see all this as a distant supernova explosion. Far away? Could a supernova explode somewhere nearby? Didn’t such stars flare up near our solar system in the past? And you will find answers to these questions in the book by Yu. P. Pskovsky.
