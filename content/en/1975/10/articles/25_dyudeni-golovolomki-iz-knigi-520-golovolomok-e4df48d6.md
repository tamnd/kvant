Puzzles
Dudeney

The names of many famous popularizers of mathematics have been mentioned more than once on the pages of our magazine: Hugo Steinhaus, Szma Loyd, Martin Gardner, Lewis Carroll (the author, apparently, of the well-known and beloved “Alice in Wonderland”). Now we want to tell you about another wonderful master of puzzles - Henry E. Dudeney (1857-1930): this year, in the same series as three books by Gardner, books by Steinhaus and Carroll, his wonderful book “520 Puzzles” * was published.

Composing and solving puzzles was not only a professional thing for Dudeney (Dudeney is a talented selfie: he never went to college) - it was his recognition, his life’s work. If some interesting idea came to his mind during dinner, he would begin to draw thoughtfully... right on the tablecloth. The answers that Dudeney himself gave to his puzzles (we will publish them in the next issue «Kvant») very often cannot even be called “solutions” - rather, they are more brilliant than a contract. The book “520 Puzzles” will certainly be of interest to all mathematics lovers. You guys will find it especially useful: it has a lot of tasks available to you. Below we publish a selection of problems from the book by G. E. Dudeney. The publication was prepared by I. N. Klimova.

Subway stairs

One day, leaving Curley Street tube station, we ran into the young athlete Percy Longman. He stayed on the ecalator and said:

— I always go up the ecalator. You know, extra training never helps. This ecalator is the longest on the line - almost a thousand steps. But here’s what’s interesting - and this also applies to another, changing ecalator, along which I often have to climb: if, while climbing up, I step through two steps, then the last step takes one step; if I take three steps, then two steps; if in four, then five; if in five, then four; if after six, then five and, finally, if I step through seven steps, then the last step takes six steps. Why this happens, I don’t know.

When Percy went further up, stepping over three steps at once, we laughed and my companion said:

“He hardly suspects that if he took a step of 20 steps, then for the last step he would have 19 left!”

How many steps are there in the ecalator at Curley Street station, if the top platform is considered a step and the bottom one is not? **)

Casting vote

A convention of the Amalgamated Society of Itinerant Beggars (better known as the Union of Beggars) met to decide whether to go on strike to demand shorter hours and more begging. It was decided that during the voting, those members of society who cast their votes in favor would remain standing, and those who were against would be blown away.

“Gentlemen,” said the chairman of the meeting after the votes were counted, “I have the convenience to inform you that the strike was approved by a majority of votes, constituting a quarter of the opposition.” (Loud cheers.)

“Mr. Chairman,” he shouted from behind, “some of us were unable to sect.”

- Why?
- There are no chairs here.

- Then, perhaps, those who wanted, but were unable to sect, do not openly raise their hands... I see there are twelve of you, so the strike is canceled by a majority of one vote. (Whistles and chaos in the hall.)
How many members of the beggar society participated in the vote?

Correct the error

Hilde Wilson demanded to multiply a certain number by 409, but she made the mistake that children make when starting to study arithmetic: she placed the first digit of the product by 4 not under the third digit from the right, as it should be, but under the second. (We all did this in childhood, when a zero was found in a multiplier.) As a result of this small mistake, Hilda received a number that differed by not less than 328,320 from the correct answer.

What number did Hilda multiply by 409?

Pat Murphy

Many years ago such an incident occurred. Participants of one expedition fell into the clutches of bloodthirsty savages. Their wax, having received rich gifts, finally relented and allowed the captives to leave, but on the condition that half of them would be flogged. The expedition included 5 Englishmen and 5 native porters. The English decided to avoid flogging by standing in a circle as shown in figure, and instructing Pat Murphy (No. 1) to name a number, counting down to which would be used as a count.

The one who received the named number left the circle and was sent to execution, and the counting continued from this point again until the named number fell on the next person, etc.

If Pat had filled out the number correctly and started counting with the right person, then the big deletes would have been a great idea. But poor Pat got the number wrong and the man started counting from the wrong place. As a result, all the English were flogged, but they didn’t rush around.

Could you please indicate:
1) the number that poor Pat called, and the person from whom the counting began;
2) the number to be called and the person to start counting with?
In each case you need to find the minimum number.

Cats and mice

One morning, at Professor Rackbrane's table, the issue of exterminating rodents was being discussed, when suddenly the professor said:

— If a certain number of cats ate a total of 999,919 mice, and all the cats ate the same number of mice, then how many cats were there in total?

Someone suggested that perhaps one cat had eaten all the mice, but Rackbrain countered that he had said "mice." Then someone else kept the sentence that each cat ate one mouse, but the professor noticed that he said "mice." He also added, to help those present, that each cat ate more mice than there were cats.

Which answer will be correct?
