When a body moves in any environment, resistance forces arise that tend to slow it down. The mechanical movement of one solid body on the surface of another is driven by the forces of dry friction; In a liquid or gas, increased tension and aerodynamic resistance appear. etc.

The interaction of a body with the environment is a rather complex process, which leads to the fact that the energy of the body turns into heat over time, or, as physicists say, energy dispersion occurs. However, environmental resistance can also play the opposite role - increasing the energy of the body.

In this case, as a rule, oscillations occur. For example, the force of dry friction between the runners of a sled and the snow slows down the movement of the sled, and the same force acting between the sled and the string causes the string to vibrate. As you will see below, the reason for the occurrence of oscillation is the decreasing dependence of the friction force on the speed of movement. Oscillations occur when the friction force decreases with increasing speed.

Similar phenomena occur not only in mechanical, but also in electrical systems, in which oscillations occur when the dependence of voltage on current decreases.

Let us first consider the process of the occurrence of mechanical oscillations.

**String vibrations**

The sound of a violin is caused by the movement of the bow. It is, of course, impossible to explain here all the complex phenomena associated with the peculiarities of the sound of a violin. However, let's try to figure out why the violin string vibrates when the bow is drawn along it.

The friction forces between the bow and the string are the forces of dry friction. We can talk about static friction forces and sliding friction forces. The first force will arise between bodies that are in contact but motionless relative to each other, the second - when one body slides along the surface of another.

The static friction force, as is known, can take any value (depending on the external force) from zero to the maximum static friction force $F^0_{\text{тр}}$, while it is always equal in magnitude and opposite in direction to the external force.

The force of sliding friction depends on the materials of the body and on the state of the rubbing surfaces, as well as on the relative speed of these bodies. We will talk about the latter in more detail. The nature of the dependence on speed is different for different bodies; When the sliding speed increases, the front first allows a decrease in the sliding friction force, and then it begins to increase. **Negative resistance**

Now we will talk about similar phenomena (the occurrence of oscillations) in electrical systems. To describe an electrical system, it is convenient to use the current-voltage characteristic, i.e., the dependence of the voltage in the system on the current strength. For example, for ordinary resistance, Ohm's law is true: voltage is proportional to current, and the current-voltage characteristic is a straight line passing through the origin. However, for many systems (electron tube, gas discharge tube, transistor, etc.), the dependence of voltage on current is more complex.

It turns out that if the current-voltage characteristic is decreasing, i.e. if, as the current through the system increases, the voltage across it decreases, electrical oscillations can be excited in the system. Some semiconductor devices, a neon lamp, an electric arc, etc. have such properties. For all these devices, the current-voltage characteristic has a falling section,

and this, under certain conditions, leads to the excitation of oscillations.

Let's try to understand the reasons for the occurrence of electrical oscillations with a falling current-voltage characteristic. First of all, let's draw an analogy with mechanical systems. When current passes through a conductor, electrical forces perform work proportional to the applied voltage. This work is equal to the work of resistance forces to the movement of charges. Therefore, we can say that the resistance forces are proportional to the voltage. On the other hand, the speed of directional movement of charges is directly proportional to the current strength. Thus, the decreasing dependence of voltage on current corresponds to the decreasing dependence of resistance force on speed, and this, as we already know, leads to the excitation of oscillations.

Let us now carry out a more detailed analysis of the conditions for the occurrence of electrical oscillations, using the same energy considerations as in mechanics. Let the current-voltage characteristic of the system be as shown in figure 5. Knowing the current, the voltage can be found from this characteristic, and vice versa. Let, for example, a direct current $i_0$ flow in the system; then the voltage is $U_0$. If for some reason there is also a small alternating current component, so

$$i = i_0 + \Delta i_0 \sin \omega t,$$

then the voltage will also become variable:

$$U = U_0 + \Delta U_0 \sin \omega t$$

($\Delta i_0$ and $\Delta U_0$ are the amplitudes of the alternating component of current and voltage, respectively). It is obvious that with a falling current-voltage characteristic, the alternating current and alternating voltage fluctuate in opposite directions.

AC power, as is known, is determined by the formula

It means that alternating current does not consume, but, on the contrary, will increase its energy (just as the energy of a string increases during oscillation due to the decreasing dependence of resistance forces on speed). This energy is obviously drawn from the energy of direct current, the power of which $P_0 = U_0 i_0$ is always positive. Thus, with a decreasing current-voltage characteristic, the energy of the alternating current will increase over time, and the amplitude of the oscillations will increase.

To quantitatively consider this issue, the concept of negative electrical resistance is introduced, which is present in systems with decreasing current-voltage characteristics. However, the concept of resistance with a nonlinear dependence of voltage on current requires some clarification. Let's talk about this in more detail.

If a constant current $i_0$ flows through the system at a constant voltage $U_0$, then the resistance is determined by the formula

$$R = U_0 / i_0$$

and is called static resistance, or direct current resistance. As can be seen from figure 5, this value is equal to the tangent of the angle of inclination of the straight line drawn from the origin of coordinates to the point of the current-voltage characteristic corresponding to the current $i_0$ and voltage $U_0$:

$$R = tg \alpha.$$

This value, generally speaking, depends on the current strength $i_0$. If the system has a linear characteristic, then $R$ coincides with the usual resistance of the conductor and does not depend on the current.

In the case of alternating current, the concept of differential resistance, or alternating current resistance $r$, is introduced. It is determined by the tangent of the tangent to the current-voltage characteristic (see Fig. 5): $r = tg \beta$ and in absolute value it is equal to the ratio of the amplitudes of alternating voltage and current: $|r| = \Delta U_0 / \Delta i_0$. For a common conductor $r = R$, however, in the general case of the nonlinear current-voltage characteristic, the resistance to direct and alternating current is different.

If the current-voltage characteristic has a falling section, then in this section the tangent to the curve makes an obtuse angle with the current axis (angle $\beta$ on figure 5), the tangent of this angle is negative and, therefore, the value $r$ is negative. Thus, systems with a decreasing current-voltage characteristic have differential resistance.
