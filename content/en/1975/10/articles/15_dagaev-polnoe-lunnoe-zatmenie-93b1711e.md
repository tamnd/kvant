A total lunar eclipse is a fairly rare phenomenon. But on the night of November 18-19, 1975, it can be observed throughout almost the entire territory of the Soviet Union, except for the easternmost regions, where day will begin at the very beginning of the eclipse and the Moon will set below the horizon.

The Moon revolves around the Earth in the direction from West to East and when its left edge enters the Earth's shadow, a lunar eclipse begins. Until the Moon is completely immersed in the earth's shadow, the eclipse is called a partial eclipse and the phases Ф of a lunar eclipse are determined by the fraction b of the lunar diameter d covered by the earth's shadow: Ф = b/d. As the Moon plunges into the earth's shadow, the eclipse phase increases and at the moment of complete immersion it becomes equal to one - a lunar eclipse begins.

During a total eclipse, the phase is determined by the formula:

$$\Phi = \frac{R + r - \Delta}{2r},$$

where R is the angular radius of the earth's shadow, r is the angular radius of the lunar disk, and Δ is the angular distance between the centers of the lunar disk and the earth's shadow. In the middle of the present total lunar eclipse R=40′07″≈40′.1, r=15′07″≈15′.1 and Δ=22′53″≈22′9; therefore, the most phase of total eclipse is Фmax=1.07.

All phases of the lunar eclipse will be visible in the territory located west of the line passing near Kyakhta, Ulan-Ude and Olekminsk to Sangar and Wrangel Island, as well as north of the geographic parallel φ = 71°, where the Moon does not set beyond the horizon on this night. To the east of this line, the lunar eclipse will also be visible, but after it ends, the Moon will go below the horizon in a partial eclipse. To the east of the line running from Buren through Nelkan and Pevsk to Wrangel Island, it will be possible to see only partial phases.

Such observations include assessments of the general (interral) brightness of the Moon in different phases of the eclipse, assessments of the color of the Moon and individual details of its surface, and determination of the moments when the earth's shadow covers parts of the lunar surface.

Estimates of the general brightness of the Moon can be carried out in various ways using simple instruments.

One way is to view the Moon through a school phone (or simply through binoculars from the object's side). Having selected a suitable smoky (neutral) filter in advance, you can reduce the brightness of the Moon to the brightness of stars of zero magnitude (for example, Veggie, Arcturus, Capella) and during the entire eclipse at regular intervals (5-10 minutes) compare the brightness of the Moon observed on the phone with the brightness of stars of different magnitudes observed with the naked eye. To compare zero stars, select stars in the range from 0 to 4 stars and remember their position in order to quickly and accurately find them in the sky during observation. When the Moon's brightness becomes very weak, use a zero light filter and continue observing without it.

If you have a sensitive photographic exposure meter, then the change in the inter-al brightness of the Moon during an eclipse can be studied using its readings. To do this, a tube of such dimensions should be rigidly installed in front of the input response of the photoexponeter so that it protects the photodement from the scattered light of the sky, and only light from the Moon passes through it. So, with a tube length of 60 cm, its internal diameter should be 10 mm. The inner surface of the tube is coated with matte red black. The photographic exposure tube with the tube is mounted on the telescope tube so that the tube is parallel to the tube. When making observations, the telescope is pointed at the Moon and followed it all the time. The readings of the photographic exposure meter characterize the brightness of the Moon.

Interesting results can be obtained by recording the visibility of stars with the naked eye or with binoculars throughout the entire eclipse (including its partial phases) in a certain area of ​​the sky. (To ensure that the results of observations are not greatly affected by the absorption of light in the earth's atmosphere, it is necessary to select a region of the sky with a constant height above the horizon. Typically this is either the region around the North Star or the region of the zenith. The zenith of the new eclipse will include the constellations Perseus and Auriga.) First, from the star atlases, you need to copy about 30 stars of successively decreasing brightness from 4 to 6 stars onto a tracing paper. magnitude (or, when observing through a lens, up to 8.2 magnitude), record them, and during an eclipse, every 5 minutes, record the number of the faintest stars visible in the foreground. By calculating the star rank of the marked stars, one can find the degree of weakening of moonlight in various phases of the eclipse.

All described methods require mandatory recording of time with an accuracy of 1 minute and two or three observations before the start of the eclipse and after its end.

The color of the Moon should be assessed during a total eclipse, describing it in words or using the Danjon scale.

Danjon scale: 0—the eclipse is very dark, in the middle of the eclipse the Moon is almost or not visible at all; 1—the eclipse is dark, grey, details of the Moon’s surface are completely invisible; 2—the eclipse is dark red or red, a darker part is observed near the center of the shadow; 3—brick-red eclipse, the shadow is surrounded by a grayish or yellowish border; 4—the eclipse is copper-red, very bright, the outer zone is light, bluish.

Amateur photographers can create an eclipse on color film by making a series of symbols (30-35 pieces) with the same exposure. Based on the images of the Moon, it will be possible to judge both the color and the change in the brightness of the Moon.

The moments of the beginning and end of the covering of large parts of the lunar surface by the earth's shadow (with an accuracy of 5-10 s), the degree of their visibility, the moments of their complete disappearance (if this happens) and appearance, as well as the color of the dark shadow and border around it cannot be registered when observed on a phone with the highest possible magnification. A schematic map of the lunar surface can be found in a school astronomy textbook.
