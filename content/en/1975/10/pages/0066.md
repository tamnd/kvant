The caretaker's confusion. We will assume that in addition to the five rooms shown on the plan (see Fig. 3 in the article), there is also a sixth - the one into which the external doors lead (garden). The existence of a route with a given property depends only on the number of rooms with an odd number of doors. Indeed, if you enter a room with an even number of doors, then you will always leave this room by going through each door once. Therefore, such rooms can be ignored in further discussions.

Consider a room with an odd number of doors. If the beginning of the route is outside such a room, then the end is inside, and vice versa. Therefore, a given route exists when there are only two rooms (including the “outer” one) with an odd number of doors. In one of them the route begins, in the other it ends. The first plan satisfies the condition for the existence of a solution: there are only two rooms with an odd number of doors - “outer” (7 doors) and “B” (5 doors).

A nickel in the hands of a resourceful student. As a result of constructing circles $O_1$ and $O_2$ we have: $O_1A \not\equiv PO_2$. A pair of circles $O_2$ and $O_3$ gives: $PO_2 \not\equiv O_3Q$.
