As a result, the friction force will increase (the relative speed corresponds to the falling section!), and the string will deflect even more. With further deflection, the elastic force at some point must completely exceed the friction force (the elastic force is proportional to the magnitude of the deflection, and the sliding friction force cannot exceed the maximum value of the static friction force), and the string begins to move in the opposite direction. It will pass the equilibrium position, deflect again, stop, etc. This will excite vibrations of the string.

It is important that these fluctuations will be insignificant. In fact, when the string moves with a speed $\Delta v$ towards the bow, the friction force does positive work, and when moving in the opposite direction, it does negative work. But the relative speed $v_1 = u - \Delta v$ in the first case is less than the speed $u_2 = u + \Delta v$ in the second case, and therefore the friction force $F_{\text{tp}}(u - \Delta v)$, on the contrary, is greater than $F_{\text{tp}}(u + \Delta v)$. Thus, the positive work of friction forces when the string moves towards the bow is greater than the negative work when it returns, and in general the friction forces do positive work. The amplitude of the oscillations will increase. However, only up to a certain limit. At $v > v_0$ (see Fig. 1) the speed goes beyond the falling section, and then the negative work of the friction force can already become greater than the positive one. The energy, and therefore the amplitude of oscillations, will decrease.

As a result, the amplitude of oscillations is established at which the total work of friction forces is zero (more precisely, this work compensates for the loss of energy due to air resistance, inelastic xa

The excitation of sound vibrations when one solid body moves over the surface of another occurs very often. Dry rubbing in the door hinge can cause the door to squeak. Positive script, shoes. The script can be done simply with your finger, running it over some smooth surface. The phenomena that occur in this case are in many ways similar to the excitation of vibrations of a violin string. Initially, there is no slipping, and elastic deformation occurs. Then sleep occurs, and body vibrations are excited. The oscillations do not die out, since thanks to the falling characteristic forces of dry friction, the necessary energy is supplied due to the work of these forces.

When the nature of the dependence of friction forces on speed changes, the squeak disappears. It is known, for example, that for this it is enough to lubricate the rubbing surfaces. The force of liquid friction (at low speeds) is proportional to the speed, and there are no conditions necessary to excite vibrations. On the contrary, when they want to excite vibrations, the surfaces are treated in a special way to make the reduction in friction forces with increasing speed sharper. For example, a violin bow is rubbed with rosin for this purpose.
