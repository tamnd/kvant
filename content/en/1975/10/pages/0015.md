**Negative resistance**

Now we will talk about similar phenomena (the occurrence of oscillations) in electrical systems. To describe an electrical system, it is convenient to use the current-voltage characteristic, i.e., the dependence of the voltage in the system on the current strength. For example, for ordinary resistance, Ohm's law is true: voltage is proportional to current, and the current-voltage characteristic is a straight line passing through the origin. However, for many systems (electron tube, gas discharge tube, transistor, etc.), the dependence of voltage on current is more complex.

It turns out that if the current-voltage characteristic is decreasing, i.e. if, as the current through the system increases, the voltage across it decreases, electrical oscillations can be excited in the system. Some semiconductor devices, a neon lamp, an electric arc, etc. have such properties. For all these devices, the current-voltage characteristic has a falling section,

⟦column⟧

and this, under certain conditions, leads to the excitation of oscillations.

Let's try to understand the reasons for the occurrence of electrical oscillations with a falling current-voltage characteristic. First of all, let's draw an analogy with mechanical systems. When current passes through a conductor, electrical forces perform work proportional to the applied voltage. This work is equal to the work of resistance forces to the movement of charges. Therefore, we can say that the resistance forces are proportional to the voltage. On the other hand, the speed of directional movement of charges is directly proportional to the current strength. Thus, the decreasing dependence of voltage on current corresponds to the decreasing dependence of resistance force on speed, and this, as we already know, leads to the excitation of oscillations.

Let us now carry out a more detailed analysis of the conditions for the occurrence of electrical oscillations, using the same energy considerations as in mechanics. Let the current-voltage characteristic of the system be as shown in figure 5. Knowing the current, the voltage can be found from this characteristic, and vice versa. Let, for example, a direct current $i_0$ flow in the system; then the voltage is $U_0$. If for some reason there is also a small alternating current component, so

$$i = i_0 + \Delta i_0 \sin \omega t,$$

then the voltage will also become variable:

$$U = U_0 + \Delta U_0 \sin \omega t$$

($\Delta i_0$ and $\Delta U_0$ are the amplitudes of the alternating component of current and voltage, respectively). It is obvious that with a falling current-voltage characteristic, the alternating current and alternating voltage fluctuate in opposite directions.
