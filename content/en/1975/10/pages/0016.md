AC power, as is known, is determined by the formula

$$P = \frac{\Delta U_0}{2} \frac{\Delta i_0}{2} \cos \varphi = \Delta U_\pi \Delta i_\pi \cos \varphi,$$

where $\Delta U_\pi$ and $\Delta i_\pi$ are the effective values ​​of voltage and current, $\varphi$ is the phase shift between voltage and current. In our case, $\cos \varphi = -1$ and therefore $P < 0$. This result has a simple physical meaning. It means that alternating current does not consume, but, on the contrary, will increase its energy (just as the energy of a string increases during oscillation due to the decreasing dependence of resistance forces on speed). This energy is obviously drawn from the energy of direct current, the power of which $P_0 = U_0 i_0$ is always positive. Thus, with a decreasing current-voltage characteristic, the energy of the alternating current will increase over time, and the amplitude of the oscillations will increase.

To quantitatively consider this issue, the concept of negative electrical resistance is introduced, which is present in systems with decreasing current-voltage characteristics. However, the concept of resistance with a nonlinear dependence of voltage on current requires some clarification. Let's talk about this in more detail.

If a constant current $i_0$ flows through the system at a constant voltage $U_0$, then the resistance is determined by the formula

$$R = U_0 / i_0$$

and is called static resistance, or direct current resistance. As can be seen from figure 5, this value is equal to the tangent of the angle of inclination of the straight line drawn from the origin of coordinates to the point of the current-voltage characteristic corresponding to the current $i_0$ and voltage $U_0$:

$$R = tg \alpha.$$

This value, generally speaking, depends on the current strength $i_0$. If the system has a linear characteristic, then $R$ coincides with the usual resistance of the conductor and does not depend on the current.

In the case of alternating current, the concept of differential resistance, or alternating current resistance $r$, is introduced. It is determined by the tangent of the tangent to the current-voltage characteristic (see Fig. 5): $r = tg \beta$ and in absolute value it is equal to the ratio of the amplitudes of alternating voltage and current: $|r| = \Delta U_0 / \Delta i_0$. For a common conductor $r = R$, however, in the general case of the nonlinear current-voltage characteristic, the resistance to direct and alternating current is different.

If the current-voltage characteristic has a falling section, then in this section the tangent to the curve makes an obtuse angle with the current axis (angle $\beta$ on figure 5), the tangent of this angle is negative and, therefore, the value $r$ is negative. Thus, systems with a decreasing current-voltage characteristic have differential resistance.
