## Daily allowance
Earth rotation

The article by Ya. A. Smorodinsky “Coriolis Force” (see p. 2 of this issue of the magazine) describes in detail the force of inertia that acts on bodies moving relative to a rotating reference frame. Such a “natural” system is, first of all, the Earth. There are many phenomena, for example, the deflection of freely falling bodies, unequal erosion of banks by rivers, the occurrence of air vortices and winds, etc., which are explained by the action of the Coriolis force.

Historically, the first experiment that confirmed the rotation of the Earth around its axis was Foucault's experiment. This article talks about a simple experiment that clearly demonstrates the daily rotation of the Earth.

Imagine a large cylindrical vessel ending in a funnel (Fig. 1). The vessel is filled with water and suspended from the ceiling using a long cord. At the initial moment, when the hole in the funnel is closed, the vessel is at rest relative to the Earth. What happens if the hole is opened? For simplicity of reasoning, we will assume that the experiment is carried out at the north pole of the Earth. In the place of the vessel where the funnel is located, the flowing water has not only a vertical, but also a horizontal velocity projection.

Let us denote by $\mathbf{v}_0$ the horizontal projection of the speed of water relative to the vessel. The value $\mathbf{v}_0$ depends on the height of the water column above the given

level in the funnel and from the distance to the axis of the vessel.

The presence of speed $v_0$ causes the emergence of the Coriolis force. figure 2 shows the directions of the Earth's rotation, velocities $v_0$ and Coriolis forces $F_К$ for a certain layer of water. The Coriolis force acting on each small element of the mass of water has a rotational moment relative to the axis of symmetry of the vessel. This causes the water in the vessel to spin. Since there is friction between the water and the walls of the vessel, the vessel will also begin to move, and the direction of movement of the vessel coincides with the direction of rotation of the Earth.

As water flows out of the container, the magnitude of the velocity $v_0$ decreases and hence the Coriolis force acting on the water decreases (since $F_К \sim v_0$). What will be the nature of the movement of water under the influence of a variable force $F_К$? The magnitude of the acceleration imparted by this force decreases, while the speed of rotation of the water increases, but the further, the slower.

The rotation of the vessel causes the thread on which it is suspended to twist. In this case, a moment of elastic deformation forces arises, which counteracts the twisting of the thread, and therefore the rotation of the vessel. The magnitude of the moment of deformation forces increases as the angle of twist of the thread increases, that is, as the speed of rotation of the vessel increases. Since the Coriolis force, co-

As a result, the speed of rotation of the vessel decreases, but the vessel still rotates in the direction of the rotation of the Earth.

At some point in time, the angular velocity of rotation of the vessel becomes equal to zero, and then, under the influence of the moment of elastic deformation forces, the vessel begins to rotate in the opposite direction. The angular speed of rotation increases. Now the force of deformation begins to decrease (the thread unwinds). The action of the moment of the Corolis force (this force is still decreasing) is now directed against the rotation of the vessel and leads to a decrease in the rotation speed. . . At some point in the rotation, the decreasing moment of the deformation forces will “catch up” in magnitude with the moment of the Corolis force, after which the rotation speed begins to decrease, etc.

When conducting the experiment, we used a cylindrical vessel with a diameter of 25 cm and a height of 30 cm (see Fig. 1). The vessel was filled with a funnel with a response diameter of 8 mm. A coupling with four symmetrically located screws was found on the top tube of the funnel.

Soft rubber on a hard base was tightly pressed to the end of the tube using a thread thrown through two opposite screws. The thread was tied in a knot on one of the screws. We suspended the vessel from the ceiling on a double badminton string 2.5 m long (you can take two cords twisted in opposite directions to determine side effects during rotation). Water was poured into the vessel almost to the top edge, and when it calmed down, the thread knot was burned out. In all cases, we observed that as water flowed out through the outlet in the funnel, the vessel rotated first counterclockwise, and then into the opposite string.

The experiment can also be carried out with glass funnels suspended on double thin scaffolding. But it turns out that the angle of rotation of the vessel significantly depends on the diameter of the funnel outlet. On the one hand, the effect of the Corolis force is more noticeable, the smaller the response. But on the other hand, as the response decreases, the role of liquid viscosity increases. We were able to observe a noticeable rotation of the funnel, together with a cord of 1 liter of water, with a response diameter of 5 mm.
