⟦figure⟧
Fig. 10.

Let's prove it. Let us place the cone on the horizontal plane I (Fig. 10, a). Let us introduce the following notations: $S$ is the vertex of the cone, $\varphi$ is its axial angle, $SQ$ is the generatrix along which the cone touches plane I.

Let us intersect the cone with plane II, parallel to I (and, therefore, parallel to the generatrix $SQ$), at a height $h$ (the distance between the planes). It is necessary to prove that plane II intersects the cone along a parabola.

Let us draw through the generatrix $SQ$ and through the axis of the cone the vertical plane $SQP$, shown separately in figure 10, b; it intersects plane II along the straight line $OY$—the axis of symmetry of the section under study. Through the point $O$ of the meeting of this line with the cone, we draw a straight line $X'X \perp OY$ in plane II and take $X'X$ and $OY$ as the coordinate axes. An arbitrary point $M$ of our section has an abscissa $ON = x$ and an ordinate $NM = OL = y$ in this coordinate system. We need to find the equation of the cutting line in this coordinate system and show that it has the form
$x^2 = 2py$.

Through $M$ we draw a circular section of the cone $QMP$, perpendicular to its axis. From the right triangle $SML$ we have

$LM^2 = SM^2 - SL^2 = SP^2 - SL^2$. (A)

Obviously,

$$SP = SO + OP = SO + OL = \frac{h}{\sin\varphi} + y.$$ (b)

From triangle $SOL$

$SL^2 = SO^2 + OL^2 -$
$-2SO \cdot OL\cos(180° - \varphi) =$

$$= \left(\frac{h}{\sin\varphi}\right)^2 + y^2 + \frac{2hy}{\sin\varphi}\cos\varphi.$$ (in)

⟦column⟧

Substituting (b) and (c) into (a), we get

$$x^2 = \left[\left(\frac{h}{\sin\varphi}\right)^2 + y^2 + \frac{2hy}{\sin\varphi}\right] -$$

$$- \left[\left(\frac{h}{\sin\varphi}\right)^2 + y^2 + \frac{2hy}{\sin\varphi}\cos\varphi\right] =$$

$$= 2h\,\frac{1-\cos\varphi}{\sin\varphi}\,y.$$

Denoting $h \cdot \dfrac{1-\cos\varphi}{\sin\varphi}$ by $p$, we have

$x^2 = 2py$. The section produces a parabola with parameter $p$.

In particular, if the axial angle of the cone $\varphi$ is straight, then $p = h$. Thus, if a cone with a right angle in the axial section is intersected by a plane perpendicular to one of its generatrices, then in the section a parabola is obtained, the parameter of which is equal to the distance from the top of the cone to the section plane. It is interesting that Menaechmus, who discovered the ellipse, hyperbola and parabola around 360 BC, obtained them as sections of a circular cone, respectively, with an acute, obtuse and right axial angle with a plane perpendicular to one of the generatrices, and they were first called *sections of an acute, obtuse and rectangular cone*. The modern names for these curves were given later by Apollonius.
