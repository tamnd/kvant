From the point of view of a stationary observer located in the inertial reporting system, the plane of oscillations of the matnik, of course, is motionless, and the Earth rotates counterclockwise. For the same reason, the trace of the ball’s movement on the horizontal plane was not a straight line, but a complex curve (Fig. 6).

An earthly observer connected with the rotating reporting system will explain the rotation of the oscillation plane by the action of the Korpolis force on the ball. For small deviations of the matnik from the equilibrium position, we can assume that the speed of the ball at any moment is perpendicular to the angular speed $\omega_3$ of the rotation of the Earth, therefore the Korpolis force always lies in the horizontal plane and is directed perpendicular to the speed of the ball to the right, when looking in the direction of movement of the ball. This force causes the rotation of the plane of the oscillations of the mat, and the angular speed of rotation $\omega$ is just equal to the angular speed of the rotation of the Earth.

It is easy to show that if the matnik is suspended in a place whose latitude is equal to $\varphi$, then the angular velocity will be equal to $\omega_3$ sin$\varphi$, vanishing at the equator. To do this, it is enough to find the projection $\omega_3$ onto the normal to the horizon plane (carry out the proof to the end).

The picture of the swinging matnik contains interesting details. If we try to understand what kind of line the Foucault matnik draws on a plane, we will find that the shape of the trajectory depends on how the matnik began to swing.

If the matnik began to move from a position of rest (vertical position on the pole), then during movement it will always pass through the stripes and will draw on the plane a curve like the one depicted at figure 6, $a$.

If at the initial moment the matnik was maximally deflected, then for an inertial observer it had an angular velocity of zero at that moment, which means that for such an observer the matnik does not swing in one plane, but is written out on a plane perpendicular to the plane of oscillations, an ellipse. An observer on Earth will see a trajectory that can be described as a rotating ellipse (Fig. 6, 6). The trajectory no longer passes through the stripes.
