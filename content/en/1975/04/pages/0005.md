In a rotating reporting system, there are two types of inertial forces: the centrifugal inertial force, which depends only on the distance to the rotation axis, and the Korpolis force, which depends only on the speed of the body (material point) relative to the rotating reporting system. The first force was described in detail in the article “Forces of Inertia”. Let us recall its main properties.

Centrifugal force of inertia

Why does a boy who rides a carcelia get a cap when the rotation speed increases? This question can be answered in different ways. From the point of view of a stationary observer standing near the carcelia, the announcement will be like this. While the rotation speed was low, the cap was held on the boy’s head by the force of friction, which imparted the necessary centripetal acceleration to the cap. With an increase in the angular velocity of rotation of the carcelium, the centripetal acceleration should increase, and the static friction force can only increase to a certain limit. Therefore, at some point, the hat loses contact with the boy’s head; it moves by inertia evenly and in a straight line, and the carcel, together with the boy, moves away from the hat.

A mobile observer associated with a carcel will say that in addition to friction forces, a centrifugal force of inertia, equal in absolute value, also acts on the cap

$$F_{и.ц} = m\omega^2 r$$

where $m$ is the mass of the cap, $\omega$ is the angular velocity of rotation of the carcelium, $r$ is the distance to the rotation axis. The centrifugal force of inertia lies in a plane perpendicular to the axis of rotation and is directed radially away from the center. The expression for $F_{и.ц}$ can be written in vector form:

$$F_{и.ц} = m\omega^2 r$$

where $r$ is the radius vector of the body under consideration; it lies in a plane perpendicular to the axis of rotation. If the centrifugal force of inertia is interrupted by the size of the servant's cap on the boy's head, the cap will fly away.

The Power of Korpolis

The situation is more complicated if the material point moves in a rotating coordinate system. In this case, in addition to the centrifugal force of inertia, another force acts on the point, depending on the speed of movement of the point relative to the rotating coordinate system. It is called the Korpolis force *).

We can determine the Korpolis force if we consider two special cases:

1) the velocity of a material point is directed perpendicular to its radius vector in a rotating reporting frame;
2) the speed of the point is directed along this radius.
