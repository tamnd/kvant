In this case, however, it is assumed that the point moves in a straight line all the time. As you now understand, such an assumption can only be made if the rotation of the Earth is not taken into account.

In fact, from the point of view of an observer in an inertial reference frame, a body thrown from a height $H$ has a horizontal speed $\omega_3(H+R)\cos\varphi$, where $R$ is the radius of the Earth, $\omega_3$ is its angular velocity, $\varphi$ is the latitude of the place. While falling, the body maintains this speed and in its horizontal motion will outstrip points at rest (relative to the Earth) (for example, windows in a house if the body falls from the roof) at heights $h<H$, since these points move at a lower speed $\omega_3(h+R)\cos\varphi$.

Let's try to calculate how much a body falling from a height $H$ will deviate from the vertical to the east (the Earth rotates from west to east, and the body overtakes the Earth). This problem is easier to solve in a reference frame associated with the Earth, using the expression for the Coriolis force *).

So, let the body fall from a height $H$, where it was at time $t=0$. The body will move uniformly accelerated, and its vertical velocity component will change over time according to the law

$$v=gt.$$

For an earthly observer, the horizontal component of the Coriolis force will act on the body, which will increase the horizontal component of the body's momentum, deflecting it to the east. Let's write the equation for this movement:

$$ma=2m\omega_3 v\cos\varphi,$$

or, taking into account the dependence of speed on time,

$$a=2\omega_3\cos\varphi\, gt=At,$$

where $A=2\omega_3 g\cos\varphi$ is a constant value.

You didn't come across this kind of equation in school. If $a$ were constant, then the movement would be uniformly accelerated, but our acceleration increases linearly with time. First, let's find an expression for speed. In uniformly accelerated motion $v=v_0+at$ and $a=\mathrm{const}$. Since now $a$ depends linearly on time, it is reasonable to assume that the speed depends quadratically on time:

$$v=v_0+\alpha t+\beta t^2.$$

Let's calculate the acceleration. To do this, let’s find the speed at the adjacent time $t+\Delta t$ $(\Delta t \ll t)$:

$$v=v_0+\alpha(t+\Delta t)+\beta(t+\Delta t)^2.$$

Subtracting the previous one from this equality and discarding the term with $(\Delta t)^2$ due to its smallness compared to the other terms, we obtain

$$a=\frac{v_{t+\Delta t}-v_t}{\Delta t}=\alpha+2\beta t.$$

Now we see that if $a=At$ then $\alpha=0$, $\beta=\dfrac{A}{2}$. Then (assuming also that $v=0$ at $t=0$, whence $v_0=0$)

$$v=\frac{At^2}{2}.$$

Now we can find the path $s$. Let's assume that

$$s=s_0+\alpha_1 t+\beta_1 t^2+\gamma t^3.$$

This expression differs by one extra term $\gamma t^3$ from the formula for uniformly accelerated motion. Let us write down the path traveled to the moment of time $t+\Delta t$ and compose, as before, the relation

$$v=\frac{s_{t+\Delta t}-s_t}{\Delta t}=\alpha_1+2\beta_1 t+3\gamma t^2$$

(here we have dropped the terms with $(\Delta t)^2$ and $(\Delta t)^3$). Comparing with the previous expression for speed and also assuming $s_0=0$, we find that

$$\alpha_1=0,\ \beta_1=0,\ \gamma=\frac{1}{6}A$$

And

$$s=\frac{At^3}{6}.$$

Returning now to our problem, we can express the time $t$ in terms of the final speed $v_0$ from the law for vertical speed: $t=\dfrac{v_0}{g}$. Since $A=2\omega_3 g\cos\varphi$, the displacement of the falling body to the east turns out to be equal to

$$s=\frac{1}{3}\frac{\omega_3\cos\varphi}{g^2}v_0^3.$$

Substitute the corresponding numbers here yourself and calculate $s$ if the body falls, for example, from a height of 1 $км$. The calculations for a body thrown upward from the surface of the Earth look somewhat longer. You can do this exercise yourself.
