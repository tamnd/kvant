Applied mathematics and mathematicians

L. E. Sadovsky

We turned out to be contemporaries of the powerful progress of mathematical knowledge, the formation of new branches of mathematics, new areas of its applications and such scientific directions as “mathematical economics”, “mathematical linguistics”, “mathematical psychology”, “mathematical methods in biology”.
The invasion of mathematics has introduced millions of areas of intellectual and practical human activity, the creation of electronic digital computers (EDC) over the past decades are essential features of the worldwide production of the process called the scientific and technological revolution.
This process required a restructuring of mathematical education at all levels: in schools, technical colleges. Willingly or unwittingly, we are all participants in this perestroika.
Primary and secondary school students are already learning mathematics using new textbooks, teaching new concepts and programs. In 1975-1976, 3,000 students will graduate from the country's experimental schools and classes, and in 1977, all graduates of our schools will be trained according to new programs.
Teachers. schools are already teaching in a new way, restructuring teaching methods. Mathematics departments of higher education institutions and universities, in turn, are forced to take into account real situations and also change a lot in long-established procedures. Scientists and engineers, in order to keep up, are forced to master new branches of applied mathematics and widely use digital computers.
Thus, a huge part of the actively working society is directly or indirectly involved in the process of mathematization of sciences.
In this case, many large and small problems will arise. Covering them, even partially, in a small country is another problem (and an insoluble one at that!).
Therefore, keeping in mind the natural interests of graduates of secondary and special physics and mathematics schools and boarding schools, it remained in a new language, generated by the growing need of modern society for “mathematized personnel”. Here, firstly, I mean the significant expansion of mathematical courses in traditional and new faculties of technical universities and, secondly, the departments (streams) of applied mathematics created in recent years in a number of the largest universities in the country.
By the end of this century, current graduates will become an active scientific and engineering force. In conditions of rapid development of technical, technical production and professional skills, an engineer is obliged to prepare for meaningful new phenomena, for changes in professional orientation, for search work at the intersection of various fields. According to conservative estimates, the ninth five-year plan required over 125,000 specialists in applied mathematics and automated control systems. More than 50,000 applied mathematicians will be trained over the current five-year period.

More than 60 universities across the country have begun training such specialists. For several years, universities and major higher education institutions have been training engineers-mathematicians (or mathematicians-engineers). Over the past years, the country has received approximately 10 thousand engineers and mathematicians. Since 1969, the specialty "engineer-mathematician" has been transformed into a new specialty - "applied mathematics", which has several specializations. The main ones:

1) ACS software,
2) computer software,
3) application of computer technology (to solve engineering, economic, managerial and other problems).

The problems faced by an engineer-mathematician are very diverse both in their origin and in difficulty. Therefore, they can only be described in general terms.

Specialists in mathematical support of automated control systems are mainly engaged in the design of basic diagrams of automatic control systems, mathematical description and selection of optimal options for production processes, management, collection and processing of information, construction of optimal volumetric plans (for a year, quarter), operational planning (for example, daily), economic and statistical research (from relationships with related industries and sales of products to a system of material incentives) and others.

The automated control system is a kind of hierarchical system. In it, the goal of each subsystem is to make certain decisions. As you move from larger parts to smaller ones, tasks become gradually more detailed. The more specific the task, the more difficult it is, as a rule. The most difficult issues include operational planning and management. Establishing criteria for utility and optimality causes great difficulties. Usually, creating a valid criterion is part of solving a problem. Approximately this range of tasks describes the specialization of mathematical support for automated control systems - one of the above-mentioned specializations of “applied mathematics”.

The second specialization (computer software) is the so-called internal computer support: compiling sets (packages) of programs aimed at solving a certain range of problems; learning various programming languages; created

To become an applied mathematician, it is not at all necessary to strive for universities or capital cities. This opportunity is open to lovers of mathematics, physics and their applications in many educational institutions of the country.

Applied mathematicians - graduates of specialized faculties of universities and colleges - are highly valued everywhere. The question of what is applied mathematics is naturally preceded by the question: “What is mathematics?”

The words of F. Engels, which have become classic, are widely known: “Pure mathematics has as its object the spatial forms and quantitative relations of the active world” (“Anti-Dühring”, K. Marx and F. Engels, op. ed. 2, vol. XX, p. 37).

Many scientists have spoken about the peculiarities of mathematics. Thus, MSU professor G.E. Shilov spoke at a meeting of the mathematical section of the Moscow House of Scientists in 1972: “Matematika is the science of mathematical structures considered in their prehistory, that is, the accumulation of facts and connections between the phenomena of reality, in their history - the analysis and dissection of logical connections and concepts, the critique of dissected concepts into axioms, in their final axiomatic design and development and, finally, in their entering other areas of both mathematics itself and other sciences.”

At the same time, the famous mathematician R. Courant Spring figuratively wrote: “To the question - what is mathematics? - it is impossible to give a detailed answer on the basis of physical generalizations, semantic definitions alone, or with the help of objectified newspaper and magazine verbiage. Just as it is impossible to give a general definition of music or painting: no one can appreciate these types of art without understanding what rhythm, harmony and structure are in music or form, color and composition in painting. To understand day-to-day mathematics, a long-term penetration into its constituent elements is even more necessary.

Taking into account all of the above, we can nevertheless give mathematics some general definition... The relationship of the general with the particular, deluchini with the constructive approach, logic with imagination - they are the very essence of mathematics...

In other words, entering into weak abstract generalities must start from the concrete and the particular and end with the concrete and the particular” (“Matematika in the modern world”, “Mir”, 1967, p. 16).

One cannot but agree that attempts to give an exhaustive definition of mathematics will introduce its boundaries, while mathematics is capable of modeling all kinds of thinking processes and numerous situations studied in science and technology. It is in the construction of mathematical models that processes and schemes are formed, and in their study the applied aspects of mathematics are revealed.

Any scientific approach is connected with the construction of a model of the phenomenon being studied. A model in the sense of defining the process of the object itself; usually it imitates not all, but only the most important of its features and is therefore more convenient for study. Models can be of different nature: mathematical, physical, chemical, economic and other. Everything depends on the object being studied (phenomenon, situation) and on those of its properties that are taken into account by the model. Thus, a mathematical model of currents flowing in an electrical circuit is a system of linear equations (Kirchhoff’s law). The physical model that reproduces the work of the train driver is automatic. A physical model of a building structure can serve as a system of elastic rods, and a mathematical model of the latter can serve as a system of equations that relates the stress in these rods. N. Wiener and his collaborators built a mechanical model that simulates the trembling of a human hand with a disturbed movement coordinate. Then a mathematical model of the phenomenon under study was built.

In reality, there is nothing unusual in constructing a model: every engineering, economic and other calculation performed using the means and language of mathematics turns out to be, in essence, mathematical modeling.

The concepts of numbers, functions, and equations that are well known to you serve, under certain conditions, as mathematical models of certain real objects. There are economic and mathematical models of the use of mathematical resources in various sectors of the economy, models of production and consumption, and others.

The problems associated with choosing a model, its study, optimization of some criteria for its quality, inevitable simplifications, assumptions, necessary calculations, and much more, are very diverse, difficult and cannot be considered here. Everything that has been said is only illustrative: here, for example, is what applied mathematics does. There is currently no consensus on the question of whether applied mathematics is an independent discipline. However, this circumstance is not existent for us. Especially in the absence of an exhaustive definition of all mathematics as such.

Apparently, there are no indisputable features by which mathematical constructions can be classified into theoretical (“numerical”) and applied. Everything is relative. For example, in the time of K. Gauss, complex numbers were considered by most mathematicians as highly abstract objects. But years passed, the theory of functions of a complex variable arose, its apparatus found applications in hydrodynamics and aerodynamics (in calculating the lift of an aircraft wing), in theoretical electrical engineering and other fields. Abstract group theory, which originated from the works of Lagrange (1736-1813), has found educational application in specific problems of crystallography, theoretical physics, quantum mechanics, coding theory (in the mathematical science of transmitting messages along communication lines), and problems of solvability of algebraic equations in radicals. The existence of some particle elements was anticipated by the group's theory long before their actual discovery *).

In terms of group theory, F. Klein classified various geometrines. The concept of a group, along with the concepts of set, function, limit, has become one of the main ones in mathematics. The history of mathematics is very rich in similar examples of the development of the pure into the applied and reverse processes.

In the already mentioned book, R. Courant wrote: “In fact, between “numerical” and “applied” mathematics it is impossible to check the clarity of the face. That is why in mathematics there should not be a division between the high priests, who worship the infallible mathematical beauty and pay attention only to their own inclinations, and the workers who serve them. Such “casteism” is, at best, a symbol of human limitations, which keeps most people from freely wandering through the vast simple human interests.”

Klein wrote about this in his book “Elementary Mathematics from a Higher Point of View” (Gostekhidat, 1934): “Purely logical concepts should constitute, so to speak, the solid skeleton of the organism of mathematics, giving it stability and reliability. But the life of mathematics itself, the most important guidance and its productivity are predominantly concerned.”
