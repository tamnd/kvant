Chess-mathematical problems of S. Loyd

Samuel Loyd (1841-1911) is one of the greatest masters of challenging problems and puzzles. He came up with many great problems that have not lost their popularity to this day. And the game “Fifteen” brought Loyd world service (a detailed study of this game can be found in «Kvant», 1974, No. 2).

Interestingly, Loyd was not only an outstanding inventor of puzzles, but also one of the greatest chess composers of all time. Everyone who has had to solve Loyd's chess problems has more than once admired the wit and uniqueness of the composer's ideas.

Until the age of 17, young Sam attended public school, and if he had gone to college, he would probably have turned out to be a very strong mathematician or engineer. However, despite his love for mathematics, his passion for chess turned out to be stronger. He did not go to college, but spent all his time playing chess. Loyd's first problem was published by a New York newspaper when the author was only 14 years old. At the age of 16, he already edited a section in the American Chess Monthly, and in the seventies, centuries passed into the daily chess page in the Scientific American magazine. Obviously his article opened with a problem in which the arrangement of the figure depicted some letter or geometric figure, for example, “Wheel within a wheel” (Fig. 1). This is how this wheel “spins”: a) 1. F: h3 + Kr: h3 2. Kg5 mate; 6) 1...Ke7 + 2. Ke4 R: f4 mate; b) 1. Qg3 + Q: g3 2. Kg6 + Q: g6 mate; d) 1...Ke7 + 2. Ke4 Kg5 + 3. Q: g5 mate (c) and d)—“reverse” mate).

Of course, Loyd sought to combine his love of mathematics and chess. And therefore it is not surprising that many of his problems have both a mathematical and chess basis. Now we will consider 15, from our point of view, the most interesting chess-mathematical problems of Samu-

Problem 3. The queen stands on square a1. Name the number of moves during which the queen can move across all squares of the board and return to the original square.

The required number of moves is 14. There are six routes in total: three are given on figure 3, the rest are obtained from them by mirror reflection relative to the diagonal a1 - h8. The squares where the queen makes stops are marked in red.

Loyd proved that if the initial square is in the blue area at figure 4, then the queen's closed route across all squares of the board already takes 15 moves. If the initial field is in the red area, then 14 moves are enough. Report for yourself how this red area is obtained on the symmetrical figure 4 (from figure 3).

Problem 4. In what minimum number of moves can the queen move around all the squares of the board $3 \times 3$ if she is cut to go beyond the board?

Four moves are enough (Fig. 5). In popular literature, this task is often formulated differently: without lifting the pencil from the paper, cross out the flowers with straight segments of nine points arranged in the shape of a square.

Problem 5. The queen stands on square d1. What is the longest (in geometric sense) path he can travel in 5 moves (traversing nine fields)?

Problem 6. Starting from square c3, move the queen around the entire board in 15 moves, ending on square f6. None of the fields must be completed twice.

Problem 7. Place 16 queens on the board so that there are no more than two of them on each vertical, horizontal and diagonal.

In this case, the turn of moves does not coincide, white and black knights are forbidden to retreat (white to the left, black to the right), and one knight can stand on each vertical.

This problem is one of the most difficult Lloyd's puzzles. Lloyd was very proud of him.

Loyd showed a lot of ingenuity in “analyzing” the initial arrangement of pieces on the board. You, of course, know that the fastest mate is possible already on the second move, and White gets it: 1.f4 e6 2.g4 Qh4 mat. This checkmate is called “stupid” in chess reference books. More famous is the four-move “children’s” mat, which chess beginners often receive: 1.e4 e5 2.Cc4 Kc6 3.Fh5 Kf6 4.Ф: f7 mat.

As for the draw outcome, the following three situations often occur in which the game ends in a draw:

a) on the stalemate board;
b) one of the opponents gives a perpetual check;
b) each side has a king left. Naturally, new challenges arise.

Problem 10. What is the fastest way for the party to reach the position at figure 8?

Here, each side must eat 15 enemy pieces and pawns. Since White cannot take anything on the first move, the game must consist of at least 16 moves. However, only 17-move games are known that lead to a complete emptying of the board. Depending on the final arrangement of the queens, there are many solutions. In our case, we need to make the following 17 moves: 1.e4 d5 2.ed Q: d5 3.Qh5 Q: a2 4.Q: h7 Q: b1 5.Q: g7 R: h2 6.R: a7 R: g2 7.R: b7 R: g1 8.R: c7 Q: b2 9.R: c8 + Kpd7 10.R: b8 R: b8 11.S: b2 L: b2 12.L: g1 L: c2 13.F: f7 L: d2 14.F: e7 + Kp: e7 15.L: g8 L: f2 16.L: f8 L: f1 + 17.K: f1Kp: f8.

Problem 12. *What is the fastest way for a game to end in stalemate if none of the pieces (neither white nor black) should be captured?*

Paradoxically, with such a seemingly strong additional condition, the game is extended by only two moves: 1. d4 d6 2. Qd2 e5 3. a4 e4 4. Qf4 f5 5. h3 Ce7 6. Qh2 Ce6 7. Ra3 c5 8. Rg3 Qa5+ 9. Nd2 Ch4 10. f3 Cb3 11. d5 e3 12. c4 f4 stalemate! (Fig.10).

It is very difficult to prove that these two games are the shortest ones - practically for this you need to go through all possible 9- and 11-move games, respectively. However, given that these batches will soon turn one hundred years old, they can be considered record-breaking without any doubt.

Problem 13. *What is the fastest way for a game to end in perpetual check?*

Three moves are enough: 1. f4 e5 2. Kr f2 Q f6 3. Kr g3 Q:f4+4. Kp h3 Qf5+ and so on.

In conclusion, we present two more of Loyd's records, also related to the initial position.

Problem 14. *Black symmetrically repeats White’s moves (as long as this is possible). In what is the least number of moves that White can checkmate the enemy king?*

Checkmate is given already on the fourth move, and in two ways: 1. c4 c5 2. Qa4 Qa5 3. Qc6 Qc3 4. Q: c8 mate; 1. d4 d5 2. Qd3 Qd6 3. Qh3 Qh6 4. Q : c8 mate.

There is such a funny story. Someone came to the chess club and announced that he had found a way not to lose with black. "How?" - they asked him. - “Very simple - repeating the enemy’s moves!” Loyd volunteered to play with the “inventor”, who declared checkmate to him in 4 moves.

Problem 15. *Again, Black repeats White’s moves. Knowing this, in how many moves can White checkmate himself?*

White can get a “reverse” mate already on the eighth move: 1. e4 e5 2. Ke2 Ke7 3. Ke3 Kreb 4. Qf3 Qf6 5. Ne2 Ne7 6. b3 b6 7. Ca3 Ca6 (Fig. 11). 8. Nd4+! ed mat.

Of course, it is difficult to compete with Loyd in ingenuity, but maybe you too will be able to set some kind of record on the chessboard?!
