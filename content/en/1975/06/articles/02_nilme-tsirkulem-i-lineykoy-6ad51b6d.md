It is easy to point out construction problems that have no solution. It is impossible, for example, to draw a tangent from a given point to a given circle if the point lies inside the circle. Much more interesting is the case when the figure to be constructed depends on the existence of useful tools. Many of you have probably heard that two village problems: the trisection of an angle and the trisection of a cube are unsolvable with a compass and a ruler. What these words mean, what constructions can and cannot be made with a circumference and a ruler - that’s what this article will talk about.

1. Solution of the problem of trisection of an angle with a compass and a ruler

Let segment AB at figure 1 be equal to the radius of the circle: AB = OB = OC = OD.

Then $\Rightarrow BAO = \Rightarrow BOA = \alpha$, $\Rightarrow CBO = \Rightarrow BCO = 2\alpha$ ($\Rightarrow CBO =$ outer corner $\Delta ABO$), $\Rightarrow COD = 3\alpha$ (like outer corner $\Delta ACO$). This leads to the following simple method of dividing an arbitrary angle into three equal parts.

Let's make two marks A and B on the ruler. With radius AB we describe a circle with the center at the vertex O of the given angle (which needs to be divided into three equal parts). We denote the points of intersection of it with the sides of the angle by C and D. We will move the ruler so that its edge always passes through point C, and point A slides along straight line OD until point B falls on the circle. When this happens, we get the arrangement depicted at figure 1, so that $\Rightarrow BAO$ ends up being equal to a third of $\Rightarrow COD$.

The described construction may give the impression that the problem of trisection of an angle (dividing an angle into three equal parts) is solved using a compass and a ruler. To explain what does not eliminate us in this construction, we will discuss the solution to the following physical problem.

How can you change its height while on the roof of a multi-story building using a barometer and a clock?

Solution. It is necessary to drop the barometer from the roof, detect the time of fall t and calculate the height of the house h using the formula $h = \frac{gt^2}{2}$, where g is the acceleration of gravity.

2. What are you allowed to do with a compass and ruler?

A ruler allows you to draw straight lines passing through two given (or previously constructed) points, and a compass - circles with a given (or constructed) center and radius *).

At first glance, the answer is clear and unambiguous. In any case, he allows you to make marks on the ruler and move it along the plane. Thus, it allows the construction from point 1 or, for example, the following construction of a tangent to the circle $S$ passing through the point $A$ (And outside $S$): we will move the ruler so that it always touches $S$; when its edge passes through the point $A$, we draw a tangent.

However, there are pitfalls here too. What does it mean, for example, that a straight line is drawn? Does this mean that the data are all points?

To answer this question, consider the problem of moving a cube.

Knowing the edge of a cube, construct the edge of another cube with twice the volume.

We are given the segment $AB$ (edge ​​of the cube). Let us draw a straight line through $A$ and $B$. If we assume that all points are given, then a point $C$ is also given such that $AC=AB^3\sqrt{2}$ and the exclusive edge $AC$ are “constructed”. From the analyzed example it is clear that further clarification is needed. It is necessary to clearly clarify that we will familiarize ourselves with the words “given a point” and “given a straight line.”

3. Elementary constructions

Let us list which construction elements can be drawn with a compass and ruler when a finite number of straight lines and circles are drawn on the plane and the number of points is marked.

$\cdot$ We have already written about the construction of compasses (see «Kvant», 1974, No. 10).

$\alpha$. If points $A$ and $B$ are marked (and $A$ does not coincide with $B$), then you can draw a straight line $AB$.

$\beta$. If the points $O$, $A$ and $B$ are marked (and $A$ does not coincide with $B$), then you can draw a circle with center $O$ and radius $AB$.

$\gamma$. If two circles are drawn (or two straight lines, or a straight line and a circle) and if they intersect or touch (but do not coincide!), then you can mark any of their common points.

4. How to formulate construction tasks?

Let a finite number of straight lines and circles be drawn on a plane and a finite number of points marked - the collection of these lines and points will be called a configuration. Let's say that the point $A$ can be constructed (with a compass and straightedge) from a given configuration if a finite number of construction elements $\alpha$, $\beta$ and $\gamma$ can be made, so that as a result the point $A$ is marked.

Any geometric construction problem can be formulated like this.

Some configuration is given. Based on it, construct points $A_1,...,A_k$ that satisfy certain conditions.

Examples. A. The problem “to construct a straight line equidistant from two given points and a given circle” is formulated in these terms as follows.

Initial configuration: circle $S$ is drawn and points $A$ and $B$ are marked. Construct points $A_1$ and $A_2$ so that $A_1$ does not coincide with $A_2$ and line $A_1A_2$ is equidistant from $A$, $B$ and $S$.

V. Zadchacha “Dan $\Delta ABC$. Construct an external circle tangent to side $BC$” is formulated as follows.

Let us formulate the main theorem (its proof will be given in Section 8), which shows which algebraic actions correspond to the elementary constructions $\alpha$, $\beta$ and $\gamma$.

Definition: We will call adding, subtracting, multiplying and dividing numbers, as well as taking the square root of a positive number *basic operations*.

Theorem 1. *If the initial configuration has parameters $p_1, \ldots, p_n$, then point $(x, y)$ can be constructed if and only if $x$ and $y$ are expressed in terms of $p_1, \ldots, p_n$ using a finite number of basic actions.*

Remark: Sometimes it is convenient to formulate construction problems like this.

Given segments with lengths $d_1, \ldots, d_n$. Construct a segment of length $x$. To such problems it is more convenient to apply not Theorem 1, but the following theorem.

Theorem 1'. *Let there be segments whose lengths are expressed by numbers $d_1, \ldots, d_n$, and let there be a unit segment among them. Then, using a compass and a ruler, one can construct a segment of length $x$ (that is, construct two points at a distance $x$) if and only if $x$ is expressed through $d_1, \ldots, d_n$ using a finite number of basic operations.*

Theorem 2. *Let the cubic equation $x^3 + ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ with rational coefficients $a$, $b$ and $c$ have no rational roots, and let $x_0$ be its root. Then $x_0$ cannot be expressed in terms of integers using a finite number of basic operations.*

Therefore, based on a unit segment, it is impossible to construct an edge of a cube with a volume of 2 using compasses and a ruler.

9. Proof of Theorem 2

Let's divide the basic operations into simple ones - complex ones, subtraction, multiplication and division, and complex ones - extracting the square root.

Let there be a certain set of numbers. Let's consider all the numbers that can be obtained from them using a finite number of simple operations.

Definition. If we denote the original set by $M$, then the resulting set $P$ is called the field generated by $M$.

It is clear that if the numbers $z$ and $t$ belong to the field $P$, then the numbers $z + t$, $z - t$, $zt$ and $z/t$ (with $t \neq 0$) also belong to $P$.

Examples. A. The set $M$, consisting of one number 1, generates a field of rational numbers.

B. The set $M$, consisting of all rational numbers and the number $\sqrt{2}$, generates a field $P$, which includes all numbers of the form $a + b\sqrt{2}$ (where $a$ and $b$ are rational) and only them. Check this by paying special attention to the division. Note that the same field $P$ is generated by two numbers: 1 and $\sqrt{2}$.

Exercise 10. Does $\sqrt{3}$ belong to field $P$ from Example B?

Let $x$ be some number that can be expressed in terms of 1 using a finite number of base actions. This means that there are numbers $x_1, \ldots, x_n$, where $x_1 = 1$, $x_n = x$, and each number $x_k$ ($1 < k \leq n$) is expressed through the previous ones using one basic action. For example, for $x = \sqrt{2} - 5\sqrt{3}$ you can take $x_1 = 1$, $x_2 = 1 + 1 = 2$, $x_3 = \sqrt{2}$, $x_4 = 1 + 2 = 3$, $x_5 = \sqrt{3}$, $x_6 = 2 + 3 = 5$, $x_7 = 5\sqrt{3}$, $x_8 = \sqrt{2} - 5\sqrt{3} = x$.

Let's construct a chain of fields $P_1, \ldots, P_n$, where $P_k$ is the field generated by the set of numbers $x_1, \ldots, x_k$. In this chain, for each field $P_k$ there are two possibilities:

1. If $x_k$ belongs to $P_{k-1}$, then $P_k$ is the same as $P_{k-1}$.
2. If $x_k$ does not belong to $P_{k-1}$, then $P_k$ is generated by a set, which is obtained by adding the number $x_k$ to the field $P_{k-1}$ (in this case, $x_k^2$ necessarily belongs to $P_{k-1}$).

From the above it is clear that to prove Theorem 2 it is enough to establish the following.

Lemma 1. Let the number $s$ not belong to the field $P$, $s^2$ belong to $P$, and $Q$ be the field generated by the set, which is obtained by adding the number $s$ to $P$. Let the equation

$$x^3 + ax^2 + bx + c = 0$$

with coefficients belonging to $P$ has no roots in the field $P$. Then it has no roots in the field $Q$.

Before proving this lemma, let us describe how the field $Q$ is structured.

Lemma 2. Any number $q$ from the field $Q$

1) represented as $q = p_1 + p_2s$, where $p_1$ and $p_2$ belong to $P$,
2) satisfies the quadratic equation $x^2 + dx + e = 0$ with coefficients $d$ and $e$ belonging to $P$.

Proof of Lemma 2.

1) It is clear that all numbers of the form $p_1 + p_2s$ belong to $Q$. To prove that there are no other numbers in $Q$, it is enough to check that simple operations on numbers of the form $p_1 + p_2s$ again belong to numbers of the same type. This follows from the formula

$$(p_1 + p_2s) \pm (r_1 + r_2s) = (p_1 \pm r_1) + (p_2 \pm r_2)s,$$

$$(p_1 + p_2s) \cdot (r_1 + r_2s) = (p_1r_1 + p_2r_2s^2) + (p_1r_2 + p_2r_1)s$$
