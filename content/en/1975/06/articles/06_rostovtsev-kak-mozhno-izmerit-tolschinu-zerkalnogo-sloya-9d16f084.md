How can I change the thickness of the mirror layer?

N. M. Rostmovtsev

When making mirrors, a thin layer of metal is applied to the polished surface of the glass, which reflects light well. Mirrors made of silver chemically deposited on glass are especially widespread.

But silver, as you know, is an expensive metal. How to control the consumption of silver, that is, change the thickness of its layer on a mirror surface? You will be able to answer the question posed and make changes in the thickness of the mirror layer if you get acquainted with the method proposed by Fizu in 1861. This method is based on the chemical interaction of odor with silver and is still used in practice.

To change the thickness of the mirror layer of silver, a grain of light is placed on it (beam 1 at Fig. 6), then it is partially reflected from the outer surface of the AgI layer (point A, ray 2), partially passes inside the layer, reflected from its second surface, that is, from the boundaries with silver (point B), and comes out (point C, ray 3). The resulting reflected waves (beams 2 and 3) are coherent, so they will interfere. The result of interference depends on the geometric path difference δ of rays 2 and 3, which can be calculated using the formula

$$\delta = AB + BC.$$

If the angle of incidence of light is zero or close to it, then $AB = BC$, so

$$\delta = 2AB = 2d,$$

(1)

where $d$ is the thickness of the “line” at the point of incidence of the light beam. From the edge to the center of the layer, its thickness $d$ gradually increases, and the path difference $\delta$ also increases. When illuminated with monochromatic light (for example, red), illumination maxima (red rings) are observed where the difference in the path of the rays is equal to an integer wavelength of light in the underlying silver. Dark rings (minimum illumination) form where the difference in the path of the rays is a multiple of half

wavelength $\lambda$ in this substance:

$$\delta = (2k-1)\,\frac{\lambda}{2}. \qquad (2)$$

Here $k = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$ is the number of the dark ring. The first is the outermost dark ring.

From relations (1) and (2) it follows that where a dark ring with number $k$ is observed, the thickness of the AgI layer can be calculated using the formula

$$d = (2k-1)\,\frac{\lambda}{4}. \qquad (3)$$

If the last dark ring with number $k_{\max}$ coincides with the edge of the central transparent spot, then the maximum thickness of layer $d_{\max}$ is equal to

$$d_{\max} = (2k_{\max}-1)\,\frac{\lambda}{4}. \qquad (4)$$

In reality, the last dark ring usually does not coincide with the edge of the transparent spot, but is located at some distance from it (as, for example, at Fig. $a$). Therefore, in fact, $d_{\max}$ is slightly larger than the value calculated using formula (4). Let's try to evaluate this inaccuracy. The difference in the thickness of the “lens” in the places corresponding to two adjacent dark rings is equal to half the wavelength. Consequently, the error in determining $d_{\max}$ does not exceed $\lambda/4$. Then, we can assume that $d_{\max}$ is equal to approximately

$$d_{\max} = (2k_{\max} - 1)\,\frac{\lambda}{4} + \frac{\lambda}{4} =$$

$$= k_{\max}\,\frac{\lambda}{2}. \qquad (5)$$

The wavelength of light in a substance $\lambda$ is related to the wavelength of light in a vacuum $\lambda_0$ by the relation $\lambda = \dfrac{\lambda_0}{n}$, where $n$ is the refractive index of the substance. Taking this into account, we write

$$d_{\max} = k_{\max}\,\frac{\lambda_0}{2n}. \qquad (6)$$

It turns out (see the exercise at the end of the article), the thickness $h$ of the silver layer on the mirror is determined by

in a different way with the maximum thickness of silver iodide $d_{\max}$:

$$h = \frac{d_{\max}}{4} = k_{\max}\,\frac{\lambda_0}{8n}. \qquad (7)$$

For silver iodide $n = 2{,}15$. Substituting this value into (7), we obtain a formula for determining the thickness of the silver layer:

$$h = k_{\max}\,\frac{\lambda_0}{17{,}2}.$$

Let the picture depicted at figure $a$ be observed through a red filter (red light wavelength $6{,}2 \cdot 10^{-5}$ cm); Then

$$h = \frac{3 \cdot 6{,}2 \cdot 10^{-5}}{17{,}2} \approx 10^{-5}\ см.$$

If the observation is carried out in white light, then $k_{\max}$ is understood as the number of colored rings, and $\lambda$ is taken equal to $5{,}6 \cdot 10^{-5}$ cm (the wavelength of green light to which our eye is most sensitive).
