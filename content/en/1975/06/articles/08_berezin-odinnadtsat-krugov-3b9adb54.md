Eleven circles

On the fourth page of the cover there are twenty-three circles in a circle. The position of each of them is determined by the points of contact either with the reference circles, or with the reference straight lines, or with each other. The centers of all support circles lie on the vertical diameter, the points of their intersection with this diameter are either the extreme points of the diameter, or the center of the circle, or the middle of the radius.

Equally surrounded circles seem congruent, don't they? But maybe the vision is deceiving us? In any case, using a compass it was not possible to distinguish between them. So what kind of equality holds - exact or approximate?

On figure 1 of figure the parts containing circles that are clearly congestive to the remaining single-link ones have been removed from the cover. Twelve circles have disappeared, the remaining ones have been deleted. Let's trace their radii in the same order. It is clear that if it is possible to prove the conjecture for circles on figure 1, ​​then it can be easily extended to figure from the cover.

Let's check with the yellow circles. Let the radius of the largest of the support circles be equal to R. From figure 2 it is clear that

$$\left(\frac{R}{4}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{R}{2} - r_1\right)^2 = \left(\frac{R}{4} + r_1\right)^2,$$

whence $r_1 = R/6$. Similarly, from figure 3 we obtain the equality

$$\left(\frac{R}{2} + r_2\right)^2 + \left(\frac{R}{2}\right)^2 = (R - r_2)^2,$$

from which it follows that $r_2 = R/6$. Now you can easily prove on your own that $r_3 = R/6$.

But in order to prove that $r_4 = R/6$, Pythogoras’ theorem is not enough; we have to use Stewart's theorem: if in a triangle with rows a, b and c from vertex A to row a a segment of length d (Fig. 4) is drawn, which divides it into segments m and n, mo

$$d^2 a = b^2 m + c^2 n - amn.$$

The proof of Stewart's theorem follows from the equalities $c^2 = d^2 + m^2 - 2mp$ and $b^2 = d^2 + n^2 + 2np$, where p is the projection of the segment d onto the base of the triangle.

The radius of any of the white circles is R/8. To prove this for the tenth circle, we can consider the right triangle highlighted in red at figure 5.

Almost everything has been proven; you can easily do the rest yourself. Or maybe you will find other evidence?

V. N. Berezin
