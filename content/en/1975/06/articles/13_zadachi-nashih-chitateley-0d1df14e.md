⟦folio 42⟧

1. You need to start preparing for the written exam in advance and solve the problems yourself. If the task “doesn’t work out,” you can ask a teacher or friend to help outline a solution plan; after that, the problem still needs to be solved, without using someone else’s premises (and without looking at the solution). In general, you can learn to solve problems only by solving problems.

2. It is better to start not with “complex” problems, but with more specific ones. For example, first solve the basic problems of planimetrines, you will need to solve triangles, try to transform expressions containing trigonometric functions, then move on to solving stereometric problems and solving trigonometric equations, and after that solve more complex problems offered in the advanced exams.

3. It is not necessary to start with task No. 1. Start with the one whose solution plan is clear to you; Having carefully written down her decision, he moves on to the next one.

4. For a complete problem, therefore, draw up a solution plan and write down the order in which the individual steps are performed. This will help you stay on track and save time.

5. For a geometric problem, make a clear and fairly large drawing, if possible, in 2-3 colors. “External” drawings are very useful: section, base, sometimes projection or side face. Don't skimp on the quality of your drawings!

6. Select enough options for your chosen university and one to two weeks before the exam, do a “rehearsal.” If you can’t cope with it on your own in 3-4 hours, then ask a teacher or university consultant to analyze this option, and then “test” with other options.

3. Prove that
$$\left(1 + \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{3} + \dots + \frac{1}{n}\right) + \left(1 + \frac{1}{2} + \dots + \frac{1}{n}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{3} + \dots + \frac{1}{n}\right)^2 + \dots + \left(\frac{1}{n}\right)^2 = 2n.$$
S. L. Manukyan
