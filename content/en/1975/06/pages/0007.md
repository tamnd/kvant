Let us formulate the main theorem (its proof will be given in Section 8), which shows which algebraic actions correspond to the elementary constructions $\alpha$, $\beta$ and $\gamma$.

Definition: We will call adding, subtracting, multiplying and dividing numbers, as well as taking the square root of a positive number *basic operations*.

Theorem 1. *If the initial configuration has parameters $p_1, \ldots, p_n$, then point $(x, y)$ can be constructed if and only if $x$ and $y$ are expressed in terms of $p_1, \ldots, p_n$ using a finite number of basic actions.*

Remark: Sometimes it is convenient to formulate construction problems like this.

Given segments with lengths $d_1, \ldots, d_n$. Construct a segment of length $x$. To such problems it is more convenient to apply not Theorem 1, but the following theorem.

Theorem 1'. *Let there be segments whose lengths are expressed by numbers $d_1, \ldots, d_n$, and let there be a unit segment among them. Then, using a compass and a ruler, one can construct a segment of length $x$ (that is, construct two points at a distance $x$) if and only if $x$ is expressed through $d_1, \ldots, d_n$ using a finite number of basic operations.*

Exercise 4. Derive Theorem 1' from Theorem 1.

Exercise 5. Theorem 1' says that one of the numbers $d_1, \ldots, d_n$ is equal to 1. Show that without this condition Theorem 1' is, generally speaking, false.

**7. Unsolvability of the problems of doubling a cube and trisection of an angle**

According to Theorem 1', the question of the solvability of the problem of doubling a cube (see Section 2) can be formulated as follows.

Given the number 1. Is it possible to express the number $\sqrt[3]{2}$ through it using a finite number of basic actions?

Exercise 6. Prove that $\sqrt[3]{2}$ is an irrational number.

⟦column⟧

The unsolvability of the problem of doubling a cube follows from this exercise and the following general theorem, the proof of which will be given in Section 9.

Theorem 2. *Let the cubic equation $x^3 + ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ with rational coefficients $a$, $b$ and $c$ have no rational roots, and let $x_0$ be its root. Then $x_0$ cannot be expressed in terms of integers using a finite number of basic operations.*

Indeed, as follows from Exercise 6, the equation $x^3 - 2 = 0$ has no rational roots, which means that its root $x_0 = \sqrt[3]{2}$ cannot be expressed in terms of 1 using a finite number of basic operations. Therefore, based on a unit segment, it is impossible to construct an edge of a cube with a volume of 2 using compasses and a ruler.

Let's move on to the problem of trisection of an angle. This, strictly speaking, is not one task, but a whole series: each corner has its own task. Some of them are solvable - for example, the angle at $90°$ can be divided into three equal parts with a compass and ruler. We will show that *in general* the angle trisection problem is *unsolvable*. To do this, it is enough to indicate at least one angle $\varphi$, which cannot be divided into three equal parts. We'll take $\varphi = 30°$.
