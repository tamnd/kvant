As an example, consider once again the decay of a free neutrion. Let an electron and a proton fly apart at an angle of 90°, the kinetic energy of the electron is $E_e = 200$ keV. What is the energy of the proton?

Let's make two equations: the law of conservation of energy

$$E_0 = E_v + E_p + E_e$$

and the law of conservation of momentum (Fig. 4)

$$p^2_v = p_e^2 + p_p^2.$$

Here $E_0 = 780$ keV is the total energy released during the β-decay of a neutrion (see Fig. 2), $E_v$ and $p_v$ are the energy and momentum of the neutrino, and $E_v = p_v c$. The momentum of the electron $p_e$ and its kinetic energy, taking into account the corrections of the theory of relative coupling between each other, are as follows:

$$p_e^2 c^2 = E_e^2 + 2 E_e m_e c^2.$$

Indeed, the momentum of the electron is equal to $p_e = \frac{m_e v}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}}$ (me is the rest mass of the electron), the total energy of the electron is equal to $E = \frac{m_e c^2}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}}$, and the rest energy is equal to $E_\pi = m_e c^2$. Then the kinetic energy of the electron

$$E_e = E - E_\pi = m_e c^2 \left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}} - 1 \right).$$
