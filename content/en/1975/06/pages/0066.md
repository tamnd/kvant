So, we got that the point $A$ will move along the usual epicycloid if $r=r_1$, extended if $r<r_1$, and shortened if $r>r_2$.

Note that the result may be $R<r$ and that the constraint $r_2>r_1$ is not significant.

This problem explains how epicycloids appeared in the Ptolemaic system (2nd century AD) *). In Ptolemy, point $O$ corresponds to the Earth, $A$ to the Sun or planet, $O_1$ is an auxiliary point. Copernicus could not do without epicycloids; only at point $O$ did he have the Sun. Only when considering elliptical orbits were epicycloids no longer required**).

Problem 5'. Let us continue, as in Problem 5, our movement to the movement of the entire plane as a solid plate. The resulting motion will have one of three types depending on $\alpha$. (See the notation in the solution to problem 5.)

Let $\alpha > 1$. Let us define $\widetilde{R}$, $\widetilde{r}$ so that

$$\widetilde{R}-\widetilde{r}=r_2, \quad \frac{\widetilde{R}}{\widetilde{r}}=\alpha, \quad \text{то есть} \quad \widetilde{r}=\frac{r_2}{\alpha-1}.$$

Let us now consider a stationary hoop of radius $\widetilde{R}$ with center $O$, along the inner side of which a circle of radius $\widetilde{r}$ rolls without slipping so that its center $O_1$ moves as stated in the problem statement. It is not difficult to verify that then the point $A$ will also move as required by the condition. This means that at $\alpha>1$ the point $A$ moves along a hypocycloid, shortened or extended depending on whether it is $\widetilde{r}=r_1$, $\widetilde{r}>r_1$ or $\widetilde{r}<r_1$.

For $\alpha<1$ we put $\widetilde{r}=\dfrac{r_2}{1-\alpha}$, $\widetilde{R}=\alpha\widetilde{r}$.

Let us consider a stationary circle of radius $\widetilde{R}$ with center $O$, along the boundary of which a hoop of radius $\widetilde{r}$ rolls without sliding with its inner side, the center of which performs the rotation prescribed by the condition of the problem ($\widetilde{r}>\widetilde{R}$). Then point $A$ will be involved in the desired movement, which means that its trajectory is a pericycloid, shortened or extended.

At $\alpha=1$, the resulting motion of the plane, as is easy to see, will not have instantaneous centers of rotation - points with zero speed (we recommend that you draw your own picture for each case $\alpha<1$, $\alpha=1$, $\alpha>1$). Since point $O_1$ moves along a circle with center $O$, it means that the remaining points move along circles resulting from the indicated shift. Try to prove this result directly.
