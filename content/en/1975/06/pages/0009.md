It remains to prove the converse statement: if the point $(x, y)$ can be constructed, then $x$ and $y$ are expressed through the parameters of the original configuration using basic actions. This statement follows immediately from the following lemma.

Lemma. *Let one of the elementary constructions $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\gamma$ be applied to the configuration with parameters $q_1, \ldots, q_N$. Then the parameters of the resulting configuration are expressed through $q_1, \ldots, q_N$ using basic actions.*

We precede the proof of the lemma with the following exercise.

Exercise 9. Prove that

a) a straight line passing through two different points $(c_1, c_2)$ and $(d_1, d_2)$ is given by the equation

$$x = c_1 \text{ при } c_1 = d_1$$

And

$$y = \frac{c_2 - d_2}{c_1 - d_1}x + \frac{c_1d_2 - c_2d_1}{c_1 - d_1} \text{ при } c_1 \neq d_1,$$

b) the distance between points $(c_1, c_2)$ and $(d_1, d_2)$ is $\sqrt{(c_1-d_1)^2+(c_2-d_2)^2}$,

c) if two lines are given by the equations

$$y = k_1x + b_1 \text{ и } y = k_2x + b_2,$$

then for $k_1 = k_2$ they are either parallel or coincide, and for $k_1 \neq k_2$ they intersect at the point

$$\left(\frac{b_2-b_1}{k_1-k_2},\ \frac{k_1b_2-k_2b_1}{k_1-k_2}\right);$$

lines $y = k_1x + b_1$ and $x = a_2$ intersect at point $(a_2, k_1a_2 + b_1)$; lines $x = a_1$ and $x = a_2$ are either parallel or coincident,

d) a circle with center $(c, d)$ and radius $r$ is given by equation $(x-c)^2 + (y-d)^2 = r^2$.

Proof of the lemma. When constructing $\alpha$, one or two numbers are added to the parameters $q_1, \ldots, q_N$ - the coefficients of the equation $x = c$ or $y = kx + b$, which specifies the drawn line. According to exercise 9a, they are expressed through $q_1, \ldots, q_N$ using the basic actions

When constructing $\beta$, a circle is drawn with center $O$ and radius $CD$. The coordinates of the points $O$, $C$ and $D$ are among the parameters, and the radius is expressed in terms of them using the basic operations of Exercise 9b.

When constructing $\gamma$, three cases are possible.

The first - when the point of intersection of two lines is marked - is dealt with using exercise 9c.

In the second case, let us mark the point $(x_0, y_0)$ of intersection or tangency of the circle $(x-c)^2+(y-d)^2=r^2$ (see exercise 9d) and the line $y = kx + b$. The coordinates $(x_0, y_0)$ satisfy both equations. Substituting $y=kx+b$ into the first equation, we obtain a quadratic equation to determine $x_0$

$$(x-c)^2+(kx+b-d)^2=r^2.$$

If we apply the formula to solve a quadratic equation, then $x_0$ (and therefore $y_0=kx_0+b$) will be expressed in terms of $k, b, c, d$ and $r$ using basic operations. For the line given by equation $x = a$, a similar result is even simpler.

In the third case, the point of intersection or tangency of the circles is marked

$$(x-c)^2+(y-d)^2=r^2,$$
$$(x-c')^2+(y-d')^2=r'^2.$$

Let's open the brackets and subtract one equation from the other. We obtain a linear equation

$$Px+Qy=R,$$

where $P=2(c'-c)$, $Q=2(d'-d)$, $R=r^2-r'^2+c'^2-c^2+d'^2-d^2$. If $Q \neq 0$, then we rewrite it in the form $y=kx+b$ (where $k=-P/Q$, $b==R/Q$), and if $Q=0$, $P \neq 0$, then in the form $x=a$ (where $a=R/P$), and thereby reduce everything to the already analyzed second case; equality $P=Q==0$ is impossible (if $R \neq 0$, the circles do not have common points, if $R = 0$ they coincide).
