What is interest needed for?
A. P. Savin

Is there a lot of salt in sea water? This question can be understood in different ways. For example, how much does salt weigh in a bucket of sea water? To answer the first question, it is enough to know the answer to the second and also find out how many buckets of water are contained in the seas and oceans.

Residents of coastal cities and towns will be able to answer the second question. To do this, just take a bucket of sea water, put it on the fire and heat until all the water has boiled away, and then weigh the salt remaining at the bottom. Is it possible to say that your neighbor will get the same amount? Apparently not. His bucket may be larger or smaller, it may be more or less full, as a result, the neighbor will evaporate a different amount of water, and therefore a different amount of salt will remain.

Thus, our measure of seawater salinity—the number of grams of salt per bucket of water—failed. Let's take another measure - the number of grams of salt per kilogram of solution. To do this, you need to weigh the solution before boiling, and then divide the weight of the resulting salt by the weight of the solution. Let the weight of the solution be 8.4 kcal and the weight of the salt 21 liters. Then we get the answer:

$$\frac{21}{8,4} = \frac{5}{2}$$

grams of salt per kilogram of solution. If the experiment is repeated, then again the result will be almost the same value.

No pouemy the number of grams in a kilogramme, and not centners in a ton or English feet in a pood? Let's count the number of grams in a gram! Then the same answer will be obtained if we count the number of tons of salt per ton of solution or poods per pood.

So, since a kilogram contains 1000 grams, the answer will be 1000 times less:

$$\frac{5}{2000} = \frac{1}{400}$$

A suitable measure has been obtained, but the recording... Tell me which number is greater: $\frac{11}{1002}$ or $\frac{12}{1090}$? You can’t repent right away, you need to count. Much easier

compare decimals! The fraction $0,01097$ is smaller than $0,01101$, because the number of units, tenths and hundredths is the same, and the number of thousandths is greater for the second. Comfortable? Certainly.

Well, let's write the result not as an ordinary fraction, but as a decimal fraction. And then...

Wait, the impatient reader will say, why so much wisdom for the sake of some sea water. Take it and taste it - salty or not so salty. Well, I’ll answer, is it necessary to know exactly the metal content in ore, fat in milk, chemical substances in medicine?.. That’s it. But the task is the same.

So, we agreed to write the answer as a decimal fraction. And with what accuracy? With a pencil and paper we can divide even to parts per billion, but where do the numbers themselves come from? If the store scale shows $520\ г$, then in reality the item may weigh either 515 or 524 grams. And two or three hundred years ago, the accuracy of scales was even less. Therefore, only the first one or two digits could be considered correct, and therefore it made sense to consider the amount of content of one substance in another with an accuracy of the first two digits: $0,27$; $0,64$; $0,37$, etc., that is, 27 hundredths, 64 hundredths, 37 hundredths.

So we came to percentages, because “percentage” is translated from Latin as a hundredth part. A special notation $27\%$, $64\%$, $37\%$ was also invented. The sign $\%$ is said to have arisen due to a typist's mistake, whose type broke, resulting in this bizarre sign, which was then recognized by the whole world.

Recording ratios has become more convenient, zero and comma have disappeared, and the symbol $\%$ immediately indicates that we have a relative value, and not grams, liters, rubles or meters.

Since then, counting has been carried out in the decimal system. In Europe, decimal fractions appeared 1000 years later, they were introduced by the Belgian scientist Simon Stevin. In 1584, he first published a table of percentages.

The introduction of percentages turned out to be convenient not only for estimating the content of one substance in another. Changes in the production of goods, monetary income began to be measured as a percentage... What is not measured as a percentage, even for poor students at school!

Over time, people learned to extract from a substance its components, which constitute thousandths of the weight of the substance itself. Then, in order not to enter zeros and commas, that is, not to write $0,6\%$, they introduced a new value - “ppm” - a thousandth, which was designated as follows: $^0/_{00}$, and instead of $0,6\%$ they began to write $6^0/_{00}$. However, this value was adopted only in those areas of science and technology where they deal with small quantities, and the need and the emerging opportunity to count more accurately led to the fact that counting began to be carried out to tenths and hundredths of a percent. You can often see entries like $27,4\%$ in technical literature and on the pages of newspapers; $6,35\%$. Expressing values ​​as percentages has become familiar to everyone, but at a class meeting it is better to say: “Seryozha Fedotov has become a poor student” than “The number of poor students in our class has increased by $3,3\%$ over the past six months.”

And now some problems on percentages.

**1.** The watermelon weighed $20\ кг$ and had a dry matter content of $1\%$. After some time, the watermelon dried out, and the dry matter began to amount to $2\%$. How much did the watermelon weigh?

**2.** The multiplicand was increased by $10\%$, and the multiplier was decreased by $10\%$. How has the work changed?

**3.** The price of the product was reduced by $10\%$, and then by another $10\%$. Would it become cheaper if its price were immediately reduced by $20\%$?
