MASS AND ENERGY
IN THEORY
RELATIVITY

What is said in the epigraph applies not only to mathematics, but also to physics. If about a hundred years ago we had turned to physics with the question whether mass and energy are a manifestation of the same property of physical bodies, then in response he would have only shrugged his shoulders, as if they were familiar:
“What pages of thought can sometimes come to mind!”
Out of condescension towards our lack of education, he could explain to us that mass is a measure of the inertia of a body, and energy determines the body’s ability to do work. This is a perfect development because energy can be imparted to a body without changing its mass. At the same time, he could draw on direct experience, recall that the mass of a cold piece of steel does not change when it is heated and melted. Indeed, even the most accurate measurements do not detect any changes in esa when the body heats up. This was known to everyone, and therefore years ago we, in all likelihood, would not have asked such a question.

So, as centuries have passed, mass is an integral property of the body. A body can have all its energy, but not its mass. You can imagine a body at rest in space far from other bodies. then its temperature is close to absolute zero, and there are no external fields, including the gravitational field, around it. Such a body cannot do work *), that is, its energy is zero, but its inertia remains the same as on Earth.

Finally, a physicist centuries later would have added that energy cannot be considered a property at all; it is present only in the body itself. Thus, the kinetic energy in the room is equal to zero in the coordinate system associated with the Earth, but in the coordinate system at rest relative to the Sun, it is many times greater than the energy of the flying projectile (relative to the Earth). Mass is determined only by the property of the body itself.

This would have been the answer to the question posed before the emergence of the theory of relative mathematics. Now we know that the mechanics of the 19th century, which is understandably called Newtonian, classical or non-relativistic mechanics, applies only when the speed of the body is small compared to the speed of light. What is the situation like in relativistic mechanics, where there is no such limitation?

*) Strictly speaking, it is also required that no chemical reactions occur. Nuclear reactions have not been known to physicists for centuries.

Before answering this question, let's see what can be said about the inertial mass in non-relativistic mechanics. From the school physics course we know that:

1) the product of mass and the acceleration of a body is equal to the force acting on it;
2) the product of mass and velocity of a body is equal to its momentum;
3) body weight does not depend on the choice of coordinate system;
4) the mass of the isolated body is conserved.

From point 3 it follows that mass should not depend on speed. Indeed, the speed of the same body in two coordinate systems moving relative to each other is different. If mass depended on speed, its magnitude would have different values ​​in these coordinate systems. The last point refers to the well-known law of conservation of mass. The mass of an isolated body, which is not acted upon by external forces, cannot change as a result of any internal processes occurring in it (for example, the effect of a change in the relative speed of movement of its part arising due to their interaction).

If the mass of a body does not depend on speed, it will not change under the action of external forces accelerating the body. Thus, the use of paragraph 3 allows us to formulate a law of conservation of mass that is broader than that assumed in paragraph 4. The two functional properties given in paragraphs 3 and 4 usually mass in Newtonian mechanics turns out to be the most important characteristic of a physical body. It is due to these properties that mass was considered a measure of the amount of matter contained in a body.

In relativistic mechanics there are two concepts: relativistic mass ($m$) and rest mass ($m_0$), which are related to each other by the following relation:

$$m = \frac{m_0}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}},$$

where $v$ is the speed of the body, $c$ is the speed of light. Momentum $\mathbf{p}$ is represented as the product of relativistic mass and body speed

$$\mathbf{p} = m\mathbf{v},$$

and energy is in the form of the product

$$E = mc^2.$$

As in Newtonian mechanics, if no external forces act on a body, then the momentum $\mathbf{p}$ and energy $E$, and therefore the relativistic mass $m$ of this body remain unchanged.

Let us now determine what quantity should be called mass in relativistic mechanics. If the momentum of the body $\mathbf{p}$ for a short period of time $\Delta t$ has changed by values ​​$\Delta \mathbf{p}$, then according to the laws of Newtonian mechanics the ratio of $\Delta \mathbf{p}$ to $\Delta t$ is equal to the acting force. This law remains true in relativistic mechanics:

$$\mathbf{F} = \frac{\Delta \mathbf{p}}{\Delta t} = \frac{\Delta (m\mathbf{v})}{\Delta t}.$$

However, according to formula (1), the value $m$ depends on the speed and changes over time $\Delta t$. Therefore, it cannot be derived from under the sign $\Delta$, that is, $\Delta (m\mathbf{v}) \neq m\Delta \mathbf{v}$. Therefore, from the law of motion described by equation (4), it is impossible to conclude that the force and acceleration vectors are proportional. Consequently, the question of the properties contained in paragraph 1 disappears.

As can be seen from expression (2), the relativistic mass has property 2 of the Newtonian mass. In addition, from the law of conservation of energy it follows that in a system isolated from the action of external forces, the relativistic mass is conserved. Consequently, it also has the properties of 4 Newtonian mass.

Unfortunately, this is where the analogies end, since $m$ depends on the speed and, accordingly, on the choice of coordinate system. Thus, in this respect, relativistic mass is more similar to energy than to Newtonian mass: it is not a characteristic that depends only on the body under study.

In contrast to the relativistic mass $m$, the rest mass $m_0$ determines the inertia of the body when the speed of the body $v$ is zero. It follows that $m_0$ does not change when moving to a moving coordinate system. Like the Newtonian mass, the rest mass is determined only by the body itself and does not depend on the coordinate system in which this body is considered. However, rest mass is not equal to the ratio of the absolute value of momentum $p$ to the absolute value of the body's velocity $v$ and is not a coefficient of proportionality between force and acceleration.

But the main difference between the Newtonian mass and the rest mass of relativistic mechanics is that the latter does not obey the conservation law. As an example, we can point out the process of annihilation of an electron and a positron, in which these two particles turn into $\gamma$-quanta of the electromagnetic field (photons). As a result of annihilation, the electron and positron disappear, and electromagnetic waves appear in their place. Momentum $\mathbf{p}$ and energy $E$ are conserved in such a system. Thus, the total relativistic mass of the system $m$ must also be conserved. At the same time, the rest mass of the system changes, since instead of two particles (electron and positron) with a rest mass different from zero, $\gamma$-quanta appear, the rest mass of which is equal to zero.

Another example is the process of fission of uranium or plutonium nuclei, which is accompanied by the formation of lighter nuclei with a lower total rest mass.

So, the rest mass is also significantly different from the Newtonian mass. Strictly speaking, in relativistic mechanics there is no specific concept at all that could be characterized by properties similar to the properties of “ordinary” mass. The role played by this quantity in classical mechanics is distributed between two quantities, each of which can, essentially, with equal right be considered the “successor” of Newtonian mass.

It is precisely in such situations that the reason for the difficulty of understanding new theories often lies. By attributing correspondence with objective reality to the basic concepts and terms of the theory, we naturally expect from the further development of science only a clarification of these concepts. In reality, the new theory sometimes treats the “realities” of the old one very disrespectfully. Essentially, each new theory redefines the arsenal of concepts and terms with which it intends to describe reality. The analogy between the terms of different physical theories is often very incomplete, and the use of the same names can easily be misleading.

A similar situation arises with the term “kinetic energy”. Nonrelativistic mechanics knew only two types of energy - potential and kinetic. Kinetic energy has the following two important properties:

a) it becomes zero when the speed of the body is zero;

b) if no external forces act on the body, the energy remains unchanged.

In relativistic mechanics there is no quantity that would have these two properties simultaneously. The energy $E$, defined by equality (3), has property b), but does not have property a). At $v = 0$

So far we have not talked about unification, but rather about the “splitting” of concepts. Now the time has come to justify the words spoken in the epigraph.

Let us consider the question of the relationship between mass and energy. Taking into account the above, it is necessary to clarify what mass and what energy we are talking about. In what follows, unless a special reservation is made, we will understand by mass the relativistic mass $m$, and by energy the total energy of a free body $E$. Then from relation (3) it follows that they differ only in the factor $c^2$, and this factor is a world constant, that is, a quantity that can never change under any conditions. In this case, any statement relating to mass automatically refers to energy and vice versa. For example, any increase or decrease in the energy of a body, say by heating and cooling or by radiation,

should lead to a corresponding change in its mass*). On the other hand, any form of energy, including electromagnetic radiation, is inert.

The fact that mass and energy are measured in different ways (for example, mass by weighing**, and energy in a calorimeter) cannot, of course, be considered an objection to the coincidence of the physical meaning of these concepts. After all, we constantly encounter situations where the same quantity can be measured in several different ways. Only theory can definitively decide whether two different experimental procedures measure the same quantity or whether they provide information about different quantities.

The difference in units in which mass and energy are expressed is also unimportant. Thus, body temperature is usually expressed in degrees, but it is often expressed in ergs or electronvolts (energy units for measuring temperature are widely used in thermonuclear research). The difference in units does not mean a difference in the physical meaning of the quantities.

Despite everything said above, two terms will remain in our language for a long time, perhaps forever: energy and mass. From the point of view of relativistic mechanics, it would be more expedient to use one name to denote the quantities that are meant here. However, spoken language is formed on the basis of everyday experience, and there are no relativistic effects in it.

The total energy, or relativistic mass, determines only the upper limit of the work that a given body can perform. In fact, in the vast majority of cases, only a tiny fraction of this energy can be converted into work. This is true even for nuclear reactions. The only exception is the process of annihilation of externality and anti-procession, in which the energy contained in externality is completely freed. But annihilation is so far carried out only with elementary particles.

From expression (5) it is clear that between rest energy and rest mass there is the same correspondence as between $E$ and $m$, that is, these concepts coincide. But the differences between the rest energy $E_0$ and the total energy (or, accordingly, between the rest mass and the relativistic mass) are significant. When conditions are determined, rest energy can transform into other types of energy. With this process in mind, people sometimes talk about the conversion of mass into energy. This is an unsuccessful expression, if only because it remains unclear what mass and what energy we are talking about. Only in Newtonian mechanics was it possible to speak unambiguously about mass without adding anything to this term.

In conclusion, let us turn once again to formulas (1), (3) and (6). The question inevitably arises: why does the speed of propagation of electromagnetic waves play such an important role in purely mechanical formulas? The fact is that in our world there is an abscissa limiting speed equal to $c$, the value of which is the same in all coordinate systems. Naturally, this fact cannot but have a direct relation to the laws of motion of physical bodies, to the laws of mechanics. That is why the quantity $c$ is included in all formulas of relativistic mechanics. By chance it turns out to be at the same speed. The limiting speed $c$ is called the speed of light according to purely historical principles: simplicity because it was first encountered in optics.
