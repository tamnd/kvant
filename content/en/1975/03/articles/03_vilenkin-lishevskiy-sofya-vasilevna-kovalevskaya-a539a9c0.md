## SOFIA VASILIEVNA KOVALEVSKAYA
N.Ya. Vilenkin
V.P. Lishevsky

(on the 125th anniversary of his birth)

**First Prize**

In 1888, the Paris Academy of Sciences awarded a prize for the best scientific work devoted to the motion of a rigid body having one fixed point. This problem was also called the problem of the movement of a top - after all, all points of a rapidly rotating top are in motion, with the exception of the end of the tip with which the top touches the floor. Since ancient times, tops (or, as they are also called, spinning tops) have been children's favorite toys. But they also attracted the attention of reputable scientists - the properties of rotating bodies were too amazing. It was not clear why the axis of a rapidly rotating top itself rotates slowly. What also seemed surprising was the desire of the top axis to maintain its direction when external forces acted on the top, and the change in the direction of the axis went in a direction different from the direction of this force. Interest in the movement of the top was not idle curiosity. Explanations for the properties of this movement were awaited by astronomers who were interested in such huge tops as planets and stars, and by gunsmiths who had long noticed that bullets and shells hit the target more accurately if they are given not only

⟦column⟧

forward, but also rotational movement, technicians who developed new models of bicycles, and many others.

Until the beginning of the 17th century, scientists, speaking about the properties of tops, gave only explanations similar to the words of Moliere’s character, who claimed that morphine puts you to sleep because it contains a soporific power. But after the work of G. Galileo, who explained the laws of falling bodies, and H. Huygens, who was interested in the movement of a pendulum, a new branch of mechanics arose - dynamics. One of her tasks was to explain the mysterious properties of the top. At the end of the 17th century, I. Newton and G. Leibniz, having created differential and integral calculus, gave into the hands of scientists the “golden key”, which made it possible to reduce problems of dynamics to the solution of equations containing the desired functions and their derivatives - the so-called *differential equations*. The power of the new calculus methods was tested on problems of celestial mechanics: with their help, I. Newton was able to explain Keplerian laws of planetary motion, and his followers were able to predict the time of return of one of the brightest comets - Halley's Comet.

Naturally, scientists tried to apply new methods to study the movement of the voluk. To do this, they had to introduce a number of new concepts - the moment of inertia, which played the same role in explaining rotational movements as the mass of a body in explaining translational motion, the ellipson of inertia, which shows the change in the moment of inertia when the axis of rotation changes, etc. Thus, a member of the St. Petersburg Academy of Sciences, one of the greatest mathematicians of the 18th century, L. Euler studied the movement of a body around the center of gravity (Fig. 1). This was of great importance, as it made it possible to explain the reason for the so-called “preliminary equivalence” associated with a change in the axis of rotation of the globe, the movement of rotating gun shells, etc. Euler’s research was supplemented by the French scientist L. Poinsot. After this, wolves became a favorite model to which physicists resorted, trying to explain certain phenomena. Further, J. Maxwell, strictly the theory of electromagnetic phenomena, resorted to mechanical models, in which wolves placed at each point in space played a larger role.

But the Euler-Poinsot theory could not explain the movement of the wolf, which kids love to play with - its fulcrum is not at the center of gravity. A die of this type has the shape of a body of rotation, and the fulcrum is located on the axis of symmetry (Fig. 2). The problem of the movement of a wolf of this shape was solved by J. Lagrange, who outlined his solution in the book “Analytical Mechanics”; This book became famous not only for the depth of its presentation, but also for the fact that it did not contain a single drawing - Lagrange considered mechanics to be the science of solving differential equations and did not resort to visual representations. Lagrange's solution was developed and improved by S. Poisson.

After Euler, Lagrange, Poinsot and Poisson, many scientists tried to find at least one more form of the wolf, in which the solution to the equation of its motion can be reduced to calculating integrals. But we were unable to move forward a single step. This was especially surprising, because just in the 19th century, in the works of K. Gauss, M. V. Ostrogradsky, C. Jacobi, O. Cauchy, B. Riemann and many others, new mathematical methods were created that made it possible to solve the most difficult problems of mathematical physics. And to gain a deeper understanding of the wolf’s movement was also necessary for practice. Although in those days neither ships, nor planes, nor rockets could use gyroscopic devices, it was clear from a technical standpoint that the time of gyroscopes was just around the corner, and there was nothing more practical than a good theory.

Therefore, it should be recognized that the choice of the topic for the competition by the Paris Academy of Sciences was successful. Having studied the submitted manuscripts, the jury recognized the best work under the motto: “Say what you know, do what you must, be what will be.” It contained a solution to the problem of the motion of an asymmetrical top, the center of gravity of which lies on the equatorial plane of the ellipsoid of inertia, the lengths of the axes of which are subject to the condition $A = B = 2C$ (Fig. 3). The author of the work showed not only great mathematical talent, but also extraordinary erudition: the work used the latest achievements of mathematics of that time - the theory of functions of a complex variable, hyperelliptic integrals, etc. The jury members liked the research so much that they decided to increase the prize amount from 3,000 francs to 5,000 francs. When they opened the envelope with the author's name, it unexpectedly turned out that the best work was written by the only woman who held the position of professor of mathematics at that time - Sofya Vasilievna Kovalevskaya.

## Childhood and youth

Long and difficult was Sofia Vasilievna’s path to this triumph, which immediately placed her above all the women who had studied mathematics before her - the Greek Hypatia (5th century AD), who lectured on philosophy and mathematics, and the Frenchwoman Emilia du Chatelet, who translated Newton’s Principia into French at the beginning of the 18th century, and the Italian Maria Gaetana Agnesi, in whose honor one of the curves was called the "Agnesi curl".

Sofya Vasilievna was born in Moscow one hundred and twenty-five years ago on January 3 (15), 1850. Her father is General V.V. Korvin-Krukovsky, head of the Moscow arsenal,

⟦column⟧

descended from the Hungarian king Matthew Corvinus. Mother, Elizaveta Fedorovna, came from a scientific family - her father F. F. Schubert was an honorary member of the Academy of Sciences, and her grandfather, academician F. I. Schubert, was a famous astronomer and mathematician.

In 1858, Vasily Vasilyevich Korvin-Krukovsky was dismissed with the rank of lieutenant general and moved with his family to his estate Palibino, Nevelsky district, Vitebsk province. He educated his children at home. Sonya's mentors were the Englishwoman M. F. Smith and home teacher I. I. Malevich. But the girl’s interest in mathematics appeared under the influence of her uncle, Pyotr Vasilyevich Korvin-Krukovsky. “From him, for example, I heard for the first time about the squaring of the circle, about the asymptotes to which the curve is constantly approaching without ever reaching them, and about many other things of the same kind” *), Sofya Vasilievna later recalled.

In addition to conversations with her uncle, there was one more circumstance, quite curious, that aroused Sonya’s interest in mathematics. This is how S.V. Kovalevskaya described this episode in her memoirs.

“When we moved to live in the village, the whole house had to be redecorated and all the rooms covered with new wallpaper. But... there wasn't enough wallpaper for one of our children's rooms... This abused room remained for many years with one side covered with paper. But, by a happy coincidence, the sheets of lithographed Ostrogradsky’s lectures on differential and integral calculus, acquired by my father in his youth, were used for this preliminary pasting.

These sheets, covered with incomprehensible formulas, soon attracted my attention. I remember how, as a child, I spent whole hours in front of this tanish wall, trying to make out at least individual phrases and find the order in which the sheets should follow each other. From long, daily contemplation, the appearance of many of the formulas remains in my memory, and the text itself left a deep imprint on my brain, although at the very moment of reading it remained incomprehensible to me.

When many years later, as a fifteen-year-old girl, I took my first lesson in differential calculus from a famous teacher in St. Petersburg, Alexander Nikolaevich Strannolyubsky, he was surprised at how quickly I grasped and internalized the concepts of limit and arbitrary, as if I knew them in advance.”

Sonya's passion for mathematics led to conflict in the family. General Korvin-Krukovsky did not at all want his daughter to become a “bluestocking” or, even worse, a short-haired student, about whom they said that “they don’t believe in God and cut live frogs.” Sonya often had to study mathematics in secret. When she went to bed, she put Bourdon’s Course in Algebra, which she had received from Malevich, under her pillow, and when everyone fell asleep, she read it by the light of the stove almost until the morning.

The Korvin-Krukovsky estate’s companion, physics professor N. N. Tyrtov, helped Sonina’s studies. One day, while visiting his neighbors, he heard from Sonya that she had read his elementary physics textbook and understood everything there. Tyrtov didn’t believe her at first. But, after talking with the girls, he was so surprised by her ability that he went to the general and began to convince him of the need to teach the girls in a very server-like manner. Tyrtov even compared Sonya to Pascal, who as a child himself derived many theorems

Euclidean geometry. And the general agreed that Sonya should continue her studies in mathematics. It began to be taught by the mathematician A. N. Strannolyubsky. During one winter, she studied analytical geometry, differential and integral calculus with him.

But it wasn’t only Sony who caused trouble for the venerable general. Unexpectedly, he intercepted a letter sent to his eldest daughter Anna. Petersburg writer and journalist Fyodor Mikhailovich Dostoevsky, had recently returned to the capital after many years of catalog work, to which he was sentenced for participating in Petrashevsky’s decision circle. The letter praised the literary merits of the story written by Anna and sent to the magazine headed by Dostoevsky. The scanial was incredible - the daughter of respected parents secretly corresponds with a stranger, and even yesterday’s catalog director!

It became clear to Anna and Sonya that in order to further develop their abilities they must break free from parental care. The most common way of emancipation (liberation) of women at that time was a fictitious marriage, after which the fictitious husbands presented their wives with complete freedom. In the fall of 1868, Sonya entered into a fictitious marriage with the future outstanding palenotologist Vladimir Onufrievitch Kovalevsky (1842-1883) and the following year she left with him and her older sister Anna to Germany to continue her education - in Russia, access to universities was closed to women.

Young Ph.D.

At first, Sofya Vasilievna settled in Heidelberg, where the local university gave lectures to prominent natural scientists Kirchhoff, Dubya-Reymond and Helmogloa.

⟦folio 7⟧

But she found the lectures of Professor Koenigsberger, a student of the greatest German mathematician of that time, Karl Weierstrass, even more interesting. It was not in Sofia Vasilievna’s character to receive knowledge second-hand, and she decided to learn from Weierstrass himself.

These were the heyday of Weierstrass's scientific and pedagogical activities. The deep and systematic development of the ideas he laid down in his first work led to results of the greatest importance. Weierstrass's main works were devoted to the theory of elliptic integrals, which was created and developed by Abel, Jacobi and other mathematicians of the previous generation. Abel also noticed that some of the elliptic functions can be written as a ratio of two power series, the sums of which exist for any value of the argument, that is, in the form

$$\frac{a_0 + a_1(x-x_0) + \ldots + a_n(x-x_0)^n + \ldots}{b_0 + b_1(x-x_0) + \ldots + b_n(x-x_0)^n + \ldots}.$$

Further development of this idea required a systematic and in-depth study of power series and the functions represented by such series.

Mathematicians encountered power series in the 17th century. It turned out that their theory contains secrets related to the infinity of the number of terms, and careless handling of infinite sums leads to misunderstandings, errors, and paradoxical results. Only after the work of Cauchy and Abel did infinite power series become a reliable tool in the hands of mathematicians.

Weierstrass studied the properties of elliptic and even more general functions using power series. At the same time, he studied the properties of such series not only for real, but also for complex values ​​of the argument. As a result of these studies, one of the

⟦column⟧

remarkable chapters of mathematics - the theory of functions of a complex variable, which now finds important applications in various fields of human activity: aero- and hydrodynamics, the theory of elasticity, cartography, etc. Weierstrass’s approach to the theory of analytic functions was characterized by strict logic. Weierstrass believed that the essence of mathematical knowledge lies in the absolute completeness of its justification. The brilliant works of Riemann, who sometimes replaced strict logic with a reference to clarity, were deeply alien to Weierstrass. At the same time, he well understood the practical significance of mathematical research and believed that his research on elliptic functions would also play a role in applications of mathematics.

Weierstrass's lectures on the theory of analytic and elliptic functions attracted crowds of listeners. After all, only at these lectures could one become acquainted with his ideas - the great mathematician had a strange aversion to printing ink and did not allow his lectures not only to be printed, but even lithographed - they could only be rewritten by hand. Sofya Vasilievna decided to become a student at Berlin University. But the rules in the capital of Prussia were much more reactionary than in the relatively liberal Heidelberg - women were not allowed to attend lectures, even as auditors. Therefore, Sofya Vasilievna had to decide on an extreme step - to turn directly to Weierstrass with a request to give her private mathematics lessons.

When Kovalevskaya came to Weierstrass’s apartment, to check how ready she was for mathematics, Weierstrass gave her several difficult problems and asked her to think about them in her spare time. Probably, in the depths of his soul, he believed that he would never again see the young Russian who converted -

coming to him with such a strange request. But how great was his amazement when exactly a week later she brought him solutions to all the problems. Weierstrass was even more amazed at the impeccably logical and precise justification of all decisions. Like himself, Sofya Vasilievna put logic first. This time the famous mathematician looked more closely at his visitor. He saw an inspired face, which vividly reflected the joyful consciousness of overcome difficulties, short-cropped dark brown hair, large gray-green eyes, in which one could read extraordinary intelligence and will.

Having received a positive review of Sofya Vasilievna from Koenigsberger, Weierstrass agreed to study with her. Since she was not allowed to attend the University of Berlin, classes had to be conducted at home. This joint work lasted four years.

Weierstrass's letters to his student have been preserved, which tell how these classes went, how the broad horizons of the new theory of functions and its applications were revealed to the young student, how problems for independent reflection appeared, and how step by step she overcame the difficulties that arose.

Weierstrass posed three problems to Sofia Vasilievna. The first of them concerned the solution of partial differential equations. Even I. Newton noticed that power series are a powerful tool for solving differential equations. But in Newton’s time, they did not think about the questions: does every power series have a sum, and is this sum actually a solution to a given equation? The new approach of Cauchy and Weierstrass opened the way to the construction of a rigorous theory and gave confidence that this equation could be solved using power series. S. V. Ko-

Valevskaya managed to prove the most general theorem. She proved that the solution to systems of equations of a very general form is *analytical*, that is, expressed through power series. While studying the solution to one of the most important equations describing the propagation of heat in a rod, Sofya Vasilievna discovered properties of this solution that turned out to be unexpected even for Weierstrass.

The second problem was closely related to elliptic integrals, the third concerned the theory of the rings of Saturn. In her latest work, Sofya Vasilievna clarified the research of the famous French mathematician and mechanic Laplace.

All these problems were successfully solved, and in 1874, at the suggestion of K. Weierstrass, the University of Göttingen awarded in absentia (without defense) the degree of Doctor of Philosophy to Sofya Vasilyevna Kovalevskaya for the works: “On the theory of partial differential equations”, “On the reduction of one class of Abelian integrals of the third rank to elliptic integrals” and “Additions and comments to Laplace’s study on the shape of the ring of Saturn”. praise. Few have received such an honor.

## At home

In July 1874, S.V. Kovalevskaya returned to Russia. At the same time, her sister Anna came to her homeland along with her husband Charles Victor Jacqular, who was an active member of the First International.

When the events of the Paris Commune broke out in the spring of 1871, the Jacqulars took a direct part in them. Victor was elected to the Central Committee of the National Guard and commanded its 17th Legion. He led the armed forces of the Communards in the Montmartre region. Anna, together with Sophia, who came from Berlin to besieged Paris, looked after the wounded.

⟦folio 9⟧

After the defeat of the Paris Commune, Victor Jacqular found himself in prison. To save his son-in-law, V.V. Korvin-Krukovsky himself, who personally knew Thiers, came to Paris. The old general managed to somewhat ease the fate of his daughter’s husband, and soon, with the help of the Kovalevsky couple and his wife Anna, Jacqular managed to escape from prison and move to Switzerland. Anna followed her husband.

So, three years later, everyone gathered together again in Palibino. An atmosphere of universal love and friendship reigned in the old estate. Sophia would be happy if it were not for thoughts about the future. What will be her future fate? Will she get a teaching position at the university? After all, according to the laws of the Russian Empire, a woman could teach mathematics only in the elementary grades of a gymnasium.

After resting in Palibino, the Kovalevskys left for St. Petersburg. Efforts began to obtain a teaching position in some higher educational institution. They ended in failure. Sofya Vasilievna was not allowed to teach even at the Higher Women's Courses.

It was said that during her wanderings through the biocratic institutions of St. Petersburg, Sofya Vasilievna somehow ended up in the office of an official, who, in response to her request for permission to teach at the university, refused, rudely adding: “In our country, men have always done this. They cope with their responsibilities, the words are rich, well, and therefore we don’t need any innovations!》 To this, the indignant Sofya Vasilievna said: “When Pythogoras revealed his famous theorem, he sacrificed 100 bulls. Since then, all the cattle have been rich in new things...”

An important event takes place in the life of the Kovalevskys. Friendship and mutual respect that bind Sophia Va-
Sil'evna and Vladimir Onufrievich, fall into love and their marriage becomes de facto. In 1878, the Kovalevskys gave birth to a daughter, who was named Sophia in honor of her mother.

At this time, Sofya Vasilievna somewhat retreats from her busy mathematics. Now she is a journalist and a diligent housewife. Her popular science articles, critical notes, and theatrical receptions appear in periodicals. In the Kovalevsky guest house there are many scientists and writers: D. I. Mendeleev, I. M. Sechenov, S. P. Botkin, A. M. Butlerov, P. L. Chebyshev, A. G. Stoletov, I. S. Turgenev, F. M. Dostoevsky and others.

At that time, a certain part of Russian society was captured by the “spirit of profit and various commercial enterprises.” This current captured both my husband and my husband, and I must show myself in my sins,” Sofya Vasilievna later recalled. The Kovalevskys wanted to provide for themselves financially, so that later, without thinking about bread, they would be employed by science.

His passion for commerce ended tragically. Vladimir Onufrievich was a dedicated scientist and a worthless businessman. He was deceived by partners, contractors, workers - everyone with whom he dealt. Soon he became completely entangled in debt and, seeing no way out, amassed a life of selfishness.

The sad news found Sofya Vasilyevna in Paris, where she was applying for a teaching position at the Higher Women's Courses. Having received the news of her husband's death, she fell ill. Having recovered, she hurried to St. Petersburg and added to establishing the non-involvement of Vladimir Onufrievich in the dark affairs of his companions. Mathematician and writer

In 1883, Sofya Vasilyevna Kovalevskaya received an invitation from the Swedish mathematician G. Mittag-Leffler (with whom she studied with K. Weierstrass) to take the position of privatdozent at Stockholm University. In November of the same year she left for Sweden. Since Sofya Vasilievna did not know Swedish, she was allowed to lecture in German during the first semester. After a few months, she was already proficient in Swedish and could teach in it.

In the summer of 1884, Sofya Vasilievna was appointed professor at Stockholm University. Over the course of 8 years, she took 12 different courses, including the theory of partial arbitrary equations, a course in mechanics, the theory of algebraic, Abelian and elliptic functions, and others.

But Sofya Vasilievna was not only studying mathematics in those years. She also had extraordinary literary talent: she wrote a number of stories, the novel “The Nihilist,” and the drama “The Struggle for the Score” (together with Mittag-Leffler’s sister, who left the most interesting memories of Sofya Vasilyevna). Kovalevskaya's house became one of the centers of intellectual life in Stockholm - the famous polar explorer Nansen, the Mittag-Lefflers lived here, they wrote a lot, and professors.

Sofya Vasilievna devoted a lot of effort to the liberation movement. She participated in socialist congresses, knew many Russian political emigrants, and actively fought for equal rights for women.

In Stockholm, Sofya Vasilyevna completed her main work - a study of the movement of a top. For this, as already mentioned, in 1888 she received the premio of the Paris Academy of Sciences. The following year she receives a prize of 1,500 kroner from the Stockholm Academy of Sciences for new work, also on rigid body rotation.

Russian mathematicians repeatedly tried to attract S.V. Kovalevskaya to scientific and pedagogical work in her homeland, but to no avail. The petition submitted by Sofia Vasilievna’s relative, General A.I. Kosich, addressed to the president of the Academy, in which he, in particular, cited the words of Napoleon that “every state should value the return of outstanding people more than the conquest of a rich city,” did not help either.

But new trends also penetrated the Academy of Sciences. On November 4 (16), 1889, the Academy fundamentally resolved the issue of “admitting the Lizhian field to election as corresponding members.” 20 people voted for this resolution (6 against). And so, on November 7 (19), at the Department of Physics and Mathematics, at the proposal of academician P. L. Chebyshev, V. G. Imshenetsky and V. Ya. Bunyakovsky, Doctor of Mathematics, Professor of Stockholm University Sofya Vasilievna Kovalevskaya was elected as a corresponding member of the Russian Academy. On December 2 (14), 1889, the general meeting of the Academy approved this decision.

Now Sofya Vasilievna enjoyed world slavery. Mathematicians were waiting for her new works on the motion of a rigid body. It was full of scientific and literary notes. But all these notes remained unimplemented. Returning to Stockholm from France in January 1891, where she spent the winter holidays, Sofya Vasilievna caught a cold and died of pneumonia on January 29 (February 10).

At her funeral there were teachers from Stockholm University, students, and numerous friends.
