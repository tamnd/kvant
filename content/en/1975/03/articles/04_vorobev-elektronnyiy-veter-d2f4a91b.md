## ELECTRONIC WIND
I.I. Vorobiev

Since the end of the last century, experiments have been conducted to study the conductivity of liquid metals. Under normal conditions, liquid metals are not common, but many metals easily form liquid solutions - amalgams - with mercury. The conductivity of liquid metals is interesting because not only conduction electrons, but also ions must contribute to the electric current. And mass transfer is associated with the movement of ions.

Let’s try to qualitatively imagine what will happen when a voltage source is connected to a bath of pure mercury. Mercury is a mixture of conduction electrons and positive ions. It would seem that everything is clear - under the influence of an electric field, electrons will move from “minus” to “plus”, and positive mercury ions will move in the opposite direction. While electrons can travel throughout an electrical circuit, the situation is different with ions. When they approach the negative electrode, it seems that they should accumulate here, increasing the level of mercury in this place.

Nothing of the kind! Experiments have shown that the level of pure mercury is almost the same everywhere, and a slight increase in the level, if it occurs, is in the “plus” and not in the “minus”. In some amalgams, ions move properly along the field, while ions of other metals move against the field! This is amazing - how can ions move in a direction other than where they are attracted by electrical forces?

The reason for the “strange” behavior of ions is the continuous bombardment of ions by electrons that we did not take into account. Let's try to understand the essence of the matter. Let us find out what effect mutual collisions of electrons and ions have on the movement of electrons and ions in the presence of an external electric field.

For clarity, we will consider the ion to be a massive elastic ball, which is hit by a beam of light particles—electrons (the mass of the ion in

That is, immediately after the reaction, all directions of motion are equal, which means that the total momentum of the electrons is zero.

Since the beam momentum changes due to the interaction with pons, this means that a force acts on the electrons from the ions. If $u$ is the average speed of electrons in the beam, then $mu$ is the average electron momentum. Let the time between collisions of an electron with pons be $t$, then we can assume that it is during this time that the average momentum $mu$ of each electron is lost. So the average force per electron is

$$f = -\frac{mu}{\tau}.$$

The loss of momentum by electrons as a whole due to collisions with pons is described by the average braking force $f$. Of course, such a force does not necessarily act on an individual electron, but it is precisely this force that is important for the motion of the beam as a whole.

Although, in addition to the directional movement with an average speed $u$, electrons also have chaotic thermal movement, this will not change the expression for the braking force. The total momentum of a large number of electrons during random heating is always zero, so the change in the total momentum is associated only with the loss of the momentum of ordered motion.

What if the metal contains a small admixture of “foreign” ions? Let the alien's charge be $|e|Z'$. Due to the fact that the dimensions of the impurity ion are different, the number of electron collisions with it will also change. If the cross-sectional area of ​​the impurity ion is $\sigma'$, and the “friend” ion is $\sigma$, then the stranger will be subjected to $\dfrac{\sigma'}{\sigma}$ times more impacts than the friendly ion. The force acting on the impurity ion from the electrons will change by the same amount. Therefore, the sum of the field forces and the electron wind for the stranger is equal to (Fig. 5)

$$\mathbf{F}' = Z'\,|e|\,\mathbf{E} + Z\,\dfrac{\sigma'}{\sigma}\,\mathbf{f}_1.$$

If $Z' > Z\,\dfrac{\sigma'}{\sigma}$, then the impurity ions will move in the direction of this force, their speed will be directed along the field. If $Z' < Z\,\dfrac{\sigma'}{\sigma}$, then the ions will move against the field.

How can we explain the fact that in pure mercury the ions move in the direction of the electron wind? We have shown that in pure metal the ions must be stationary! The fact is that all ions were assumed to be completely identical, but this is not entirely true. Although most ions are indeed in the same "normal" energy state, some ions always have energy greater than normal.

Such ions are called "activated". The probability of collisions between electrons and ions increases with increasing ion energy. We can say that the cross sections of activated ions seem to have increased compared to normal ones. Thus, activated ions can be considered foreigners with the same charge as a normal ion, but with a larger cross-section. Such ions are carried away by the electron wind and are carried towards the positive pole of the source. This is the basis for the separation of mercury isotopes in an electric field. Ions of different isotopes have almost the same charge and cross section, but activated ions of light isotopes are carried away by the electron wind faster than activated ions of heavy isotopes due to their lower mass. This leads to an increase in their concentration at the “plus”, that is, if mercury is sucked out near the positive pole, it will be enriched with a light isotope.

The “paradoxical” transfer of ions in mercury amalgams was first discovered in 1907. At the same time, the concept of mutual friction of ions and electrons was introduced. Subsequent theories, which lasted until 1959, took a step back: the movements of ions and electrons were considered independent. This was partly due to the inconclusiveness of the experimental results. In 1953, the effect of separating mercury isotopes by direct current was discovered. After this, a number of works appeared on the study of ion electrical transport. The first theoretical works, where the physical mechanism of action of the electron wind was revealed, date back to the beginning of 1959.

A whole group of interesting and important effects in both liquid and solid metals and semiconductors is associated with the electron wind.
