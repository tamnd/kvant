## Hyperbola

I. N. Bronstein

---

What is a hyperbole? This question is answered in different ways. The student will answer: “Graph of inverse proportionality.” Anyone familiar with conic sections will say: “The section of a right circular cone by a plane that touches both its floors.” And a university or technical school student will give the answer: “A set of points on a plane such that the difference in the distances of each of them to two fixed points is the same.” Each definition uses some characteristic property of a hyperbola, and the remaining properties can be inferred from the definition.
In this article we will start with the school definition of a hyperbole - from it its form is immediately revealed.

**I. EQUILATERAL HYPERBOLA**

**Inverse proportionality graph**

Readers are familiar with the definition of a hyperbola as a graph of inverse proportion. As will be seen, this is the definition of a particular type of hyperbola - an equilateral hyperbola (the origin of this name will become clear later). It is to this curve that we devote the first part of the article. For brevity, we will for now call an equilateral hyperbola simply a hyperbola.

Two variables $x$ and $y$ are said to be inversely proportional if their product is constant:
$$xy = k. \tag{1}$$

Thus, equality (1) is, for each $k$, a certain function with a specification domain $x > 0$ and a range of values ​​$y > 0$. The graph of this function, shown in figure 1, is called *one branch of an equilateral hyperbola*; for brevity, we will initially call it simply a hyperbola, not only omitting the word “equilateral,” but also not repeating the words “one branch.”

Our definition is based on the concept of the graph of a function. It is possible, however, to accept another, “purely geometric” formulation: *a hyperbola is the set of fourth vertices $M$ of rectangles of a given area $k$, in which one vertex $O$ coincides with the vertex of a given right angle, and two - $L$ and $N$ - lie on its sides* (see Fig. 1).

The point $O$ is called the *center* of the hyperbola - the origin of this name will be clarified further.

Through each point $M_1$ with coordinates $x_1$ and $y_1$ *) there will pass one (but only one!) hyperbola - precisely the one for which the constant $k$, included in the definition of a hyperbola, is equal to $x_1 y_1$. On figure 2, in addition to the red hyperbola, several more are depicted: at $k = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5$ and $16$. Thus, hyperbolas are an infinite set, each of them is determined by the value of the parameter $k$ **).

## The shape of a hyperbola, its vertex and asymptotes

Hyperbolas with different values ​​of the parameter $k$ are similar to each other, but they are not congruent - they cannot be combined by superposition. However, it is easy to prove that they are all similar, even located similarly with the center of similarity $O$. The reader is invited to prove this using figure 3.

Let us describe the form of a hyperbola (more precisely, one branch of an equilateral hyperbola), that is, we will list its properties that are primarily striking.

*Hyperbola has infinite extension* - it cannot all fit on a limited piece of plane. No matter how large the number $A$ is given, it is possible to indicate a point on the hyperbola that is at a distance greater than $A$ from the point $O$ (prove).

*The hyperbola is symmetric with respect to the bisector $OT$ of the angle $XOY$* (Fig. 4), that is, for any point $M_1(x_1,y_1)$ of the hyperbola, the symmetric point $M_2(x_2,y_2)$ also belongs to this hyperbola (prove).

The point $A$ of intersection of a hyperbola with its axis of symmetry $OT$ is called the *vertex* of the hyperbola. Each vertex coordinate of a hyperbola with parameter $k$ is equal to $\sqrt{k}$. The distance of the vertex from the center of the hyperbola (we will denote it by $a$) is equal to $OA=\sqrt{2k}$. The vertex is the closest point of the hyperbola from the center (prove).

Let us dwell on the most remarkable property of a hyperbola and determine its shape: a point that moves infinitely along a hyperbola in one direction will indefinitely approach one coordinate axis, and a point that moves infinitely in the other direction will indefinitely approach another coordinate axis **). This property is mathematically formulated as follows: the coordinate axes OX and OY are its asymptotes (from the Greek asimptotos - divergent).

Let us prove this property of hyperbole. Let us take a point $M_1$ on it, lying to the right of the vertex $A(x_1 > V\sqrt{k})$, a distance $y_1$, which is less than $\varepsilon$ (Fig. 5) to the OX axis. This can always be done by taking $x_1 > \frac{k}{\varepsilon}$. Then $M_1N_1 = y_1 = \frac{k}{x_1} < \varepsilon$ and for all points $M(x, y)$ of the hyperbola lying to the right of $M_1$, $x > x_1$ and $y = \frac{k}{x} < y_1 < \varepsilon$, that is, the OX axis is an asymptote of the hyperbola. From the symmetry of the curve it follows that the OY axis is its asymptote.

Knowing the vertex of the hyperbole and its asymptotes, you can quite accurately draw the hyperbole by hand.

## Equation of an equilateral hyperbola. Hyperbola and circle

The equation of a hyperbola, defined as a graph of inverse proportionality, is the well-known equality $xy = k$. We will write it in a slightly different form: instead of the parameter $k$, we introduce the semi-axis $a$ into (1). Since $k= \pm \dfrac{a^2}{2}$, where the sign is “+” for the red hyperbola, “—” is for the blue hyperbola, the equations of both hyperbolas will take the form
$$xy = \pm \frac{a^2}{2}. \tag{2}$$

Each of these two equations is called *the equation of a hyperbola with respect to asymptotes*. These words express that the coordinate axes in this case are the asymptotes of the hyperbola.

The equation of any line depends not only on its shape and size, but also on how it is located in the coordinate plane. Let us now take (Fig. 7), in the plane, in addition to the coordinate system $XOY$ (“old”), another coordinate system $X'OY'$, obtained by rotating the axes $OX$ and $OY$ by $45°$ counterclockwise. Let us take any point $M$ of the plane on which both coordinate systems are introduced. It has 4 coordinates: old $(x, y)$ and new $(x', y')$. It can be proven that the old coordinates are expressed in terms of the new ones by the following formulas:
$$x = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}(x' - y'), \quad y = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}(x' + y'). \tag{3}$$

II. GENERAL HYPERBOLE

Definition of a general hyperbola

From the article “Ellipse”, readers know that if a circle is subjected to a stretching-rolling transformation (RS)*, then it will turn into an ellipse. We will be interested in the figure in which each of the equilateral hyperbolas depicted on figure by 9 dashed lines will go, as a result of PC relative to the symmetry axis $A'A$.

The fundamental square $pqr$ of these hyperbolas will transform into the rectangle $PQRS$ (at Fig. 9), its diagonals $pr$ and $qs$ will also transform into straight lines $PR$ and $QS$.

Conjugate (red and blue) equilateral hyperbolas will transform into red and blue solid lines as a result of PC. Each of them also consists of two branches and a symmetry with respect to the midlines of the functional rectangle $PQRS$, the diagonals of which serve as asymptotes of these curves. But these new lines will no longer be congestive. We will call them still conjugate hyperbolas.

A curve consisting of two branches and obtained as a result of PC of a “complete” equilateral hyperbola relative to the midline of a functional square is called a general hyperbola or a simple periola.

III. HYPERBOLA - CONIC SECTION

The similarity between an ellipse and a hyperbola does not stop there. It is known that an ellipse is an inclined section of a circular cylinder.

Let us prove, using the geometric definition of a hyperbola, that each hyperbola can be obtained as a section of a certain circular cone to a plane parallel to its axis.

In the proof, we will consider only one hyperbola - our properties for points in this hyperbola follow from the symmetry of both sexes.

Given a branch of a hyperbola and its asymptotes OX and OY, intersecting at an angle $\omega$ (Fig. 11, a). By definition, for each of its points the area of ​​the ONML parallelgram is the same - it is equal to ON·OL·sin $\omega$.

Let us denote the product ON·OL, constructed for any point M of the hyperbola, by k: ON·OL = ML $\times$ MN = k. Let us draw a perpendicular QQ' through M to the symmetry axis of the hyperbola; it will intersect the asymptotes OX and OY at points Q and Q'. The product of the segments MQ and MQ' is also constructed for any point on the branch of the hyperbola M. Indeed, MQ = 2·MN·sin $\omega/2$,

$$MQ' = 2ML \cdot \sin \omega/2,$$

$$MQ \cdot MQ' = 4k \cdot \sin^2 \omega/2 = (k')^2,$$

Focal property of hyperbole

It is known that the ellipse has a remarkable “focal” property: in its plane there are also points $F_1$ and $F_2$ (foci), which from the sum of the distances of each point $M$ of the ellipse to its foci is the same - it is equal to the length of the major axis of the ellipse:

$$F_1M + F_2M = 2a.$$ (6)

Hyperbole has a similar property. In its plane there are two points (foci of the hyperbola), such that the difference in distances of each point $M$ of the hyperbola for these lobes is the same - it is equal to the distance between the vertices of the hyperbola:

$$F_1M - F_2M = 2a.$$ (7a)

Here $F_1$ is the focus more distant from $M$ than $F_2$ (Fig. 12). Both foci lie on the symmetry axis of the hyperbola passing through the vertices $A$ and $A'$; the distance of each focus from the center of the hyperbola is

$$F_1O = F_2O = c = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2},$$

where $a$ and $b$ are the lengths of the semi-axes of this hyperbola and its conjugate **). Important connection between the vertex "hyperbole elements $a$, $b$ and $c$

$$c^2 = a^2 + b^2$$ (8)

is analogous to the $c^2 = a^2 - b^2$ connection for an ellipse.

Drawing a hyperbola in a continuous motion

Drawing a hyperbola using two threads, similar to the well-known drawing of an ellipse, is based on the focal property. Place a sheet of paper on the drawing board and pin two push pins $F_1$ and $F_2$ (Fig. 13) onto the sheet of paper. Let's take two threads, the difference in length (2a) is less than the distance between the buttons (2c), tie the end of each thread to the legs of the buttons, and tie the remaining ends with a knot (point C). Now holding the knot in your left hand, protect both threads with the point of a pencil $M$ and move the pencil on the paper so that both threads $F_1MC$ and $F_2MC$ are always in a taut position. Then the tip $M$ of the pencil is always on the same branch of the hyperbola with the foci $F_1$ and $F_2$ and the axis length $2a = F_1M - F_2M$ (at $CMF_1 > CMF_2$), since
$$F_1M - F_2M = (F_1M + MC) - (F_2M + MC) = 2a.$$
To draw the second branch, you should swap the buttons with the threads attached to them. The drawn arcs of the hyperbola will be longer, the longer the thread.

Application of hyperbole in military affairs

It is necessary to determine on the map how to direct the ordine in order to hit a stationary sounding target, for example, the enemy’s shooting ordine (Fig. 14).
