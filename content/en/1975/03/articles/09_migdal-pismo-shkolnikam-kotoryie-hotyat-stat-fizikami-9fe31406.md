A. B. Midal
Letter to schoolchildren who want to become physicists

Students from school No. 1 in Lida sent a letter to the famous Soviet theoretical physicist Academician Arkady Beinusovich Migdal. In the letter they asked to tell how his passion for physics began and how to prepare for this profession. We present A. B. Midal's answer because we hope it will be of interest to all our readers.

When I was 12 years old, I broke Donat’s book “Physics in Games” and began making physics games. I came up with some of them myself. Then he began to build various physical devices. I made a thermal current limiter of my own design. I made detector receivers (there were no parts in the product at that time).

After graduating from school, I worked for a year in a school laboratory in physics. At the same time, he published a note in the journal “Physics, Mathematics, Chemistry in Secondary School” “A device that replaces the Atwood machine” (a device for testing Newton’s 2nd law). But he really began to study physics after entering Leningrad State University.

As you probably know, physicists are divided into two types: theoretical physicists and experimental physicists. It became clear to me already from the first year that I would be a theoretician. A theoretical physicist is close in nature to mathematics. His tools are paper and pencil. Using the already known laws of physics, he must find new correspondences between different quantities. I will give two examples.

During the siege of Leningrad, theorists calculated how much ice it would be possible to safely transport trucks with people across Lake Ladoga. This is a very simple task. It was only necessary to use the ice permeability limit found in many experiments and apply the well-known equations of the theory of elasticity.

But the task is immeasurably more complex, which required moving to understandable classical mechanics. The great theoretical physicist Albert Einstein analyzed the course of physical processes in moving bodies and came to the conclusion that in a fast moving system time flows more slowly. Later, this conclusion was brilliantly confirmed in experiments studying the decay of fast elementary particles (for example, m-mesons). Moving particles decay more slowly than stationary particles, and the dependence of decay time on speed exactly coincides with that predicted by Einstein.

In this example, the experimenters' task is to test the predicted theory. However, more often the opposite happens - experimental physicists find a new pattern that does not fit into existing theories, and this gives impetus to the development of theoretical physics.

In the case of Einstein's relative theory, which was just mentioned, this was only Michelson's change experiment, in which the difference in the speed of light changed in the direction of the Earth's movement and perpendicular to it. It was shown that both speeds coincide with tremendous accuracy. This result contradicted the existing theory of light propagation at that time. Attempts to explain the result of Michelson's experiment led Einstein to the creation of the theory of relative Einstein and experimental physics mutually stimulate each other.

Sooner or later, the future physicist must decide which work is closer to his mentality—theoretical or experimental? The sooner this decision comes, the better.

I will tell you about my first work in physics. In my second year, I got an internship at the laboratorium of the Elektropribor plant (now the Vibator plant). Just at this time, a large batch of voltmeters was rejected due to the fact that when the device turned on the alternating current, the position of the end of the arrow was blurred by several millimeters. I decided to try to find out the reason for the marriage. It was clear that it was a matter of resonance. It was necessary to find out exactly which part of the arrow is in resonance, and what should be changed to get rid of it.

I took the pointer out of the device and secured it to the frame of the frequency meter (a device that measures the frequency of alternating current). Then I placed graph paper under the arrow and, changing the speed of the generator that supplied current to the frequency meter, plotted a curve of the dependence of the amplitude of oscillations of the end of the arrow on the frequency of the supplied current. The result is a typical resonance curve with a maximum at 70 cycles per second (Fig. 1). But why 70? After all, the maximum placement in the device was with a normal current having a frequency of 50 cycles per second. I temporarily put this question aside and continued to find out the nature of the needle’s oscillations. To do this, I made precise scratches in various places on the arrow and illuminated each of them in turn with side light so that a moving luminous point was obtained. By observing these points under magnification (using a micromanipulator), it was possible to determine the amplitude of oscillations of various parts of the arrow. In this way it was possible to prove that the resonance corresponds to the twisting of the vertical elbow of the arrow (Fig. 2), that is, that the long part of the arrow rotates as a whole around the axis, passing through the vertical elbow.

Based on the stiffness of the materials and the distribution of mass along the arrow, it was possible to calculate the vibration frequency. The calculated frequency turned out to be close to the observed one, that is, it was about 70 periods per second. So would-

It has been proven that the nature of the oscillations has been established correctly. After this, it was necessary to find out what was the reason for the decrease in the resonant part when the arrow was placed in the device.

On the frame of the frequency meter, the needle was rigidly fixed, whereas in the device it was attached to a frame through which current flows and which itself can oscillate. To find the frequency of the arrow with such a non-rigid fixation, it was necessary to solve the problem of two coupled oscillatory systems. The calculation showed that the frequency of the arrow, attached to the movable frame, should actually decrease from 70 to 50 periods per second. Thus the cause was established. All that remains is to calculate how to change the thickness of the vertical part of the arrow in order to avoid resonance.

After this, a series of 1000 devices with a modified arrow was manufactured. It turned out that the needle was in a plane position and would not produce any vibrations. Only in two devices out of 1000 was the position of the end of the needle still blurred, but when these devices were opened, it turned out that the old hands had been placed there by mistake. After that, I was offered a job in a factory laboratory in parallel with my work at the university. I agreed and during this time I learned to independently pose and solve applied theoretical problems. This helped me a lot in the future.

Like most theoretical physicists, I had to work in various areas of physics. Atomic physics, cosmic rays, the theory of metals, the atomic nucleus, quantum field theory, astrophysics - all these sections of physics seem interesting to me. Now the most fundamental questions are being solved in the theory of elementary parts and in quantum field theory, but in other areas of physics there are many interesting unsolved problems.

To get acquainted with various areas of physics, and most importantly - to feel their interconnection, it is very useful to carefully read the wonderful “Feynman Lectures on Physics”, which were written by the famous American physicist Richard Feynman.

Try to read actively, trying to solve the problems posed in the book before reading the solution.

In order to engage in science, you must not only know and be able to do a lot, but also cultivate certain character qualities in yourself. Here are some tips.

Do not mix all the concepts from the very beginning to the end. Understanding comes gradually, as a result of hard work, as you get used to new concepts. A real, deep concept necessarily leads to new results, but it comes only after you have worked well in this area.

If any idea comes to mind, it is necessary not only to confirm it, but even more so to refute it. This is the only way to learn to think conscientiously and objectively.

The main driving force should be interest in the matter, and not the desire for effective solutions. I know many examples where talented people lost the ability to work conscientiously because of the desire to make a “world discovery.”

The task of a scientist is persistent and conscientious research of the phenomenon that interests him, when every small step brings joy. Discovery comes only as a by-product of such research.

From an idea, even the best one, to a reliable scientific truth - a difficult and difficult path, through doubts and agreements, through ups and downs, like climbing an unconquered peak from which new horizons open.
