## SOFIA VASILIEVNA KOVALEVSKAYA
N.Ya. Vilenkin
V.P. Lishevsky

(on the 125th anniversary of his birth)

**First Prize**

In 1888, the Paris Academy of Sciences awarded a prize for the best scientific work devoted to the motion of a rigid body having one fixed point. This problem was also called the problem of the movement of a top - after all, all points of a rapidly rotating top are in motion, with the exception of the end of the tip with which the top touches the floor. Since ancient times, tops (or, as they are also called, spinning tops) have been children's favorite toys. But they also attracted the attention of reputable scientists - the properties of rotating bodies were too amazing. It was not clear why the axis of a rapidly rotating top itself rotates slowly. What also seemed surprising was the desire of the top axis to maintain its direction when external forces acted on the top, and the change in the direction of the axis went in a direction different from the direction of this force. Interest in the movement of the top was not idle curiosity. Explanations for the properties of this movement were awaited by astronomers who were interested in such huge tops as planets and stars, and by gunsmiths who had long noticed that bullets and shells hit the target more accurately if they are given not only

forward, but also rotational movement, technicians who developed new models of bicycles, and many others.

Until the beginning of the 17th century, scientists, speaking about the properties of tops, gave only explanations similar to the words of Moliere’s character, who claimed that morphine puts you to sleep because it contains a soporific power. But after the work of G. Galileo, who explained the laws of falling bodies, and H. Huygens, who was interested in the movement of a pendulum, a new branch of mechanics arose - dynamics. One of her tasks was to explain the mysterious properties of the top. At the end of the 17th century, I. Newton and G. Leibniz, having created differential and integral calculus, gave into the hands of scientists the “golden key”, which made it possible to reduce problems of dynamics to the solution of equations containing the desired functions and their derivatives - the so-called *differential equations*. The power of the new calculus methods was tested on problems of celestial mechanics: with their help, I. Newton was able to explain Keplerian laws of planetary motion, and his followers were able to predict the time of return of one of the brightest comets - Halley's Comet.
