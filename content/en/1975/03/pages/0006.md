⟦folio 4⟧

Naturally, scientists tried to apply new methods to study the movement of the voluk. To do this, they had to introduce a number of new concepts - the moment of inertia, which played the same role in explaining rotational movements as the mass of a body in explaining translational motion, the ellipson of inertia, which shows the change in the moment of inertia when the axis of rotation changes, etc. Thus, a member of the St. Petersburg Academy of Sciences, one of the greatest mathematicians of the 18th century, L. Euler studied the movement of a body around the center of gravity (Fig. 1). This was of great importance, as it made it possible to explain the reason for the so-called “preliminary equivalence” associated with a change in the axis of rotation of the globe, the movement of rotating gun shells, etc. Euler’s research was supplemented by the French scientist L. Poinsot. After this, wolves became a favorite model to which physicists resorted, trying to explain certain phenomena. Further, J. Maxwell, strictly the theory of electromagnetic phenomena, resorted to mechanical models, in which wolves placed at each point in space played a larger role.

But the Euler-Poinsot theory could not explain the movement of the wolf, which kids love to play with - its fulcrum is not at the center of gravity. A die of this type has the shape of a body of rotation, and the fulcrum is located on the axis of symmetry (Fig. 2). The problem of the movement of a wolf of this shape was solved by J. Lagrange, who outlined his solution in the book “Analytical Mechanics”; This book became famous not only for the depth of its presentation, but also for the fact that it did not contain a single drawing - Lagrange considered mechanics to be the science of solving differential equations and did not resort to visual representations. Lagrange's solution was developed and improved by S. Poisson.

After Euler, Lagrange, Poinsot and Poisson, many scientists tried to find at least one more form of the wolf, in which the solution to the equation of its motion can be reduced to calculating integrals. But we were unable to move forward a single step. This was especially surprising, because just in the 19th century, in the works of K. Gauss, M. V. Ostrogradsky, C. Jacobi, O. Cauchy, B. Riemann and many others, new mathematical methods were created that made it possible to solve the most difficult problems of mathematical physics. And to gain a deeper understanding of the wolf’s movement was also necessary for practice. Although in those days neither ships, nor planes, nor rockets could use gyroscopic devices, it was clear from a technical standpoint that the time of gyroscopes was just around the corner, and there was nothing more practical than a good theory.
