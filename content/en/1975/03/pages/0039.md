⟦folio 37⟧

In the case of Einstein's relative theory, which was just mentioned, this was only Michelson's change experiment, in which the difference in the speed of light changed in the direction of the Earth's movement and perpendicular to it. It was shown that both speeds coincide with tremendous accuracy. This result contradicted the existing theory of light propagation at that time. Attempts to explain the result of Michelson's experiment led Einstein to the creation of the theory of relative Einstein and experimental physics mutually stimulate each other.

Sooner or later, the future physicist must decide which work is closer to his mentality—theoretical or experimental? The sooner this decision comes, the better.

I will tell you about my first work in physics. In my second year, I got an internship at the laboratorium of the Elektropribor plant (now the Vibator plant). Just at this time, a large batch of voltmeters was rejected due to the fact that when the device turned on the alternating current, the position of the end of the arrow was blurred by several millimeters. I decided to try to find out the reason for the marriage. It was clear that it was a matter of resonance. It was necessary to find out exactly which part of the arrow is in resonance, and what should be changed to get rid of it.

I took the pointer out of the device and secured it to the frame of the frequency meter (a device that measures the frequency of alternating current). Then I placed graph paper under the arrow and, changing the speed of the generator that supplied current to the frequency meter, plotted a curve of the dependence of the amplitude of oscillations of the end of the arrow on the frequency of the supplied current. The result is a typical resonance curve with a maximum at 70 cycles per second (Fig. 1). But why 70? After all, the maximum placement in the device was with a normal current having a frequency of 50 cycles per second. I temporarily put this question aside and continued to find out the nature of the needle’s oscillations. To do this, I made precise scratches in various places on the arrow and illuminated each of them in turn with side light so that a moving luminous point was obtained. By observing these points under magnification (using a micromanipulator), it was possible to determine the amplitude of oscillations of various parts of the arrow. In this way it was possible to prove that the resonance corresponds to the twisting of the vertical elbow of the arrow (Fig. 2), that is, that the long part of the arrow rotates as a whole around the axis, passing through the vertical elbow.
