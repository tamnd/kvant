Despite everything said above, two terms will remain in our language for a long time, perhaps forever: energy and mass. From the point of view of relativistic mechanics, it would be more expedient to use one name to denote the quantities that are meant here. However, spoken language is formed on the basis of everyday experience, and there are no relativistic effects in it.

The total energy, or relativistic mass, determines only the upper limit of the work that a given body can perform. In fact, in the vast majority of cases, only a tiny fraction of this energy can be converted into work. This is true even for nuclear reactions. The only exception is the process of annihilation of externality and anti-procession, in which the energy contained in externality is completely freed. But annihilation is so far carried out only with elementary particles.

From expression (5) it is clear that between rest energy and rest mass there is the same correspondence as between $E$ and $m$, that is, these concepts coincide. But the differences between the rest energy $E_0$ and the total energy (or, accordingly, between the rest mass and the relativistic mass) are significant. When conditions are determined, rest energy can transform into other types of energy. With this process in mind, people sometimes talk about the conversion of mass into energy. This is an unsuccessful expression, if only because it remains unclear what mass and what energy we are talking about. Only in Newtonian mechanics was it possible to speak unambiguously about mass without adding anything to this term.

In conclusion, let us turn once again to formulas (1), (3) and (6). The question inevitably arises: why does the speed of propagation of electromagnetic waves play such an important role in purely mechanical formulas? The fact is that in our world there is an abscissa limiting speed equal to $c$, the value of which is the same in all coordinate systems. Naturally, this fact cannot but have a direct relation to the laws of motion of physical bodies, to the laws of mechanics. That is why the quantity $c$ is included in all formulas of relativistic mechanics. By chance it turns out to be at the same speed. The limiting speed $c$ is called the speed of light according to purely historical principles: simplicity because it was first encountered in optics.
