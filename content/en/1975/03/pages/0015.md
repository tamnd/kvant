A sequential beam scatters evenly in all directions (Fig. 1) (a mirror ball also scatters a directed beam of light evenly in all directions). That is, immediately after the reaction, all directions of motion are equal, which means that the total momentum of the electrons is zero.

Since the beam momentum changes due to the interaction with pons, this means that a force acts on the electrons from the ions. If $u$ is the average speed of electrons in the beam, then $mu$ is the average electron momentum. Let the time between collisions of an electron with pons be $t$, then we can assume that it is during this time that the average momentum $mu$ of each electron is lost. So the average force per electron is

$$f = -\frac{mu}{\tau}.$$

The loss of momentum by electrons as a whole due to collisions with pons is described by the average braking force $f$. Of course, such a force does not necessarily act on an individual electron, but it is precisely this force that is important for the motion of the beam as a whole.

Although, in addition to the directional movement with an average speed $u$, electrons also have chaotic thermal movement, this will not change the expression for the braking force. The total momentum of a large number of electrons during random heating is always zero, so the change in the total momentum is associated only with the loss of the momentum of ordered motion.
