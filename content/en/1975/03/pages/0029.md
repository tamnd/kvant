Unfortunately, this is where the analogies end, since $m$ depends on the speed and, accordingly, on the choice of coordinate system. Thus, in this respect, relativistic mass is more similar to energy than to Newtonian mass: it is not a characteristic that depends only on the body under study.

In contrast to the relativistic mass $m$, the rest mass $m_0$ determines the inertia of the body when the speed of the body $v$ is zero. It follows that $m_0$ does not change when moving to a moving coordinate system. Like the Newtonian mass, the rest mass is determined only by the body itself and does not depend on the coordinate system in which this body is considered. However, rest mass is not equal to the ratio of the absolute value of momentum $p$ to the absolute value of the body's velocity $v$ and is not a coefficient of proportionality between force and acceleration.

But the main difference between the Newtonian mass and the rest mass of relativistic mechanics is that the latter does not obey the conservation law. As an example, we can point out the process of annihilation of an electron and a positron, in which these two particles turn into $\gamma$-quanta of the electromagnetic field (photons). As a result of annihilation, the electron and positron disappear, and electromagnetic waves appear in their place. Momentum $\mathbf{p}$ and energy $E$ are conserved in such a system. Thus, the total relativistic mass of the system $m$ must also be conserved. At the same time, the rest mass of the system changes, since instead of two particles (electron and positron) with a rest mass different from zero, $\gamma$-quanta appear, the rest mass of which is equal to zero.

Another example is the process of fission of uranium or plutonium nuclei, which is accompanied by the formation of lighter nuclei with a lower total rest mass.

So, the rest mass is also significantly different from the Newtonian mass. Strictly speaking, in relativistic mechanics there is no specific concept at all that could be characterized by properties similar to the properties of “ordinary” mass. The role played by this quantity in classical mechanics is distributed between two quantities, each of which can, essentially, with equal right be considered the “successor” of Newtonian mass.

It is precisely in such situations that the reason for the difficulty of understanding new theories often lies. By attributing correspondence with objective reality to the basic concepts and terms of the theory, we naturally expect from the further development of science only a clarification of these concepts. In reality, the new theory sometimes treats the “realities” of the old one very disrespectfully. Essentially, each new theory redefines the arsenal of concepts and terms with which it intends to describe reality. The analogy between the terms of different physical theories is often very incomplete, and the use of the same names can easily be misleading.

A similar situation arises with the term “kinetic energy”. Nonrelativistic mechanics knew only two types of energy - potential and kinetic. Kinetic energy has the following two important properties:

a) it becomes zero when the speed of the body is zero;

b) if no external forces act on the body, the energy remains unchanged.
