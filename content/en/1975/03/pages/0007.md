Therefore, it should be recognized that the choice of the topic for the competition by the Paris Academy of Sciences was successful. Having studied the submitted manuscripts, the jury recognized the best work under the motto: “Say what you know, do what you must, be what will be.” It contained a solution to the problem of the motion of an asymmetrical top, the center of gravity of which lies on the equatorial plane of the ellipsoid of inertia, the lengths of the axes of which are subject to the condition $A = B = 2C$ (Fig. 3). The author of the work showed not only great mathematical talent, but also extraordinary erudition: the work used the latest achievements of mathematics of that time - the theory of functions of a complex variable, hyperelliptic integrals, etc. The jury members liked the research so much that they decided to increase the prize amount from 3,000 francs to 5,000 francs. When they opened the envelope with the author's name, it unexpectedly turned out that the best work was written by the only woman who held the position of professor of mathematics at that time - Sofya Vasilievna Kovalevskaya.

## Childhood and youth

Long and difficult was Sofia Vasilievna’s path to this triumph, which immediately placed her above all the women who had studied mathematics before her - the Greek Hypatia (5th century AD), who lectured on philosophy and mathematics, and the Frenchwoman Emilia du Chatelet, who translated Newton’s Principia into French at the beginning of the 18th century, and the Italian Maria Gaetana Agnesi, in whose honor one of the curves was called the "Agnesi curl".

Sofya Vasilievna was born in Moscow one hundred and twenty-five years ago on January 3 (15), 1850. Her father is General V.V. Korvin-Krukovsky, head of the Moscow arsenal,

⟦column⟧

descended from the Hungarian king Matthew Corvinus. Mother, Elizaveta Fedorovna, came from a scientific family - her father F. F. Schubert was an honorary member of the Academy of Sciences, and her grandfather, academician F. I. Schubert, was a famous astronomer and mathematician.

In 1858, Vasily Vasilyevich Korvin-Krukovsky was dismissed with the rank of lieutenant general and moved with his family to his estate Palibino, Nevelsky district, Vitebsk province. He educated his children at home. Sonya's mentors were the Englishwoman M. F. Smith and home teacher I. I. Malevich. But the girl’s interest in mathematics appeared under the influence of her uncle, Pyotr Vasilyevich Korvin-Krukovsky. “From him, for example, I heard for the first time about the squaring of the circle, about the asymptotes to which the curve is constantly approaching without ever reaching them, and about many other things of the same kind” *), Sofya Vasilievna later recalled.

In addition to conversations with her uncle, there was one more circumstance, quite curious, that aroused Sonya’s interest in mathematics. This is how S.V. Kovalevskaya described this episode in her memoirs.

“When we moved to live in the village, the whole house had to be redecorated and all the rooms covered with new wallpaper. But... there wasn't enough wallpaper for one of our children's rooms... This abused room remained for many years with one side covered with paper. But, by a happy coincidence, the sheets of lithographed Ostrogradsky’s lectures on differential and integral calculus, acquired by my father in his youth, were used for this preliminary pasting.
