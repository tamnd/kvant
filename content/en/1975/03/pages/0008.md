These sheets, covered with incomprehensible formulas, soon attracted my attention. I remember how, as a child, I spent whole hours in front of this tanish wall, trying to make out at least individual phrases and find the order in which the sheets should follow each other. From long, daily contemplation, the appearance of many of the formulas remains in my memory, and the text itself left a deep imprint on my brain, although at the very moment of reading it remained incomprehensible to me.

When many years later, as a fifteen-year-old girl, I took my first lesson in differential calculus from a famous teacher in St. Petersburg, Alexander Nikolaevich Strannolyubsky, he was surprised at how quickly I grasped and internalized the concepts of limit and arbitrary, as if I knew them in advance.”

Sonya's passion for mathematics led to conflict in the family. General Korvin-Krukovsky did not at all want his daughter to become a “bluestocking” or, even worse, a short-haired student, about whom they said that “they don’t believe in God and cut live frogs.” Sonya often had to study mathematics in secret. When she went to bed, she put Bourdon’s Course in Algebra, which she had received from Malevich, under her pillow, and when everyone fell asleep, she read it by the light of the stove almost until the morning.

The Korvin-Krukovsky estate’s companion, physics professor N. N. Tyrtov, helped Sonina’s studies. One day, while visiting his neighbors, he heard from Sonya that she had read his elementary physics textbook and understood everything there. Tyrtov didn’t believe her at first. But, after talking with the girls, he was so surprised by her ability that he went to the general and began to convince him of the need to teach the girls in a very server-like manner. Tyrtov even compared Sonya to Pascal, who as a child himself derived many theorems

Euclidean geometry. And the general agreed that Sonya should continue her studies in mathematics. It began to be taught by the mathematician A. N. Strannolyubsky. During one winter, she studied analytical geometry, differential and integral calculus with him.

But it wasn’t only Sony who caused trouble for the venerable general. Unexpectedly, he intercepted a letter sent to his eldest daughter Anna. Petersburg writer and journalist Fyodor Mikhailovich Dostoevsky, had recently returned to the capital after many years of catalog work, to which he was sentenced for participating in Petrashevsky’s decision circle. The letter praised the literary merits of the story written by Anna and sent to the magazine headed by Dostoevsky. The scanial was incredible - the daughter of respected parents secretly corresponds with a stranger, and even yesterday’s catalog director!

It became clear to Anna and Sonya that in order to further develop their abilities they must break free from parental care. The most common way of emancipation (liberation) of women at that time was a fictitious marriage, after which the fictitious husbands presented their wives with complete freedom. In the fall of 1868, Sonya entered into a fictitious marriage with the future outstanding palenotologist Vladimir Onufrievitch Kovalevsky (1842-1883) and the following year she left with him and her older sister Anna to Germany to continue her education - in Russia, access to universities was closed to women.
