Before answering this question, let's see what can be said about the inertial mass in non-relativistic mechanics. From the school physics course we know that:

1) the product of mass and the acceleration of a body is equal to the force acting on it;
2) the product of mass and velocity of a body is equal to its momentum;
3) body weight does not depend on the choice of coordinate system;
4) the mass of the isolated body is conserved.

From point 3 it follows that mass should not depend on speed. Indeed, the speed of the same body in two coordinate systems moving relative to each other is different. If mass depended on speed, its magnitude would have different values ​​in these coordinate systems. The last point refers to the well-known law of conservation of mass. The mass of an isolated body, which is not acted upon by external forces, cannot change as a result of any internal processes occurring in it (for example, the effect of a change in the relative speed of movement of its part arising due to their interaction).

If the mass of a body does not depend on speed, it will not change under the action of external forces accelerating the body. Thus, the use of paragraph 3 allows us to formulate a law of conservation of mass that is broader than that assumed in paragraph 4. The two functional properties given in paragraphs 3 and 4 usually mass in Newtonian mechanics turns out to be the most important characteristic of a physical body. It is due to these properties that mass was considered a measure of the amount of matter contained in a body.

In relativistic mechanics there are two concepts: relativistic mass ($m$) and rest mass ($m_0$), which are related to each other by the following relation:

$$m = \frac{m_0}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}},$$

where $v$ is the speed of the body, $c$ is the speed of light. Momentum $\mathbf{p}$ is represented as the product of relativistic mass and body speed

$$\mathbf{p} = m\mathbf{v},$$

and energy is in the form of the product

$$E = mc^2.$$

As in Newtonian mechanics, if no external forces act on a body, then the momentum $\mathbf{p}$ and energy $E$, and therefore the relativistic mass $m$ of this body remain unchanged.

Let us now determine what quantity should be called mass in relativistic mechanics. If the momentum of the body $\mathbf{p}$ for a short period of time $\Delta t$ has changed by values ​​$\Delta \mathbf{p}$, then according to the laws of Newtonian mechanics the ratio of $\Delta \mathbf{p}$ to $\Delta t$ is equal to the acting force. This law remains true in relativistic mechanics:

$$\mathbf{F} = \frac{\Delta \mathbf{p}}{\Delta t} = \frac{\Delta (m\mathbf{v})}{\Delta t}.$$

However, according to formula (1), the value $m$ depends on the speed and changes over time $\Delta t$. Therefore, it cannot be derived from under the sign $\Delta$, that is, $\Delta (m\mathbf{v}) \neq m\Delta \mathbf{v}$. Therefore, from the law of motion described by equation (4), it is impossible to conclude that the force and acceleration vectors are proportional. Consequently, the question of the properties contained in paragraph 1 disappears.

As can be seen from expression (2), the relativistic mass has property 2 of the Newtonian mass. In addition, from the law of conservation of energy it follows that in a system isolated from the action of external forces, the relativistic mass is conserved. Consequently, it also has the properties of 4 Newtonian mass.
