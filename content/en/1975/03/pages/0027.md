⟦folio 25⟧

MASS AND ENERGY
IN THEORY
RELATIVITY

One of the hottest moments
I in the history of mathematics is the moment
when it turns out that two partitions
mathematicians, previously treated separately and thought to be independent, are in reality two hidden forms of the same thing.

W. W. Sawyer. "Prelude to Mathematics"

What is said in the epigraph applies not only to mathematics, but also to physics. If about a hundred years ago we had turned to physics with the question whether mass and energy are a manifestation of the same property of physical bodies, then in response he would have only shrugged his shoulders, as if they were familiar:
“What pages of thought can sometimes come to mind!”
Out of condescension towards our lack of education, he could explain to us that mass is a measure of the inertia of a body, and energy determines the body’s ability to do work. This is a perfect development because energy can be imparted to a body without changing its mass. At the same time, he could draw on direct experience, recall that the mass of a cold piece of steel does not change when it is heated and melted. Indeed, even the most accurate measurements do not detect any changes in esa when the body heats up. This was known to everyone, and therefore years ago we, in all likelihood, would not have asked such a question.

So, as centuries have passed, mass is an integral property of the body. A body can have all its energy, but not its mass. You can imagine a body at rest in space far from other bodies. then its temperature is close to absolute zero, and there are no external fields, including the gravitational field, around it. Such a body cannot do work *), that is, its energy is zero, but its inertia remains the same as on Earth.

Finally, a physicist centuries later would have added that energy cannot be considered a property at all; it is present only in the body itself. Thus, the kinetic energy in the room is equal to zero in the coordinate system associated with the Earth, but in the coordinate system at rest relative to the Sun, it is many times greater than the energy of the flying projectile (relative to the Earth). Mass is determined only by the property of the body itself.

This would have been the answer to the question posed before the emergence of the theory of relative mathematics. Now we know that the mechanics of the 19th century, which is understandably called Newtonian, classical or non-relativistic mechanics, applies only when the speed of the body is small compared to the speed of light. What is the situation like in relativistic mechanics, where there is no such limitation?

*) Strictly speaking, it is also required that no chemical reactions occur. Nuclear reactions have not been known to physicists for centuries.
