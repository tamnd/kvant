## Hyperbola

I. N. Bronstein

---

What is a hyperbole? This question is answered in different ways. The student will answer: “Graph of inverse proportionality.” Anyone familiar with conic sections will say: “The section of a right circular cone by a plane that touches both its floors.” And a university or technical school student will give the answer: “A set of points on a plane such that the difference in the distances of each of them to two fixed points is the same.” Each definition uses some characteristic property of a hyperbola, and the remaining properties can be inferred from the definition.
In this article we will start with the school definition of a hyperbole - from it its form is immediately revealed.

**I. EQUILATERAL HYPERBOLA**

**Inverse proportionality graph**

Readers are familiar with the definition of a hyperbola as a graph of inverse proportion. As will be seen, this is the definition of a particular type of hyperbola - an equilateral hyperbola (the origin of this name will become clear later). It is to this curve that we devote the first part of the article. For brevity, we will for now call an equilateral hyperbola simply a hyperbola.

Two variables $x$ and $y$ are said to be inversely proportional if their product is constant:
$$xy = k. \tag{1}$$

Thus, equality (1) is, for each $k$, a certain function with a specification domain $x > 0$ and a range of values ​​$y > 0$. The graph of this function, shown in figure 1, is called *one branch of an equilateral hyperbola*; for brevity, we will initially call it simply a hyperbola, not only omitting the word “equilateral,” but also not repeating the words “one branch.”

Our definition is based on the concept of the graph of a function. It is possible, however, to accept another, “purely geometric” formulation: *a hyperbola is the set of fourth vertices $M$ of rectangles of a given area $k$, in which one vertex $O$ coincides with the vertex of a given right angle, and two - $L$ and $N$ - lie on its sides* (see Fig. 1).

The point $O$ is called the *center* of the hyperbola - the origin of this name will be clarified further.

Through each point $M_1$ with coordinates $x_1$ and $y_1$ *) there will pass one (but only one!) hyperbola - precisely the one for which the constant $k$, included in the definition of a hyperbola, is equal to $x_1 y_1$. On figure 2 for red hyperbola $x_1 = 2,4$, $y_1 = 3,5$, $k_1 = 8,4$. If you mentally depict all hyperbolas on a general drawing (that is, hyperbolas for all possible $k$), then they will fill the entire “inside” of the angle $XOY$. On figure 2, in addition to the red hyperbola, several more are depicted: at $k = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5$ and $16$. Thus, hyperbolas are an infinite set, each of them is determined by the value of the parameter $k$ **).

---
*) This is usually denoted as follows: $M_1(x_1, y_1)$.
**) The term *parameter* (from the ancient Greek parametron - measuring) - is used in mathematics in various senses. Most often, this is a quantity that retains a constant value only under the conditions of a given problem.
