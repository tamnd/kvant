Crushed drop

Analogy is born at the crossroads of memory and reflection and sometimes links together images and events that are very distantly related. An unexpected analogy, even a separate or superficial one, born at the right time, can help break the deadlock and illuminate the path to a solution.

Once upon a time, in the late 40s, I participated in experimental work. Its goal was to determine the physical characteristics of a substance that had not previously been studied. Previously, this substance simply did not exist in its pure form; chemists obtained it at the cost of great effort.

At first glance, the problem is not at all new, and it should be solved by marveling at the paths trodden by many researchers who studied the physical characteristics of other substances. Our task, however, was complicated by the fact that we could only experiment with microsopp grains. Each grain weighed about one millionth of a gram, and its size was several ten microns. We were very limited in the number of grains - chemists added them with difficulty.

The group I worked with had to determine the melting point and surface tension of a substance in the liquid phase.

In a conventional “macrosopsis” experiment, changing the melting temperature is easy and simple: a thermometer is immersed in the sample and monitored as its readings change as the sample is heated. the temperature gradually increases. When it reaches a certain value, its growth will stop due to the fact that the heat flowing to the sample begins to be spent not on heating, but on the melting process. This temperature is the melting temperature. When the mass of a grain is one millionth of a gram, it is impossible to insert a thermometer into it, and for

One of the members of our group, who had worked in a literary workshop, proposed a completely unexpected solution to the problem. His memory held a memory that gave birth to an analogy. During the war years,” he said, “I ran a shop simultaneously in several identical electronic crucibles. He loaded them with aluminum ingots and, in order to determine the beginning of melting of the charge in the furnace, without climbing onto its loading platform, vertically installed a length of metal rod in each furnace between the ingots, which was visible above the furnace. At the moment the melting began, the rod tilted - this served as a signal.

This memory prompted an idea with which it was possible to change the melting point of the grain. The experience was as follows. There is a grain on a carefully polished plate. It was covered on top with another plate of a grain, touching the grain and forming an angle with the first plate. This is a heating device, and at the moment when the grain melted, the upper plate was crushed by the forming drop and the angle between the plates shifted abruptly. To register this moment more reliably, a mirror coating was applied to the outer surface of the upper plate and we monitored how the beam reflected from it shifted abruptly. The plastic, changing its position, was like a metal rod that tilted, indicating the beginning of melting. Since the mass of the grain is negligible compared to the mass of the grain plates between which it is sandwiched, the temperature of the grain is equal to the temperature of the plates and,

therefore, changing its scales is simple.

In the experiment described, according to the well-known saying, we were able to try two birds with one stone: to determine, firstly, the melting temperature, and, secondly, the magnitude of the tension surface of the molten substance. The fact is that the upper plate, crushing a fresh heavy drop, turned it into a cake of a certain thickness. No matter how many times the experiment of melting the same grain was repeated, the resulting liquid drop with the weight of the plate was flattened to the same thickness $h$. This value could be reduced by increasing the weight of the top plate. It is easy to understand that further flattening turns into surface tension LIEDX applied to that part of the surface of the flattened drop that borders the air. In our experiments, the substance of the drop practically does not wet quartz (that is why the experiments were carried out with quartz plates) and, therefore, we can assume that the radius of curvature of the free surface is $r = \frac{h}{2}$.

The value of surface tension $\alpha$ can be determined from the condition of equality of the pressure exerted by the plate on the liquid drop $(P_\mu)$ and the Lapas pressure $(P_\pi)$ - excess pressure due to the curvature of the free surface of the drop. If the plate presses on the drop with a force of $F$, and the area of ​​its contact with the flattened drop is $\pi R^2$, then $P_\pi = \frac{F}{\pi R^2}$. Magnitude $P_\pi = \frac{\alpha}{r} = \frac{2\alpha}{h}$. Equating $P_\pi$ to $P_\mu$, we find a formula that can be used to determine the surface tension of a substance:

$$\alpha = \frac{Fh}{2\pi R^2}$$

The magnitude of $h$ and $R$ can be changed with great accuracy, and the force $F$ can be easily determined by knowing the weight of the top plate.

The method of solving the problem facing us, which was suggested by the analogy that arose, was, of course, not the only possible one. In this one, it was possible to come up with other techniques, but we were attracted to it by the unexpectedness of the analogy and... the possibility of defining proverbs about two bunnies.

There is a lot that needs to be studied and explained about this phenomenon. Why does one drop fall, while others freeze, leaning against the glass? Why does the drop leave a trail? And does it always remain?

Before discussing what happens to a djedish drop on a vertical window glass, let us consider the behavior of a drop on a smooth surface of a solid body, which forms a certain angle $\psi$ with the horizon. If there were not a liquid drop on a smooth surface, but, say, a solid cube, the following would happen. Up to a certain value of the angle $\psi$ the cube would not move on the surface, and then, with a further increase in the angle, it would begin to slide along the surface. They talk about this in detail at school during physics lessons, saying that three forces act on the cube: the gravitational force $mg$, the support reaction force $N$ and the friction force $F_{TP}$.

Whether or not the cube will slide along an inclined plane depends on the correspondence between the magnitude of the friction force and the projection of the gravity force on the direction of possible movement of the cube ($mg \sin \psi$):

As the angle $\psi$ increases, the friction force decreases (since $F_{TP}$ is proportional to $N$, and $N$ decreases with increasing angle $\psi$), and the magnitude of the projection of the gravity force increases.

Starting from a certain value $\psi$ (at which $mg \sin \psi = F_{TP}$), with a further increase in the angle, the cube begins to slip.

Now let's return to the drop. Schematically, everything here is the same as in the case of a solid cube: there is a force of gravity, there is also a force similar to the force of friction, only in the case of a drop this force is somewhat different, since the drop does not slip, but flows over the surface. Along the inclined surface, liquid drops from the surface flow into the LOBOV part. In this process, any area of ​​liquid that comes into contact with the surface eventually finds itself no longer needed. The force that is necessary for this is an analogue of the frictional force that acts when a solid cube slides along a hard surface.

To understand what happens on the window glass during aging, it is necessary to determine two competing forces: the projection of the gravitational force in the direction forming an angle $\psi$ ($F_{1}$) with the horizon, and the force necessary to separate the liquid from the solid surface ($F_{2}$) in the region of the solid part of the drop’s movement:

The force $F_{1}$ is related to the angle $\psi$ by a very specific ratio: $F_{1} = mg \sin \psi$ (m is the mass of the drop).

The origin of the force $F_{2}$ is due to the fact that the liquid and the solid body on the surface of which it is located are conjugated to each other by fusions of molecular interaction. This amount of interaction can be characterized by the energy that must be expended in order to separate the liquid from the solid surface along the contact area 1 $cm^2$. Before separation, the energy associated with the liquid-solid interface is $\alpha_{жт}$. After separation of the liquid from the solid, two surfaces are formed: one of them is the free surface of the liquid with energy $\alpha_{жт}$, the second is the free surface of the solid with energy $\alpha_{жт}$.

Thus, the lifting energy of interest per 1 $cm^2$ is equal to $\Delta \alpha = \alpha_{жт} + \alpha_{жт} - \alpha_{жт}$.

Keeping in mind a drop that touches a circle of diameter 2R from the surface of a solid body, the magnitude of the force $F_{2}$ can be calculated following obvious logic. Let us mentally sweep the drop as a whole distance $x$ on the neototor. In this case, work will be performed (or energy expended) equal to the product of the area over which the liquid is separated from the solid body by the value $\Delta \alpha$. It is easy to report that this area is 2Rx and therefore $A = 2R\Delta \alpha x$ work is done. And since the work is equal to the product of the force $F_{2}$ and the path $x$, then $F_{2} = 2R\Delta \alpha$. The question may arise: why is the energy expenditure for separating the solid part of a drop from the surface of a solid body unified and the gain of energy due to the “running” LOBOVY of a part of the drop onto this surface not unified? The fact is that the energy gained during the “running forward” is not used to facilitate the breakaway. It simply dissipates, perhaps due to the heating of the drop. (It is not easier for someone walking through a swamp to throw his right leg out of the quagmire because his left leg easily falls into it at this time.)

In order for a drop to crawl along an inclined surface, the following condition must be met: $F_{1} > F_{2}$, or $mg \sin \psi > 2R\Delta \alpha$.

SPRING DROPS

Many physical phenomena are associated with spring drops. For example, we can consider the pattern of formation of a “droplet” shape, changing in perfection and beauty, prepared from a melting icicle. I can’t explain why, but the shape of the swelling drop seems to me the height of geometric perfection. Only the sphere can compare with it in beauty and logical pattern of form. You can consider the reflections of the sun living on the surface of a drop that swells at the tip of an icicle. The glare fluctuates in the rhythm of the breathing of a swelling drop. You can consider the spring zone, which, according to the poet, accompanies the flight of an icicle from winter to spring. The sound of a drop sounds in many poetic and musical lines, and, of course, one should talk about the acoustics of a drop hitting the surface of water or ice, covered with a layer of water. The drop breaks into small fragments, and each of the fragments adds sound to the spring ringing.
