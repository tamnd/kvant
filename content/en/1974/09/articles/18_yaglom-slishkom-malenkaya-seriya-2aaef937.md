Series too small

We will discuss the mathematical part of the series “Library of the Physics and Mathematics School” published by the mass production company “Nuka” (editor: Corresponding Member of the USSR Academy of Sciences I. M. Gelfand).

To date, 8 books “Library of the Physics and Mathematics School” have been published (editor: Corresponding Member of the USSR Academy of Sciences I. M. Gelfand).

Turning to the contents of the individual books in the series, starting with the easiest-to-solve brochures 1* and 3*: “Mathematical Problems” and “Modern Mathematical Problems.” The very names of these books fully reveal their character: these are simply collections of (rather heterogeneous in topics and methods of solution) problems offered at various competitions in mass specialized “mathematical” schools; at the end of the brochures there are collected solutions (or instructions for solving) all the problems of the “main part” of the books without exception. In addition, each of the books ends with a section “Additional problems”, the solutions to which are not indicated.

The high level of the task and the careful preparation of answers and instructions is guaranteed by the very list of authors, including well-known mathematicians who have been working in “mathematical” schools for many years. To solve most of the problems in issues 1* and 3* of the “Library”, they are carried out quite significantly in the scope of the mathematics course of the first eight years of high school. In terms of the nature of the problems, they are in many ways close to those published in the “Problem Book «Kvant»”, being, however, on average somewhat simpler. The second one seems to me to be the more successful of these two brochures, partly this is explained by the subtitle “Arithmetic and Algebra” of issue 3*, or in general the geometric part of the collection 1* seems to be the most complex.

In this regard, we can only hope that the compiler of the “additional” series of “Libraries” to lovers of geometry LINK sees some “debt” in the continuation of issue 3* and the soon to be published issue 4*: N. B. Vasiliev, S. A. Molchanov, A. L. Rosenal, A. P. Savin, Mathematical modern (geometry).

Let us now move on to the “most basic” part of the series, represented by issues 1, 2 and 5. Despite the fact that here, of course, problems and exercises constitute an essential component of the text (but how can one learn mathematics without problems?), yet the main part of these three issues are educational materials of a theoretical nature. The connection between these three books (especially the first two of them) with the mathematical school existing at Moscow University is obvious: these issues set out the ZMS course, including information of a theoretical nature and “control tasks” offered to students to check the degree of mastery of the material (these tasks formed the basis of the brochures accompanying each section, exercises for independent solution).

Written at a high scientific and methodological level, devoted to the most important issues of a general mathematical nature, these brochures enjoy well-deserved popularity and, undoubtedly, have a significant impact on mathematics-intensive schoolchildren. Named because the author evaluates these brochures quite highly, even we are not talking about their indisputable merits, but about the fact that the author is not completely satisfied with them.

First of all, I am not satisfied with the number of brochures.

A series of books is only worthy of such a name if the books included in it follow one another quite regularly. One can only regret that the information contained in the first editions of issue 2 about the rapid continuation of the topic “Functions and Graphs” (which is indirectly covered in the subtitle “Basic Techniques” of issue 2) was not only not implemented, but even completely disappeared from further editions of this book.

Further, the analogy of the themes of issues 1 and 2 of “Library” seems to be insufficiently emphasized. Essentially, these two books cover two aspects of the applications of the coordinate method. In the first case, the concept of equation $F(x, y) = 0$ or $L$ is at the forefront of the class (transition: $M(x, y) \in L \Leftrightarrow F(x, y) = 0$); in the second, the concept of the graph of the function $y = f(x)$ (reverse transition: $y = f(x) \Leftrightarrow M(x, y) \in \gamma$). These two ideas are consistently revealed in issues 1 and 2 of the Library, but the technical connection is not emphasized enough. Let us note right away that the development of mathematical language is more important for beginners than for experienced mathematicians - and the absence of basic theoretical and logical symbols or their insufficiently free use somewhat binds the authors of issues 1 and 2.

In contrast, in issue 5, which is mainly devoted to a discussion of the very meaning of the expressions “equations”, “system of equations”, “equivalence of an equation”, “solution of an equation (or inequality)”, etc., the corresponding symbols are used quite widely, however, for a schoolchild who consistently studies books from the Library, the lack of habituation to such entries may make it somewhat difficult to understand this brochure.

I would also be inclined to consider the attention of the authors of issue 2 of the Library to the function $y = |x|$ and its various combinations to be somewhat exaggerated.

And in any case, the inclusion of the transition $y = f(x) \Rightarrow y = |f(x)|$, which has a rather particular meaning, among the “basic techniques” for constructing graphs, is an unjustified quality for me.

In the latest editions of Issue 1, the attention to particular types of equations is also somewhat exaggerated (after all, a schoolchild, what the hell, finds it necessary to memorize the “equation in segments on wasps”!). However, along with this, it is difficult not to express my appreciation for, say, the successful treatment theme about the four-dimensional cube in issue 1, the carefully selected examples and problems (in all issues), the multi-dimensional (and rare for Soviet educational or popular science literature) use of fields for figure and drawings, bright inserts on separate sheets of books, methodologically thoughtful use of common road signs - and many others that you will become familiar with an inquisitive schoolboy who wanted to know the books from the “Library Library of the Physics and Mathematics School” not only from the real reception.

So far, nothing has been said about issues 3, 4 and 2*, which can be considered the interval between the “most basic” and “additional” series. The fact is that if issues 1* and 3* can, unfortunately, be called “assigned”, and issues 1, 2 and 5 are “textbooks”, then issues 3, 4 and 2* are, so to speak, “assigned textbooks”, that is, assignments in form, but textbooks in essence. These three small books contain a wealth of serious theoretical material. For example, issue 2* of the Library is devoted to one of the most difficult topics in the school mathematics course - limits. However, this material is presented in the form of collections of problems, accompanied by episodic, albeit quite important, “theoretical” essays and, which, unfortunately, plays an even greater role here - analysis of a number of character examples, or constructing the main features of the concepts and prims under study. This way of presenting mathematics requires considerable skill - and the success of the authors of these books seems great and fundamental. And several specific claims can be made against these books. The nanoscale number is a replacement and is closest in theme to the author of these lines, issue 4, dedicated to geometry. For example, several mathematical symbols could be given here (APV for the intersection of the sets A and B and AUB for tracing them). Further, the “alphabet” of geometric places (component of § 2 of the issue) seems too extensive (figuratively speaking, including the long-outdated “yats” and “zhiztsa”, but it was freely possible to get by with a smaller alphabet).

It was very successful to use both analytical and purely geometric methods for solving problems in one book, but even here it built almost even further, making analytical (that is, algebraic or “coordinate”) approaches completely equal to synthetic (geometric). But the “most important” dissatisfaction with the gray decision is still this: we still have too few excellent books from the “Physics and Mathematics School Library” series!
