The famous story about the days “lost” during Magellan’s steep voyage testifies, in particular, to the fact that sailors, setting off on long voyages, were soon deprived of information about the local time at the point of departure. It was important for them to “keep” this time not only for sentimental reasons - for the convenience of thinking about their home and family. The issue of “storing” dough time is related to the problem of measuring geographic coordinates on board a ship. It is difficult to imagine that it is possible to make long voyages without having reliable methods of measuring coordinates on the open sea. However, historical facts indicate that reliable methods for measuring the height of the sun (based on the height of the rise of the Sun at noon) were available at least in the 16th century, and navigators were not able to measure longitude on the open sea with any accuracy even by the beginning of the 17th century. This problem interested the maritime powers from purely economic messages, and it is not surprising that from the very beginning of the 17th century, the French kings Henry IV and Louis XIV, King Philip II of Spain, the English parliament and the General Staff of Holland, as if in competition, offered huge prizes for the creation of a satisfactory method of measuring longitude.

Although such a method did not exist, the original idea was proposed by Hipparchus: we must take advantage of the fact that the difference in longitude at two points on the globe is proportional to the difference in local time at these points at any fixed point in time. At points whose longitudes differ by 15°, the difference in local time is 1 hour (360°/24 = 15°). Therefore, the task of measuring longitude was to compare the local time on the ship with the local time of some point whose longitude is known. In 1655, the States General of Holland received another project for changing the debt - this time with the help of observations of the height of Lunya. Review of the project was entrusted to Christaan ​​Huygens, the 27-year-old son of an influential member of the State Council. H. Huygens, already known by that time for his scientific achievements, in particular, the discovery of the ring of Saturn, was convinced of the inconsistency of the project, but became interested in the problem itself. After this, Huygens now tries to find a design for a clock suitable for measuring debts. On January 12, 1657, he wrote: “These days I found a new design of a clock, with the help of which time is measured so accurately that there is considerable hope of being able to measure debts with its help, even if they have to be driven by sea.” Huygens himself was good at working with his hands (together with his brother Konstantin, he polished lenses for his telescon), but still he chose to entrust the manufacture of the first copy of the clock to the Hague watchmaker Solomon Koster. On June 16, 1657, the patent of the States General assigned Huygens authorship of the pendulum clock, and in 1658 the brochure “Nogologit” was published describing the invention.

Huygens proposed several technical innovations in the design of clocks, but the main one was the use of the period of oscillation of the pendulum as a measure of time. For the first time, Halley drew attention to the striking stability of the period of free oscillations of the pendulum when the amplitude of the pendulum changes: “The oscillations of the pendulum occur at certain times with such inevitability that it is absolutely impossible to force them to occur at other times except by lengthening or shortening the thread. Another feature, an amazing concept, is that one and the same magnet vibrates with the same frequency or with a very small and almost imperceptibly different frequency, whether the oscillations occur along the largest or smallest friend of the same circle.” Huygens was, of course, familiar with this observation of Gallius, published in the book “Conversations and mathematical proofs concerning two new branches of science, relating to mechanics and local motion.”

The story of this remarkable discovery is described in a letter from Vivanius, a famous student of Gallius, to Prince Leopold Medcici, also a former student of Gallius:

“In 1583, being about a year old, Gallia was in Pisa, where, following the advice of his father, he studied philosophy and medicine. However, being in the cathedral of this city, he, with his characteristic curiosity and ingenuity, decided to observe the movement of the chandelier suspended at the very top - whether the duration of its swings, both along large arcs and along medium and small ones, would not be the same; it seemed to him that the duration of the passage of a large arc could be shortened due to the greater speed to which, as he saw the chandelier moving, on higher and inclined sections. And while the chandelier moved steadily, he made a rough estimate - his general expression - of how the movement back and forth occurs, with the help of the beats of his own pulse, as well as the tempo of the music, in which he was already skilled at that time, to a great extent for himself. And based on such calculations, it seemed to him that he was not mistaken, having calculated that the oscillation times were the same, but not satisfied with this, he returned home, and in order to more reliably verify this, he decided to do the following.”

He tied two lead balls on threads of exactly the same length so that they could swing freely..., and, deflecting them from the vertical by a different number of degrees, for example, one ball by 30, the other by 10, he released them at the same instant. With the help of a tovarash, he observed that while one pendant made such and such a number of oscillations in large arcs, the other made exactly the same number in small arcs.

Huygens decided to take advantage of the fact that the period of oscillation of the pendulum does not change either during the natural attenuation of oscillations from air resistance, or when rocking during rough seas. It turns out that Galileo also suggested using a pendulum to create a clock. But Galileo returned to considering the oscillations of the pendulum after the experiments of 1583 only 50 years later, when in 1634, after the trial of the Inquisition and the renunciation of Copernicus’ “lies,” he decided to record in his “Conversations” his discoveries in mechanics obtained in his youth (the book was published in 1638). On June 5, 1636, in a letter to the administrator of Real, Galileo wrote about connecting a pendulum with a vibration counter. Only in 1641, just a year before his death, did Galileo attend to the practical implementation of the project. But this engineering task was no longer up to the blind old man. He brought its completion to his son Vincenzo, who was not present for work for several years, and died soon after its resumption.

This activity did not receive wide publicity, but Galileo’s students knew about it and, having read Huygen’s brochure, they tried to restore the priority of the teacher. Leopold Medci sends to the influential French astronomer Buyot, who patronized Huygen, a letter about Galileo's priority, Vivani's detailed account of the history of the discovery (we quoted him above) and Galileo's figure watch for transmission to Huygen. Perhaps Huygen was even suspected of plagiarism. Galileo's students knew about negotiations with the States General of Holland regarding the method of measuring geographical debt, which Galileo kept in the strictest secrecy, fearing the Inquisition. However, at the last stage, the Florentine inquisitor learned about his contacts with Holland and got them banned. In conclusion, he reassured Rome that Galileo “would rather lie in a coffin than engage in mathematical constructions.” The negotiations were so secret that Galileo's students did not know what method was proposed by their teacher, and assumed that the reb could be talking about measuring the debt using a pendulum clock, and that Christan Huygens, whose father was a member of the commission that examined Galileo's proposal, could have used the materials sent. We now know that this is not so, and the reb was talking about the use of Jupter shades.

Having familiarized himself with the materials received from Buyot, Geugens concluded that Galileo put forward the idea of ​​a matrix clock, but was far from the technical implementation of the project. It is characteristic that Max Laus in his “History of Physics”, noting that “the decisive step for the creation of a clock in the modern sense was taken by Christan Heugens in 1657,” highlights not the use of the matrix oscillation period as a measure of time, but the idea of “feedback”: in the Heugens clock, for the first time, energy is delivered to the source of oscillation without disturbing the oscillation period, and “the source of oscillation itself determines the moments in time when delivery is required energy." In Heugens, this role is played by a simple and ingenious device in the form of an anchor with cohesive teeth, a rhythmic pushing matrix.

In 1673, Geugens wrote: “Some claim that Galileo is trying to make this invention; but did not complete the matter; these persons rather diminish the glory of Galileo than is possible, since it turns out that I accomplished the same task with greater success than he.” It is impossible to forget that when Galileo was engaged in this task, he was blind and 50 years older than Heuguens.

In general, Heugens, to a greater extent than anyone else, followed the path of Galileo. According to Lagrange, it was up to Heugens to improve and develop Galileo's important discoveries. This connection between the two great scientists had a significant beginning: 17-year-old Geugens was going to prove that thrown bodies move in parabolas, but discovered the proof in the book of Galileo and did not want to “write the Iliad after Homer.”

Geugens, even during the construction of the first clock, noticed that Galileo’s statement about the isochronism of the oscillations of the mathematical matrix (independence of the period $T$ from the maximum angle of deviation from the vertical $\alpha$) is not accurate. This statement holds with good accuracy for small $\alpha$, but is noticeably violated, for example, for $\alpha = 60^\circ$. It is not clear how Galileo overlooked this circumstance: it should have been revealed in the experiments described by Vivania. Geugens noted in a book published in 1673 that the ratio of the period for $\alpha = 90^\circ$ to the period for small friends is 34:29. The accuracy with which this relation was found allows this formula to be used for small $\alpha$. Obtaining a formula for the period of matrix oscillations for any $\alpha$ requires the use of a mathematical apparatus not known in Heugens time. Despite this, Geugens obtained very accurate estimates for the period, and it is not always clear by what means he achieved this *).

Having built the first clock, Heugens became “sick” with the challenge of building accurate clocks that would be reliable in seafaring conditions. Geugens noted that for this purpose it is possible to specialize in a way to take into account the length of the matrix as the deflection angle increases. In the first design, he selected the dependence of the length on the experimental angle, then he managed to find the desired dependence theoretically. As a result, Heugens invented *).

To facilitate understanding, let us make two preliminary remarks.

1. Whatever problem Heugens was working on, he always thought about possible applications of obtaining results to watches. And in this case he wanted to use the Konec matrix. This is the name of a thread with a group that rotates around an axis passing through the suspension point (Fig. 1). Let $l$ be the length of the thread, $\alpha$ the angle of the thread with the vertical, $R$ the distance from the load to the axis. If the magnet moves in a circle and the angle $\alpha$ remains constant, then $\frac{mv^2}{R} = mgtg\alpha$. Hence $v = \sqrt{gRtg\alpha}$. For the period—temporary turnover—we obtain (since $T = 2\pi R/v$):

$$T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{R}{g}}ctg\alpha = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{l\cos\alpha}{g}} = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{u}{g}}.$$ (1)

Here $u = l\cos\alpha$ is the length of the projection of the thread onto the magnet axis.

There are numerous discussions of the formula for the finite matrix period in the Heuguens text. The movement of the finite matrix is ​​compared with two movements that had been thoroughly studied by that time: with a free pad and oscillations of a simple, or mathematical, matrix (Geugens calls its oscillations lateral, in contrast to the circular oscillations of the finite matrix).

Huygens came up with an extremely ingenious method of suspension. The secret of this ingenious method of suspension is based on the same mathematical considerations as the method of suspension of the cyclondal matrix.
