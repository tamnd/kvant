Each of the entries (2)-(8) contains two theorems, direct and converse (and not in all cases both theorems are studied in school). For example, theorem (2) states: for my chain ABCD to be a parallelogram, it is necessary and sufficient that the sides AB and CD are equal and parallel. This formulation contains two theorems. However, one of them is that the chain ABCD is a parallelogram, my AB||CD and AB = CD. It is written like this:
(∀ABCD) (ABCD - par-mm) ⇒ (AB||CD and AB = CD). (9)

Another theorem (the converse) says: if in the chain ABCD we have AB||CD, and AB = CD, then ABCD is a parallelogram. Here is her entry:
(∀ABCD) (AB||CD and AB = CD) ⇒ (ABCD - par-mm). (10)

Both formulations (9) and (10), each of which contains a one-way arrow ⇒ (“if..., then...”), are combined by one entry (2), containing a double-sided arrow ⇔ (“... necessary and sufficient...”). In a similar way, each of theorems (3) - (8) can be divided into two theorems, direct and converse. Most of these theorems are well known to students. The rest (not proven in school) can be considered as proof problems. The reader is advised to recall (or invent) proofs of all these theorems, both original and converse.

It should be noted that the notation of each of theorems (2)—(8) is very similar to the notation of definition (1) - only the designation “Def” is missing. Each of the entries (1)–(8) states that the chain ABCD is a parallelogram if and only if one or another property is satisfied. However, the role of statement (1)–(8) is not the same. The first of them is a definition, that is, it is used for the initial introduction of the concept of a parallelogram. Therefore it does not need proof. Relations (2)–(8) are not definitions, but are theorems and require proof.

Note that we could introduce the concept of a parallelogram in another way; for example, one could take (2) to be the definition of a parallelogram. In this case, correspondence (2) would not need to be proven (it would take place “by definition”), but then statement (1) should already be considered as a theorem, which (as well as the other statements (3)-(8)) should be proven using statement (2).

The division of the definition into three parts (see (1)), although similar to the division of the theorem into three parts (see (2)-(8)), has a slightly different connotation. The first part of the definition indicates the generic concept. In this case, this is the concept of a quadrilateral (that is, parallelograms are distinguished from the set of all chains of quadrangles). The second part of the definition will highlight the name of the new concept (the new term - in this case, “parallelogram”). finally, the third part of the definition contains a description of the specific difference, in this case a description of the property (pairwise parallelism of opposite sides) that distinguishes parallelograms from all chains of artefacts.
