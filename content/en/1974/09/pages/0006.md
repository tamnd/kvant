In 1655, the States General of Holland received another project for changing the debt - this time with the help of observations of the height of Lunya. Review of the project was entrusted to Christaan ​​Huygens, the 27-year-old son of an influential member of the State Council. H. Huygens, already known by that time for his scientific achievements, in particular, the discovery of the ring of Saturn, was convinced of the inconsistency of the project, but became interested in the problem itself. After this, Huygens now tries to find a design for a clock suitable for measuring debts. On January 12, 1657, he wrote: “These days I found a new design of a clock, with the help of which time is measured so accurately that there is considerable hope of being able to measure debts with its help, even if they have to be driven by sea.” Huygens himself was good at working with his hands (together with his brother Konstantin, he polished lenses for his telescon), but still he chose to entrust the manufacture of the first copy of the clock to the Hague watchmaker Solomon Koster. On June 16, 1657, the patent of the States General assigned Huygens authorship of the pendulum clock, and in 1658 the brochure “Nogologit” was published describing the invention.

Huygens proposed several technical innovations in the design of clocks, but the main one was the use of the period of oscillation of the pendulum as a measure of time. For the first time, Halley drew attention to the striking stability of the period of free oscillations of the pendulum when the amplitude of the pendulum changes: “The oscillations of the pendulum occur at certain times with such inevitability that it is absolutely impossible to force them to occur at other times except by lengthening or shortening the thread. Another feature, an amazing concept, is that one and the same magnet vibrates with the same frequency or with a very small and almost imperceptibly different frequency, whether the oscillations occur along the largest or smallest friend of the same circle.” Huygens was, of course, familiar with this observation of Gallius, published in the book “Conversations and mathematical proofs concerning two new branches of science, relating to mechanics and local motion.”

The story of this remarkable discovery is described in a letter from Vivanius, a famous student of Gallius, to Prince Leopold Medcici, also a former student of Gallius:

“In 1583, being about a year old, Gallia was in Pisa, where, following the advice of his father, he studied philosophy and medicine. However, being in the cathedral of this city, he, with his characteristic curiosity and ingenuity, decided to observe the movement of the chandelier suspended at the very top - whether the duration of its swings, both along large arcs and along medium and small ones, would not be the same; it seemed to him that the duration of the passage of a large arc could be shortened due to the greater speed to which, as he saw the chandelier moving, on higher and inclined sections. And while the chandelier moved steadily, he made a rough estimate - his general expression - of how the movement back and forth occurs, with the help of the beats of his own pulse, as well as the tempo of the music, in which he was already skilled at that time, to a great extent for himself. And based on such calculations, it seemed to him that he was not mistaken, having calculated that the oscillation times were the same, but not satisfied with this, he returned home, and in order to more reliably verify this, he decided to do the following.”
