There is a lot that needs to be studied and explained about this phenomenon. Why does one drop fall, while others freeze, leaning against the glass? Why does the drop leave a trail? And does it always remain?

Before discussing what happens to a djedish drop on a vertical window glass, let us consider the behavior of a drop on a smooth surface of a solid body, which forms a certain angle $\psi$ with the horizon. If there were not a liquid drop on a smooth surface, but, say, a solid cube, the following would happen. Up to a certain value of the angle $\psi$ the cube would not move on the surface, and then, with a further increase in the angle, it would begin to slide along the surface. They talk about this in detail at school during physics lessons, saying that three forces act on the cube: the gravitational force $mg$, the support reaction force $N$ and the friction force $F_{TP}$.

Whether or not the cube will slide along an inclined plane depends on the correspondence between the magnitude of the friction force and the projection of the gravity force on the direction of possible movement of the cube ($mg \sin \psi$):

As the angle $\psi$ increases, the friction force decreases (since $F_{TP}$ is proportional to $N$, and $N$ decreases with increasing angle $\psi$), and the magnitude of the projection of the gravity force increases.

Starting from a certain value $\psi$ (at which $mg \sin \psi = F_{TP}$), with a further increase in the angle, the cube begins to slip.

Now let's return to the drop. Schematically, everything here is the same as in the case of a solid cube: there is a force of gravity, there is also a force similar to the force of friction, only in the case of a drop this force is somewhat different, since the drop does not slip, but flows over the surface. Along the inclined surface, liquid drops from the surface flow into the LOBOV part. In this process, any area of ​​liquid that comes into contact with the surface eventually finds itself no longer needed. The force that is necessary for this is an analogue of the frictional force that acts when a solid cube slides along a hard surface.

To understand what happens on the window glass during aging, it is necessary to determine two competing forces: the projection of the gravitational force in the direction forming an angle $\psi$ ($F_{1}$) with the horizon, and the force necessary to separate the liquid from the solid surface ($F_{2}$) in the region of the solid part of the drop’s movement:

The force $F_{1}$ is related to the angle $\psi$ by a very specific ratio: $F_{1} = mg \sin \psi$ (m is the mass of the drop).

The origin of the force $F_{2}$ is due to the fact that the liquid and the solid body on the surface of which it is located are conjugated to each other by fusions of molecular interaction. This amount of interaction can be characterized by the energy that must be expended in order to separate the liquid from the solid surface along the contact area 1 $cm^2$. Before separation, the energy associated with the liquid-solid interface is $\alpha_{жт}$. After separation of the liquid from the solid, two surfaces are formed: one of them is the free surface of the liquid with energy $\alpha_{жт}$, the second is the free surface of the solid with energy $\alpha_{жт}$.

Thus, the lifting energy of interest per 1 $cm^2$ is equal to $\Delta \alpha = \alpha_{жт} + \alpha_{жт} - \alpha_{жт}$.

Keeping in mind a drop that touches a circle of diameter 2R from the surface of a solid body, the magnitude of the force $F_{2}$ can be calculated following obvious logic. Let us mentally sweep the drop as a whole distance $x$ on the neototor. In this case, work will be performed (or energy expended) equal to the product of the area over which the liquid is separated from the solid body by the value $\Delta \alpha$. It is easy to report that this area is 2Rx and therefore $A = 2R\Delta \alpha x$ work is done. And since the work is equal to the product of the force $F_{2}$ and the path $x$, then $F_{2} = 2R\Delta \alpha$. The question may arise: why is the energy expenditure for separating the solid part of a drop from the surface of a solid body unified and the gain of energy due to the “running” LOBOVY of a part of the drop onto this surface not unified? The fact is that the energy gained during the “running forward” is not used to facilitate the breakaway. It simply dissipates, perhaps due to the heating of the drop. (It is not easier for someone walking through a swamp to throw his right leg out of the quagmire because his left leg easily falls into it at this time.)

In order for a drop to crawl along an inclined surface, the following condition must be met: $F_{1} > F_{2}$, or $mg \sin \psi > 2R\Delta \alpha$.
