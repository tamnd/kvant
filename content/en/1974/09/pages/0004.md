The famous story about the days “lost” during Magellan’s steep voyage testifies, in particular, to the fact that sailors, setting off on long voyages, were soon deprived of information about the local time at the point of departure. It was important for them to “keep” this time not only for sentimental reasons - for the convenience of thinking about their home and family. The issue of “storing” dough time is related to the problem of measuring geographic coordinates on board a ship. It is difficult to imagine that it is possible to make long voyages without having reliable methods of measuring coordinates on the open sea. However, historical facts indicate that reliable methods for measuring the height of the sun (based on the height of the rise of the Sun at noon) were available at least in the 16th century, and navigators were not able to measure longitude on the open sea with any accuracy even by the beginning of the 17th century. This problem interested the maritime powers from purely economic messages, and it is not surprising that from the very beginning of the 17th century, the French kings Henry IV and Louis XIV, King Philip II of Spain, the English parliament and the General Staff of Holland, as if in competition, offered huge prizes for the creation of a satisfactory method of measuring longitude.

Although such a method did not exist, the original idea was proposed by Hipparchus: we must take advantage of the fact that the difference in longitude at two points on the globe is proportional to the difference in local time at these points at any fixed point in time. At points whose longitudes differ by 15°, the difference in local time is 1 hour (360°/24 = 15°). Therefore, the task of measuring longitude was to compare the local time on the ship with the local time of some point whose longitude is known.
