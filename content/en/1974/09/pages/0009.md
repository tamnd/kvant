The cyclopdal pendulum, which was first used in a clock in 1659. Huygens had great hopes for a clock with a cyclopdal pendulum, took out patents in different countries, and organized sea trials, which, alas, met with varying degrees of success. The watch performed well in 1663 during Captain Holmes's voyages from London to Lisbon, in 1664 during a voyage from Guinea, and in 1668 on a French squadron sailing near the island of Crete. On the other hand, trials on the voyages of Admiral Richer to Canada and England in 1670, Captain de Graaff to the Cape of Good Hope in 1687 and trials of Huygens himself on the Zuiderzee in 1685 for negative results. The cyclopdal pendulum did not prove its worth in the “land clock” either.
The same fate befell another source of isochronous oscillations proposed by Huygens - a conical pendulum, based on one of Huygens’ most seminal works, “On Centrifugal Force.” However, the mathematical and mechanical results obtained in connection with these inventions became one of the brightest pages in the history of science.

Nevertheless, Huygens managed to find a way in which, although not by himself, a marine chronometer was created (it is believed that the first satisfactory chronometer was designed by Harrison already in the 18th century). In 1675, Huygens proposed the idea of ​​an attached clock with a balance wheel. His last work on clocks was published in 1693, two years before his death.

As for pendulum clocks, it later turned out that it is most convenient to add isochronism by attaching a specially selected attachment to the upper end.

By 1673, Huygens had come up with a large number of conditions for pendulum clocks, and the Parisian watchmaker Isaac Thuret made a copy of the clock, taking into account all the news.

In addition, Huygens put in order the research in mathematics and mechanics undertaken in the process of working on the clock. All this was confirmed in the book “The Pendulum Clock,” published in 1673 in Paris. On the title page it was written: “Christan Huygens, son of Constantine, from Zeilichem. Pendulum clocks, or Geometric proofs, refer to the movement of pendulums fitted to clocks. Paris. In the printing house of F. Muge, printer of the King and His Serene Highness, on Guitar Street, under the sign of the Three Magi. 1673. With the king involved.” Huygens's book, along with Halley's Discourses and Newton's Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy, is one of the three books on which mechanics of the 17th century is based.
