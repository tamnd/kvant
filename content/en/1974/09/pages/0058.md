⟦folio 56⟧

(∀ □ ABCD) (ABCD is a rectangle) ⇔ (the midline is the axis symmetric).

In connection with the last theorem, we note that the midline of a quadrilateral is a straight line connecting the midpoints of its two opposite sides (so that in any quadrilateral there are two midlines). Theorem (20) states that parallelogram ABCD is only a rectangle if one of its midlines serves as its symmetrical axis:

Rule definition:
(∀ ABCD) (ABCD - trapezoid) ⇔ (AB||CD or AD||BC)

requires some clarification. The conjunction “or” has an inseparable meaning in mathematics (as opposed to ordinary speech). In other words, if a mathematical text says that a or b occurs, then three possibilities are allowed: 1) a occurs, but not b; 2) b occurs, but not a; 3) there are o b a conditions of a and b.

Turning now to the definition of a trapezoid, we conclude that a quadrilateral ABCD is considered a trapezoid in the following three cases:

1) AB||CD, but AD||BC (in this case, the bases of the trapezoid will be segments AB and CD);
2) AD||BC, but AB||CD (the bases will be the segments AD and BC);
3) AB||CD and AD||BC (ABCD is a parallelogram).

The theorem that the middle line of a trapezoid is parallel to the bases, and its length is equal to half the lengths of the bases, at first glance has no converse. However, it is easy to break it down into two theorems, each of which contains a necessary and sufficient condition:

(∀ ABCD; M and N - mid-side AB and CD) (ABCD - trapezoid) ⇔
⇔ (MN = \frac{AD + BC}{2});
(∀ ABCD; M and N - mid-side AB and CD) (ABCD - trapezoid) ⇔
⇔ (MN||AD).

Let us also note the theorem about equilateral trapezoids:
(∀ ABCD; AD||BC, AB||CD) (A = D) ⇔ (AB = CD).

Here the indication of AB||CD in the explanatory part is essential, since otherwise the converse theorem will be false.

Let us now recall the theorems on attached and circumscribed quadrilaterals. A polygon is called inscribed (also called “inscribed”) if there is a circle passing through all its vertices. A polygon is called circumscribed if there is a circle touching all its sides. Here are the necessary and sufficient conditions for quadrilaterals to have these properties:

(∀ ABCD) (ABCD - inscribed) ⇔ (A + C = 180°);
(∀ ABCD) (ABCD - described) ⇔ (AB + CD = AD + BC).

In difference in proving theorems:

1. (∀ ABCD; l, m, n, p - bisketrices of angles A, B, C, D) (ABCD - par-mm) ⇔ ⇔ (straight lines l, m, n, p form a rectangle);

2. (∀ ABCD; M, N are the midpoints of the diagonals) (MN = \frac{|AD - BC|}{2}) ⇔ (AD || BC);

3. (∀ ABCD; AB = BC = AC) (ABCD - written) ⇔ (BD = AD + CD);

4. (∀ ABCD; S is its area) (AC ⊥ BD) ⇔ (S = \frac{1}{2} AC \cdot BD);

5. (∀ ABCD; M, N are the midpoints of AD, BC, O is the point of intersection of the diagonals) (AD || BC) ⇔ (MN passes through O).
