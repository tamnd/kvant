⟦folio 21⟧

To reduce the melting temperature, workarounds should be sought.

One of the members of our group, who had worked in a literary workshop, proposed a completely unexpected solution to the problem. His memory held a memory that gave birth to an analogy. During the war years,” he said, “I ran a shop simultaneously in several identical electronic crucibles. He loaded them with aluminum ingots and, in order to determine the beginning of melting of the charge in the furnace, without climbing onto its loading platform, vertically installed a length of metal rod in each furnace between the ingots, which was visible above the furnace. At the moment the melting began, the rod tilted - this served as a signal.

This memory prompted an idea with which it was possible to change the melting point of the grain. The experience was as follows. There is a grain on a carefully polished plate. It was covered on top with another plate of a grain, touching the grain and forming an angle with the first plate. This is a heating device, and at the moment when the grain melted, the upper plate was crushed by the forming drop and the angle between the plates shifted abruptly. To register this moment more reliably, a mirror coating was applied to the outer surface of the upper plate and we monitored how the beam reflected from it shifted abruptly. The plastic, changing its position, was like a metal rod that tilted, indicating the beginning of melting. Since the mass of the grain is negligible compared to the mass of the grain plates between which it is sandwiched, the temperature of the grain is equal to the temperature of the plates and,

therefore, changing its scales is simple.

In the experiment described, according to the well-known saying, we were able to try two birds with one stone: to determine, firstly, the melting temperature, and, secondly, the magnitude of the tension surface of the molten substance. The fact is that the upper plate, crushing a fresh heavy drop, turned it into a cake of a certain thickness. No matter how many times the experiment of melting the same grain was repeated, the resulting liquid drop with the weight of the plate was flattened to the same thickness $h$. This value could be reduced by increasing the weight of the top plate. It is easy to understand that further flattening turns into surface tension LIEDX applied to that part of the surface of the flattened drop that borders the air. In our experiments, the substance of the drop practically does not wet quartz (that is why the experiments were carried out with quartz plates) and, therefore, we can assume that the radius of curvature of the free surface is $r = \frac{h}{2}$.

The value of surface tension $\alpha$ can be determined from the condition of equality of the pressure exerted by the plate on the liquid drop $(P_\mu)$ and the Lapas pressure $(P_\pi)$ - excess pressure due to the curvature of the free surface of the drop. If the plate presses on the drop with a force of $F$, and the area of ​​its contact with the flattened drop is $\pi R^2$, then $P_\pi = \frac{F}{\pi R^2}$. Magnitude $P_\pi = \frac{\alpha}{r} = \frac{2\alpha}{h}$. Equating $P_\pi$ to $P_\mu$, we find a formula that can be used to determine the surface tension of a substance:

$$\alpha = \frac{Fh}{2\pi R^2}$$

The magnitude of $h$ and $R$ can be changed with great accuracy, and the force $F$ can be easily determined by knowing the weight of the top plate.

The method of solving the problem facing us, which was suggested by the analogy that arose, was, of course, not the only possible one. In this one, it was possible to come up with other techniques, but we were attracted to it by the unexpectedness of the analogy and... the possibility of defining proverbs about two bunnies.
