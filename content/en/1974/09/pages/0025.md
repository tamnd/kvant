For simplicity, we assume that on the surface of a horizontal stack the drop has the shape of a hemisphere. Then

$$m = \frac{2}{3} \pi R^3 \rho \approx 2R^3 \rho$$

(ρ is the density of the liquid drops). With this in mind, from the previous correspondence we easily obtain the following result: drops will crawl along the surface of the window stack, the radius of which satisfies the conditions:

$$R > \left( \frac{\Delta\alpha}{\rho g} \right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.$$

The stated messages and simple formulas make it possible to obtain much of what happens while reaching the window glass. Firstly, it becomes clear that a moving drop will leave a trail behind itself provided that the values ​​are $\Delta\alpha > 2\alpha_{ж}$. In this case, it is more advantageous for the drop to mix over the liquid layer left on the glass than to expose the solid surface. We compare the value $\Delta\alpha$ with the value $2\alpha_{ж}$ because when a liquid drop separates from the liquid layer, two liquid surfaces are formed. If the value $\Delta\alpha$ turns out to be less than $2\alpha_{ж}$, the drops will roll down the glass without leaving behind a wet layer.

On dry, or rather, almost dry window glass, drops leave a mark. This means that in the last formula we can write $2\alpha_{ж}$ together with $\Delta\alpha$. For water $\alpha_{ж} = 70 \text{ дин}/\text{см}$, and therefore drops will roll down the window glass, the radius of which is greater than 0.35 cm. The look while walking on the window glass is due to the fact that new drops appear on it all the time. Some of them are new drops, and some are small drops that arose from the disintegrating sludge left by the moving large drops.
