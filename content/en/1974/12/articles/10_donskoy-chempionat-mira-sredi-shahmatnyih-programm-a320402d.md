History of tour chess programs
In 1950, English mathematician Claude Chenon proposed a way to teach a machine to play chess - the idea of ​​composing a chess program. Shenon did not have his own program, but his idea is used in all existing programs.

The first working chess program was created in the late 50s at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology by Bernstein. She played as a starting player.

In 1957, the first computer chess match in history took place - a match between the Soviet program at the Institute of Theoretical and Experimental Physics and the American program at Stanford University. The four-game match ended with a score of 3:1 (2 draws and 2 wins) in favor of the Soviet program.

In 1968, at the IFIP (International Federation of Education and Information) congress, scientists fully developed the possibility of playing chess programs at the master level. All congress participants had the opportunity to experience the chess programming program firsthand - to play a game with the best American chess program - Greenblatt's program. The machine lost a little more often, but Greenblatt attributed this to the fact that mathematicians are always better at chess programs.

At the same congress, a funny event happened. Mathematicians made a bet with chess master D. Levy that in 10 years a program would be created that would beat Levy. In all likelihood, the mathematicians will lose this bet, but in the person of Levi they have received a self-responsible organization of tour guides for chess programs.

Since 1970, a draw of American chess programs has been held annually in America. The Chess-4.0 program has been launched four times already. She has 13 victories, 1 draw and no defeats on her tour account (before the world record).

After-effects, the American scoop fruit served at the same time as a selection for the scoop fruit of the world. The best four American programs - "Chess-4.0", "Tech-2", "Chaos" and "Ostrich" received the right to play in the tournament.

World Championship
During the IFIP competition, the 1st World Championship among chess programs took place in Stockholm.

The Soviet Union was represented by the Kansa program, created at the Institute of Management Problems by G. M. Adelson-Velsky, V. L. Arlazarov, the author of the article, and others.

The parties took place for five consecutive evenings in the concert hall of the Birger Jarl Hotel. Six games were being played at the same time, and the attention of a large audience (200-300 people) moved from one fight to another. In order for the audience not to get bored, the director of the tournament, international master D. Levy, commented on the games all the time, and this commentary was immediately translated into Swedish.

On the stage there were two small computers, six terminals and eleven telephones that connected the hall with different cities in Europe. One such telephone connected the hall with the Institute of Management Problems in Moscow, where Kansa worked.

The tournament was full of surprises and oddities.

In the first game against the author’s program “Franz”, after move 30, “Kanssa” suddenly began to think for a long time, and if she had not put checkmate on move 34, she would probably have run out of time. This is explained by the fact that on the 30th move of “Kanssa” it was midnight in Moscow, and “Kanssa”, without understanding the clock, decided that she had more than 24 hours to think about it. Perhaps it was precisely this slowness that allowed her to find a mating combination.

Surprises continued in the second.type in the game against Tech-2. According to the tour rules, in case of technical problems, you could take a half-hour break to correct them. The author of Tech, tired of the protracted game of the 1st round (it lasted until 3 am), simply overslept the beginning of the round and was exactly half an hour late. This was read to him as a break due to technical problems, and then during the game he had no right to leave his watch on.

The inaccuracy of the author of "Tech" affected the game - he made the wrong move that the machine told him, and noticed it too late. The move was reversed, but this was only for “Kansu” and led her to a mistake. In the end, I had to enter this position into the machine again and replay the game.

Technical errors of our opponents have become an unkind tradition. In the third.type, after Kansa’s victory was declared in the game “Kanssa” with “Chaos”, the authors of “Chaos” announced that they had made a mistake when entering “Kanssa’s” penultimate move into their machine. They asked permission to replay the game from this move, but they didn’t expect that we would accept this offer, especially after Levi said that if Kansa’s machine breaks down during replay, then there will be no process. Nevertheless, after consultation with Arlazarov, we decided to continue this

When he saw that “Kanssa” knew this opening, he literally grabbed his head, since “Ostrich” did not have a single sequel in its library.

Nevertheless, in this game, as in others, the main drawback of our program was reflected - the incompatibility of the ideas laid down in the debut reference book and the ideas laid down in the game part of the program.

As a result, Kansa started with a loss of exchange. When, succumbing to panic, I considered that in order to replay for first place we needed to hold out for 48 moves, Arlazarov’s firm voice from Moscow declared: “We will win this game anyway,” although nothing would be enough to win the tournament. And indeed, by eleven o'clock at night things were approaching a resolution. "Kanssa", having won back two pawns, led her pawns to queens and ended the game, as always, with an elegant checkmate.

The organizers of the tournament were only dissatisfied with the fact that the best programs of the tournament - “Kanssa” and “Chess-4.0” did not play with each other.

Therefore, we decided to play a friendly game. This time, the owner came out of the opening not only with a premium, but simply in a won position - an endgame with two extra passed pawns. There had already been a proposal in case of “Kansa” losing quickly to play another game, changing the color of the pieces, but “Kansa” surprised again - she managed not only to win back the pawns, but also to win a piece. However, this premium was not enough to win.

After each round, the participants gathered in a “peaceful” corner of the hall and calmly discussed the scientific results of the tour and the development of chess programming. These discussions were very useful for the participants.

When we parted, we agreed to meet at the next IFIP congress in 1977 in the Canadian city of Toronto.
