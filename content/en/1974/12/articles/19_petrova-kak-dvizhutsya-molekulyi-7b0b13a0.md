How do molecules move?

T. S. Petrova

In 1827, the English botanist Brown discovered, through microscopy observations, the random movement of molecules suspended in a liquid. This movement did not stop, no matter how long the observation was carried out. The reason causing this movement, which later became known as Brownian movement, remained mysterious for a long time. They tried to explain to him that the vessel with the liquid would cooperate; it was assumed that this movement is a consequence of chemical reactions occurring in the vessel; they associated this phenomenon with uneven heating of the liquid under the influence of light, etc. However, all attempts at this kind of explanation turned out to be unsuccessful.

At the end of the 19th century, physicists expressed the idea that Brownian motion, which can be observed not only in liquids, but also in gases, is caused by the random thermal movement of the molecules of the liquid or gas. Almost 80 years passed after this motion was discovered before the question of its cause was finally resolved: the motion of the Brownian parts is suspended by the impacts of the molecules of the substance in which these parts are suspended. The establishment of this fact played a huge role in confirming and developing the well-established molecular-kinetic theory that the molecules of matter are constantly moving chaotically.

Numerous experiments related to the observation of Brownian motion made this theory “visual” and undeniable.

What can be said about the characteristics of molecular motion?

According to the kinetic theory of gas molecules, their chaotic thermal motion describes igzag trajectories, which are a collection of a huge number of straight segments. On each such segment, the movement of molecules occurs due to inertia, and such a segment ends where a molecule collides with another molecule or with the wall of a vessel.

Direct confirmation of this picture of the movement of molecules was obtained in the experiment of the French physicist Dunaye, carried out in 1911. The diagram of this experiment led to figure 1. The glass tube is divided into three compartments by partitions with small holes, which are located on the same straight line. The air has been pumped out of the tube. In compartment A is a piece of sodium metal. This part of the tube is heated, and the fusible sodium begins to evaporate. Sodium vapor fills compartment A. After some time, a sodium coating forms on the walls in compartments B and C. In compartment B, it covers the walls of the tube and the septum cc′, and in compartment C, plaque forms only near point O′. The explanation for this picture is very indo.

How can Brownian motion be related to the chaotic motion of molecules? Of course, the trajectories of gas molecules cannot be identified with the trajectories of Brownian particles. After all, the mass of such a particle is many times greater than the mass of an individual molecule, the speed of the particle is very small compared to the speed of the molecules, and its movement is the result of the collision of a huge number of molecules with it.

The above words “chaotic movement” seem familiar. But in this case they will start asking questions. Indeed, chaotic means disorderly. But in a chaotic movement, one of the directions cannot be considered predominant; all directions have equal rights. Why do Brownian particles still move? The fact is that their sizes are very small (although huge compared to the sizes of molecules), they are of the order of $10^{-4}-10^{-5}$ cm. At each moment, the particle receives many impacts from the liquid or gas molecules surrounding it. The larger the particle, the more molecules collide with it at each moment, the more likely it is that the forces of opposite directions will balance, accumulate each other, and the particle remains motionless (Fig. 2). The smaller the particle, the smaller the total number of collisions with molecules, the more likely it is that in their random “jumping” onto the particle, some molecules will “miss”, and as a result, the equilibrium of the particle will be upset - it begins to move. Thus, Brownian motion indeed confirms the existence of random motion of molecules.
