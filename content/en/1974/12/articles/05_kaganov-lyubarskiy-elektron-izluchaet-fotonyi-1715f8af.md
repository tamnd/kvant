Classical radiation theory

The existence of an electromagnetic field in nature is a long-established fact. A charged particle placed in an electromagnetic field begins to move at an accelerated rate. Consequently, it is influenced by the electromagnetic field. The field, accelerating the charge, does work on it, but itself loses a certain amount of energy - the field is weak, that is, it acts on a unit charge with less force. On the contrary, if the field slows down the charge, it increases. In other words, a new field created by the charge is added to the original field. The weakening of the field can also be considered as the addition of a new field created by the charge, but the direction of the additional field is opposite to the direction of the first.

Experiments have shown that the field created by a charge depends on the magnitude of the charge and on the characteristics. The cause that causes the charge to move is a complete non-existence. To calculate the field created by a moving charge, you need to solve a system of differential equations (these are the replaced Maxwell equations).

What is important for us now is the fact itself: a charge moving with rooting can change the energy of the electromagnetic field. This phenomenon is called radiation. Note that the well-known Coulomb's law, by virtue of which the field of a stationary charge can be calculated once and again at any point in space, shows that a stationary charge does not radiate. In fact, the field of such a charge does not change over time, that is, the energy of the field does not change.

Let's conduct a thought experiment. Let's take a stationary charge. Let's make him describe a circle, return to the starting point and stay. What will the electromagnetic field pattern be? At the first stage, when the charge is motionless, it creates a general electrostatic field. During the second stage, a stationary field is created. Will it disappear after the charge stops? No, it won't disappear! But excuse me, the field around a stationary charge must be electrostatic. Is this really wrong?

True, but not entirely. The additional electromagnetic field created at the second stage begins to deform, so that t seconds after the charge stops, a Coulomb field is established inside a ball of radius ct (c is the speed of light) (Fig. 1). Outside this ball, the field will differ significantly from the Coulomb field. So, the radiation field quickly moves away from the place where it was created, giving way to the Coulomb field to more and more new areas of space. Let us emphasize that this property is to “escape” at the speed of light from the place of ignition (and carry away

There is another important case when moving charges do not emit. Let us consider a direct current flowing along some curved circuit. It is well known from experiment and theory that such a current creates a constant magnetic field, but does not radiate. But every current is the movement of charged particles. Planetary model atom and radiation

Temporarily ignoring the quantum nature of atomic particles, we will describe the simplest atom in the language of classical physics - a hydrogen atom, consisting of one proton and one electron.

The electron rotates around the proton in a circle of radius $r_B^*$ (Fig. 2), so that the force causing centripetal acceleration, $mv^2/r_B$, is equal to the Coulomb force of attraction $e^2/r_B^2$ (m is the mass of the electron):

$$\frac{mv^2}{r_B} = \frac{e^2}{r_B^2}.$$

From here we can express the speed of rotation of the electron

$$v^2 = \frac{e^2}{mr_B}.$$ (2)

The greater the speed of an electron, the smaller the distance between it and the proton.

The total energy of an electron in a hydrogen atom is the sum of its kinetic energy $E_{kин} = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 = \frac{e^2}{2r_B}$ and pseudospatial energy $E_{пот} = -e^2/r_B$:

$$E = \frac{e^2}{2r_B} - \frac{e^2}{r_B} = -\frac{e^2}{2r_B}.$$ (3)

Classical physics cannot explain the fact that all hydrogen atoms are the same size. For now we will simply accept, considering this an experimental factor, that all hydrogen atoms have $r_B \approx 10^{-10} m$.

The centripetal acceleration of an electron when moving in a circle is equal to

$$a = \frac{v^2}{r_B} = \frac{e^2}{mr_B^2}.$$

But if an electron moves with acceleration, it radiates. What is the intensity of this radiation, is it small or large? Let's estimate it by substituting the acceleration value $a$ into formula (1):

$$I = \frac{2e^2}{3c^3} \left( \frac{e^2}{mr_B^2} \right)^2 = \frac{2e^6}{3c^3m^2r_B^4}.$$

Electron emits phototons

How does quantum mechanics dictate why the electron does not fall onto the nucleus?

First of all, it should be emphasized that in order to bind the properties of the microworld, it was necessary to create a fundamentally new science: it turned out to be impossible to correct classical mechanics so that it would bind the properties of atoms and molecules. The main novelty of quantum mechanics is taking into account the unity of wave and corpuscular properties of microethics.

According to the strict conclusions of quantum mechanics, the energy of an electron can take not any, but only discrete values. Energy values ​​in a hydrogen atom

$$E_n = -\frac{2\pi^2 me^4}{h^2} \frac{1}{n^2},$$

where $n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$

Among all the electron states in a hydrogen atom, there is one special state - the ground state with the lowest energy. It corresponds to $n=1$. An electron cannot have energy less than $E_1 = -2\pi^2 me^4/h^2$. This prohibition is associated with the wave properties of the electron. It is so strict that it ensures an infinite lifetime of the atom.

So, an electron in its ground state does not emit electromagnetic waves (photons)*. If the electron is in one of the excited states with $n > 1$ ($n = 2, 3, 4, \dots$), it can go to a state with a lower energy, emitting an electromagnetic wave. Like any process in nature, the radiation process obeys the law of conservation of energy. It is easy to write if you use the correspondence connecting the frequency of the electromagnetic wave $v$ with the energy of the photon

$$E_n - E'_n = hv,$$

where $n, n' = 1, 2, 3, \dots; n > n'$.

*) Not only the hydrogen atom, but also any other atom does not emit energy if it is in the ground state.

From here

$$v = \frac{E_n - E'_n}{h}.$$

For hydrogen

$$v = \frac{2\pi^2 me^4}{h^3} \left( \frac{1}{n'^2} - \frac{1}{n^2} \right).$$

Formulas (5) and (6) determine the spectrum (set) of frequencies that a hydrogen atom will emit or absorb.

Quantum mechanics makes it possible to determine not only the frequency spectrum of radiation and absorption, but also the intensity of radiation.

The formulas of quantum mechanics have one interesting feature. They turn into the formulas of classical physics when the “quantum” is negligible, that is, when the movement is almost classical. (This important circumstance even received a special name - the correspondence principle.) In the case we are considering, the smallness of the quantum effectives is manifested in the fact that for values ​​of $n$ tending to infinity, $\Delta E = E_n - E_{n-1}$ tends to zero. After all, in classical physics, energy can have any meaning! We have given only one example, but any formula of quantum mechanics can be verified by the principle of composition. It’s better to say it differently: any formula of classical physics is the limiting value of a quantum formula. Quantum mechanics did not cancel classical mechanics, but indicated its place (and a very honorable place). Classical mechanics is valid when we are quantum efficient, and valid to the accuracy that quantum mechanics allows.

acceleration. But there is one exception to this rule. An electron moving at a constant speed, but not in emptiness, but in some medium, emits light. This effect is called Vavilov-Cherenkov radiation.

The words “Cherenkov radiation”, “Cherenkov radiation”, “Cherenkov effect”, as they are called, are known to many people. And many people know that such radiation occurs when the charged part of the movement with a speed of $v$ is greater than the speed of light in the medium surrounding the particle. Superluminal radiation was discovered by P. A. Cherenkov, a student of S. I. Vavilov, and was announced by the theoretical physicists I. E. Tamm and I. M. Frank. For this work, Cherenkov, Tamm and Frank were awarded the 1958 Nobel Prize in Physics.

The speed of light in a medium with a refractive index $\varepsilon$ (ε is the dielectric constant of the medium) is $u = c/1/\varepsilon$. Since $\varepsilon > 1$, the speed of light in the medium $u$ is less than the speed of light in vacuum $c$. This allows a particle (such as an electron) to fly through a medium at "superluminal" speed $v$:

$$\frac{c}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}} = u < v < c.$$

Condition for the occurrence of Cherenkov radiation

$$v > \frac{c}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}$$

remarkable in that it does not contain Planck's constant. This means (according to the correspondence principle) that it can be obtained by means of both quantum and classical physics. It is interesting to note that the quantum and classical descriptions of the Cherenkov effect lead to practically identical results. But we will limit ourselves only to the quantum picture, since a complete classical Cherenkov theory requires a complex mathematical apparatus.

In this case, we will need additional information about photons.

1. The photon has not only a certain energy $E$, but also a certain momentum $p$. The direction of the pulse can be any, and the magnitude is determined by the energy:

$$p = \frac{1}{c} E.$$

2. The electromagnetic field in a medium with a dielectric constant ε also consists of photons. Photons in a dielectric differ from photons in a vacuum in that the relationship between their energy and momentum contains the dielectric constant ε:

$$p = \frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{c} E = \frac{1}{u} E,$$

$$E = p \frac{c}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}.$$

The relationship between energy and frequency for photons in a medium is the same as for photons in emptiness: $E = hv$.

3. Any interaction of photons with other particles obeys the law of conservation of energy and the law of conservation of momentum.

In theory, consider an electron moving in a medium with a constant speed $v$. Let at some moment of time it emits a photon of particle $v$ with momentum $p$. Let's denote the electron radiation pulse $P$, and then $P'$. Then the energy of electron radiation is $E = P^2/2m$, and after radiation - $E' = P'^2/2m$. According to the law of conservation of momentum $P' = P - p$, and the law of conservation of energy can be written as

$$\frac{P^2}{2m} = \frac{P'^2}{2m} + \frac{c}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}} p.$$

Let's check whether this equality can always hold. Let us introduce the angle $\theta$ between the momentum of the particle before radiation and the momentum of the photon. The cosine of this angle is found from the law of conservation of momentum, written in scalar form:

$$P'^2 = P^2 + p^2 - 2Pp \cos\theta$$

(we used the cosine theorem). From here

$$\cos \theta = \frac{P^2 - P'^2 + p^2}{2Pp} = \frac{c}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}} \left( 1 + \frac{p}{2mc} \sqrt{\varepsilon} \right),$$

where $v$ is the speed of the emitting particle. Let us remember that $p = \frac{hv}{c} \sqrt{\varepsilon}$.

Therefore, $\frac{p \sqrt{\varepsilon}}{2mc} = \frac{hv\varepsilon}{2mc^2}$.

This is the only dimensionless combination of parameters in formula (8), which includes Planck’s constant. It makes it possible to assess the degree of quantumness of a phenomenon. For visible light

$$v \approx 10^{14} c^{-1}, \varepsilon \geq 1$$

and $$\frac{\varepsilon h v}{2mc^2} \approx 10^{-7}$$.

This shows that, without making a big mistake, we can simply neglect the second term in the numerator of formula (8). Therefore, the classical consideration also leads to the correct result.

Since cosθ must be less than unity, we can derive conditions (7): having a particle, lemma with a constant speed, a model emitter of light waves, its speed must exceed the speed of light in the medium.

The above reasoning explains why an electron with a speed of $v < c/\sqrt{\varepsilon}$ does not radiate and why the cone of possible directions of radiation (in the case of $v > c\sqrt{\varepsilon}$) is determined by formula (8). Calculating the intensity of Cherenkov radiation is much more complicated, and we will not bother with it.

The classical meaning of condition (7) is very simple: the particle “runs away” from its created electromagnetic field, which propagates independently in the form of waves.

Approximate (from the classical point of view - exact) equality

$$\cos \theta = \frac{c}{v\sqrt{\varepsilon}}$$

shows the direction of the emitted waves.

Let us think again about what the choice of formula (9) and the conditions for Cherenkov radiation (7) are based on. Firstly, on the laws of conservation of energy and momentum in the process of radiation; secondly, on the fact that the photon energy and the magnitude of its momentum are linearly related, and, finally, thirdly, on the smallness of the quantum correction. If I had decided not about the electron wave, but about the sound wave, then all these “firstly”, “secondly” and “thirdly” were exactly fulfilled. For a sound wave, its energy quantum - phonan and the magnitude of its impulse are related by a linear relationship

$$p = \frac{1}{s} E,$$

where $s$ is the speed of sound. But since the speed of sound is significantly less than the speed of light, the conditions for Cherenkov radiation $v > s$ can be satisfied even when moving at the maximum speed of light.

An example of Cherenkov sound radiation is the click of a whip, the tip of which, in skillful hands, moves at supersonic speed.

Cherenkov radiation of light has found important applications in experimental physics. But this is a separate large and interesting topic. For us, please, it is important that Cherenkov radiation is a very general phenomenon. Whenever a particle moves at a speed exceeding the speed of waves in a given medium, it radiates.
