How do molecules move?

T. S. Petrova

In 1827, the English botanist Brown discovered, through microscopy observations, the random movement of molecules suspended in a liquid. This movement did not stop, no matter how long the observation was carried out. The reason causing this movement, which later became known as Brownian movement, remained mysterious for a long time. They tried to explain to him that the vessel with the liquid would cooperate; it was assumed that this movement is a consequence of chemical reactions occurring in the vessel; they associated this phenomenon with uneven heating of the liquid under the influence of light, etc. However, all attempts at this kind of explanation turned out to be unsuccessful.

At the end of the 19th century, physicists expressed the idea that Brownian motion, which can be observed not only in liquids, but also in gases, is caused by the random thermal movement of the molecules of the liquid or gas. Almost 80 years passed after this motion was discovered before the question of its cause was finally resolved: the motion of the Brownian parts is suspended by the impacts of the molecules of the substance in which these parts are suspended. The establishment of this fact played a huge role in confirming and developing the well-established molecular-kinetic theory that the molecules of matter are constantly moving chaotically.

Numerous experiments related to the observation of Brownian motion made this theory “visual” and undeniable.

What can be said about the characteristics of molecular motion?

According to the kinetic theory of gas molecules, their chaotic thermal motion describes igzag trajectories, which are a collection of a huge number of straight segments. On each such segment, the movement of molecules occurs due to inertia, and such a segment ends where a molecule collides with another molecule or with the wall of a vessel.
