2. Let us describe a circle around the triangle $ABC$ and, taking an arbitrary point $D$ on $AB$, continue the segment $CD$ until it intersects the circle at point $E$ (see Fig. 4). We get $CD \cdot DE = AD \cdot DB$.

From this we conclude that the value $CD$ will be the geometric mean of the values ​​$AD$ and $DB$ if and only if the point $D$ bisects the segment $CE$. This means that finding a point $D$ using is equivalent to finding a point $E$ on a circle such that $CD = DE$, which is possible if and only if $CF \leq HG$, where $CF$ is the height of the triangle $ABC$, $HG$ is the height of the segment $AHB$. It is easy to calculate that if $r$ is the radius of the circumscribed circle, then $CF = 2r\sin\alpha \cdot \sin\beta$, and $HG = r - r\cos\gamma = 2r\sin^2\frac{\gamma}{2}$.

This directly implies the statement of the problem.

3. Let us introduce the notation

$$A_m = \sum_{k=0}^{k+1} C_m^{2k+1} \cdot 2^{3k}, B_m = \sum_{k=0}^{k+1} C_m^{2k} \cdot 2^{3k}$$

When $m$ is odd, greater than 1, $A_m$ gives the sum appearing in the problem statement. It is easy to check that for $m \geq 1$ $A_m + B_m = A_{m+1}$ and $8A_m + B_m = B_{m+1}$, and $A_1 = B_1 = 1$.

Let us now show by induction that $A_m$ when $m$ is odd, and $B_m$ when $m$ is even, is not divisible by 5. For all $m \leq 3$ this is true, since $A_1 = 1$, $A_3 = 11$, $B_2 = 9$. Let for some $i > 3$ this be true for all $m \leq i$. Let us check that then this is true for all $m \leq i + 1$. In fact, the recurrence relations written above give

$$A_{m+3} = 5(5A_m + 2B_m) + B_m,$$
$$B_{m+3} = 5(17A_m + 5B_m) + 3A_m.$$

From here we get what we want to prove.
