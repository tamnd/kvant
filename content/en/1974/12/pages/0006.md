Theorems 1 and 2 give qualitative laws for changes in speed, that is, they show how it will change over time. For example, let a car drive downhill for some time and then drive onto a horizontal section of the road (Fig. 3). Moving downhill, the car with the engine turned off accelerates and enters the horizontal section at an increased speed (greater than $V$). What will happen next? According to Theorem 1, during the entire further movement the speed will be greater than $V$, and, by virtue of Theorem 2, it will gradually decrease, getting closer and closer in the horizontal section will not be uniform, and only in the limit (at $t \to \infty$) will we obtain uniform movement (so speed $v = V$).

A similar picture is observed if the driver (moving, say, at a speed of 60 km/h) intends to increase the speed. To this end, he presses his right foot harder on the accelerator pedal, thereby increasing the octoro, and therefore the force $F$ developed by the engine. The new value of the force $F$ corresponds to a new value of the magnitude $V$, say, $V = 80$ km/h. At the moment you press the pedal, the car moves at a speed of 60 km/h, that is, at a speed less than a neck $V$. According to Theorem 1, during the entire further movement the speed will be less than $V$, and, by virtue of Theorem 2, it will increase increasingly closer to the horizontal section with an increased speed (greater than $V$). We see that in this case the movement of the car will not be uniform, and only in the limit (at $t \to \infty$) will uniform movement be obtained (so speed $v = V$).

In both examples considered, the car moves at a variable speed, which gradually approaches the limit value $V$. Thus, what we usually think of as "uniform motion" is a complex range of processes. The forces acting on the car, theoretically speaking, do not balance each other (the movement occurs at a variable speed, and therefore the acceleration is not zero). And only in the limit (at $t \to \infty$) does the speed approach a constant value $v = V$, the acceleration tends to zero, and the acting forces more and more accurately balance each other (that is, an equal tendency to zero). And this “gradual balancing” is not at all connected with the actions of the driver (after all, he only maintains a constant value of the force $F$), but follows from the very specification of the problem, that is, mathematically speaking, it is a consequence of equation (*).
