⟦folio 25⟧

The position of the “bunny” could not be controlled from such a mirror. To make a mirror controllable by organems, it is necessary to provide for the possibility of changing its focal length, and the time for such adjustment should be very short. Was it possible at that time to build such a complex device? Apparently not. However, Archimedes had another opportunity: to line up several thousand people with mirrors on the fortress wall (it is enough to place 1,400 people with mirrors of 0.5 m² each), who would direct the “bunnies” from their mirrors to a predetermined place on one of the ships. If the design of the walls of Syracuse allows this to be done (which is quite possible, since Archimedes himself took part in their construction) and if the mirrors were prepared in advance, then all this could be done quite quickly. Of course, if the Romans knew why thousands of people with mirrors were coming out to the walls of Syracuse and how this threatened them, then they, shooting from the galleys, could have dispersed the unarmed townspeople. But they didn't know! After the first ship caught fire, the rest could have left, but it is very likely that panic arose - after all, people saw what. So the inhabitants of Syracuse could freshen up several ships and retire under the protection of the fortress walls. Of course, this is only a hypothesis, but there is nothing physically impossible in it.

To set fire or not to set fire?

In the user “Around the World” (No. 12, 1974), a message was sent about another verification of the legend, according to which Archimedes szerfered the Roman fleet entering the harbor of Syracuse.

This legend was supported by the change, since the technical technology of that time did not allow the construction of a collecting mirror of such a size that the light flux directed by it could set fire to ships at a sufficiently large distance. It was believed that this was a legend composed by Greek storytellers.

But if you cannot build one large mirror, then you can take several small mirrors. In December 1973, Greek physicist Jonas Sakkos tested this hypothesis experimentally, not in Syracuse, but in the port of Athens. 70 soldiers and a copy of a Roman wooden ship were placed at his disposal. Each of the soldiers was given a polished sheet of copper measuring 150x90 cm, attached to a holder. The soldiers must direct the reflected sunlight to one place on board the ship, located 200 m from the shore.

After several attempts, we managed to get everything in one place on board the ship. Two seconds later the ship began to smoke, and three seconds later it burst into flames. A minute passed, and the remains of the ship disappeared under water.

So a December day in 1973 brought a new victory to Archimedes.
