The life of Dunoyer clearly shows that gas molecules move in all possible directions and that in the absence of collisions their movement is rectilinear.

How can Brownian motion be related to the chaotic motion of molecules? Of course, the trajectories of gas molecules cannot be identified with the trajectories of Brownian particles. After all, the mass of such a particle is many times greater than the mass of an individual molecule, the speed of the particle is very small compared to the speed of the molecules, and its movement is the result of the collision of a huge number of molecules with it.

Fig. 2.

The above words “chaotic movement” seem familiar. But in this case they will start asking questions. Indeed, chaotic means disorderly. But in a chaotic movement, one of the directions cannot be considered predominant; all directions have equal rights. Why do Brownian particles still move? The fact is that their sizes are very small (although huge compared to the sizes of molecules), they are of the order of $10^{-4}-10^{-5}$ cm. At each moment, the particle receives many impacts from the liquid or gas molecules surrounding it. The larger the particle, the more molecules collide with it at each moment, the more likely it is that the forces of opposite directions will balance, accumulate each other, and the particle remains motionless (Fig. 2). The smaller the particle, the smaller the total number of collisions with molecules, the more likely it is that in their random “jumping” onto the particle, some molecules will “miss”, and as a result, the equilibrium of the particle will be upset - it begins to move. Thus, Brownian motion indeed confirms the existence of random motion of molecules.
