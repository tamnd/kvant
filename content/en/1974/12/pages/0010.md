Let us note one important circumstance. Since the electromagnetic field of radiation takes away with it all the energy received from the moving charge, the energy density of such a field, that is, the energy arriving per unit volume, decreases in inverse proportion to the volume of the spherical layer in which it is located. What waves does a charged particle moving with acceleration emit? The frequency of the emitted wave depends on the nature of the particle's motion. If the frequency of a perfect oscillation is with a frequency of $v_0$, then the frequency of the emitted waves will be equal to $v_0$.

Classical electrodynamics (Maxwell's equations) makes it possible to calculate the radiation intensity, that is, the amount of energy emitted per unit time. So, for example, the radiation intensity of a charge $e$ moving with acceleration $a$ is equal to

$$I = \frac{2e^2a^2}{3c^3}.$$

In the SGSE system, the radiation intensity can be calculated with an accuracy of a factor of 2/3, if you know what it depends on. In this system, all units are expressed through three basic ones: length, mass and time. (In the SI system there are four basic units - an additional unit of current is added. Therefore, electrostatic units are not expressed in terms of mechanical units, and such consideration cannot be carried out.)

The magnitude of the radiation intensity is determined by the charge $e$, which has dimension $(e^{1/2} \cdot cm^3/2 \cdot c^{-1})$, its acceleration $a$ $(cm \cdot c^{-2})$ and the speed of light $c$ $(cm \cdot c^{-1})$. From these quantities, only one combination can be constructed, having dimension $(\partial p \cdot c^{-1})$, which leads to expression (1). Try checking this out.
