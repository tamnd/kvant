6. Let us first assume that each of the polynomials $P(x)-1$ and $P(x)+1$ has at least three different integer roots (and they are obviously different from the roots of the other). Of these integers, we take the smallest and denote it by $a$. Without loss of generality, one may assume that $a$ is the root of the polynomial $P(x)+1$. Then the representation $P(x)+1 = (x-a)Q(x)$ is valid, where $Q(x)$ is also a polynomial with integer coefficients. Let $p, q, r$ be three distinct integer roots of the polynomial $P(x)-1$, all of which are greater than $a$. But $P(x)-1 = (x-a) \times Q(x)-2$. Hence $2 = (p-a) \cdot Q(p) = (q-a) \cdot Q(q) = (r-a) \cdot Q(r)$, where $p-a, q-a, r-a$ are distinct positive integers. But then at least one of them is greater than 2, which obviously leads to a contradiction.

Thus, our assumption is incorrect, and it means that at least one of the equations $P(x)=1$ and $P(x)=-1$ has a number of integer roots less than or equal to 2. From here and from the fact that the number of roots of each of these equations does not exceed deg($P$), the inequality being proved follows.
