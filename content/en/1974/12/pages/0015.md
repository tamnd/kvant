Therefore, $\frac{p \sqrt{\varepsilon}}{2mc} = \frac{hv\varepsilon}{2mc^2}$.

This is the only dimensionless combination of parameters in formula (8), which includes Planck’s constant. It makes it possible to assess the degree of quantumness of a phenomenon. For visible light

$$v \approx 10^{14} c^{-1}, \varepsilon \geq 1$$

and $$\frac{\varepsilon h v}{2mc^2} \approx 10^{-7}$$.

This shows that, without making a big mistake, we can simply neglect the second term in the numerator of formula (8). Therefore, the classical consideration also leads to the correct result.

Since cosθ must be less than unity, we can derive conditions (7): having a particle, lemma with a constant speed, a model emitter of light waves, its speed must exceed the speed of light in the medium.

The above reasoning explains why an electron with a speed of $v < c/\sqrt{\varepsilon}$ does not radiate and why the cone of possible directions of radiation (in the case of $v > c\sqrt{\varepsilon}$) is determined by formula (8). Calculating the intensity of Cherenkov radiation is much more complicated, and we will not bother with it.

The classical meaning of condition (7) is very simple: the particle “runs away” from its created electromagnetic field, which propagates independently in the form of waves.

Approximate (from the classical point of view - exact) equality

$$\cos \theta = \frac{c}{v\sqrt{\varepsilon}}$$

shows the direction of the emitted waves.

Let us think again about what the choice of formula (9) and the conditions for Cherenkov radiation (7) are based on. Firstly, on the laws of conservation of energy and momentum in the process of radiation; secondly, on the fact that the photon energy and the magnitude of its momentum are linearly related, and, finally, thirdly, on the smallness of the quantum correction. If I had decided not about the electron wave, but about the sound wave, then all these “firstly”, “secondly” and “thirdly” were exactly fulfilled. For a sound wave, its energy quantum - phonan and the magnitude of its impulse are related by a linear relationship

$$p = \frac{1}{s} E,$$

where $s$ is the speed of sound. But since the speed of sound is significantly less than the speed of light, the conditions for Cherenkov radiation $v > s$ can be satisfied even when moving at the maximum speed of light.

An example of Cherenkov sound radiation is the click of a whip, the tip of which, in skillful hands, moves at supersonic speed.

Cherenkov radiation of light has found important applications in experimental physics. But this is a separate large and interesting topic. For us, please, it is important that Cherenkov radiation is a very general phenomenon. Whenever a particle moves at a speed exceeding the speed of waves in a given medium, it radiates.
