Let's calculate how much energy $\Delta E$ the electron loses during one revolution around the nucleus - during one period $T = \frac{2\pi r_B}{v}$:

$$\Delta E = I \frac{2\pi r_B}{v} = \frac{4\pi}{3} \frac{e^5}{r_B^{5/2} m^{3/2} c^3}.$$

Let's transform this expression to the form

$$\Delta E = \frac{8\pi}{3} \left( \frac{1}{r_B} \frac{e^2}{mc^2} \right)^{3/2} \frac{e^2}{2r_B}.$$

(4)

In this formula, the term $\frac{e^2}{2r_B} = E_a$ is the characteristic “atomic” energy of an electron in an atom. Consequently, all steel quantities included in formula (4) must form a dimensionless combination. Indeed, the quantity $e^2/mc^2$ has the dimension of length. It is called the classical electron radius and is denoted $r_e$:

$$r_e = \frac{e^2}{mc^2} \approx 2,5 \cdot 10^{-15} \text{ m}.$$

Thus,

$$\frac{\Delta E}{E_a} \approx 10^{-6}.$$

So, with each revolution, the electron loses part of its energy - one ten-millionth part. Let's look at this value from a different point of view.

Let's try to estimate how long an atom will “live” if the electron emits electromagnetic energy even in such small portions. During one revolution, the energy of the electron will decrease by values ​​of $\Delta E \approx 10^{-6} E_a$, and at the same time the distance from the electron to the nucleus will also decrease. And in approximately $10^6$ revolutions, the electron will completely swap onto the nucleus!

But an electron completes one revolution in

$$\frac{2\pi r_B}{v} = \frac{2\pi r_B}{e} (mr_B)^{1/2} \approx 10^{-16} c.$$

Consequently, if the laws of classical mechanics and classical electrodynamics were valid in the world of atoms and electrons, atoms could exist no more than $10^{-10} c$.

This is perhaps one of the most disastrous discrepancies between classical theory and experiment: “eternal” atoms turned out to be extremely unstable.

There is another contradiction between the planetary model of the atom and experiment. The experiment shows that atoms emit (although for this they must first be excited, that is, transfer them a certain amount of energy) electromagnetic waves of strictly defined frequencies. The set of these frequencies is a kind of calling card of the atom (spectral analysis is based on this).

From the point of view of the planetary model, excited by the fact that its electron has a slightly higher energy and, therefore, a larger orbital radius $r_1 > r_B$. During the process of radiation, the distance from the electron to the nucleus gradually decreases to a value of $r_B$, while the rotation frequency continuously changes (increases). Consequently, the spectrum of emitted frequencies must not be interrupted from the initial frequency

$$\frac{v_1}{2\pi r_1} = \left( \frac{e^2}{mr_1} \right)^{1/2} \frac{1}{2\pi r_1}$$

up to maximum frequency

$$\frac{v}{2\pi r_B} = \left( \frac{e^2}{mr_B} \right)^{1/2} \frac{1}{2\pi r_B}.$$

The classical planetary model cannot explain the true discrete spectrum of radiation from atoms. Mechanics is needed to explain all this.
