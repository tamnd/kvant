As a second example, let’s take A. S. Sorokin’s problem “The Failed Lunch” («Kvant» No. 11, 1972). Let us recall its conditions, somewhat shortening the formulation.

Problem 2

$N$ wanted to invite all of his neighbors to dinner if possible: $A$, $B$, $C$, $D$, $E$, $F$, $G$ and $H^*$). At the same time, he, however, faced the following obstacles:

$A$ will not come if you invite $B$ or $C$, or if $D$ and $E$ are invited at the same time (1);

$D$ will come only if $E$ (2) is also invited;

$E$ will not receive an invitation if $B$ arrives (3);

$F$ charges only when accompanied by $G$ (4);

$H$ will not object to the presence of $F$ only if $A$ is also invited (5);

But if $F$ is not invited, then $H$ will also be against the invitation $E$ (6); for SEL $G$, you need to invite $D$ or $H$ (7);

Finally, $G$ will refuse the invitation if $E$ is invited without $A$, and also if $B$ or $C$ is invited (8).

What is the maximum number of hosts (and what exactly) can $N$ invite?

The basic set of statements of the problem consists of eight statements of the form “$A$ ($B$, $C$, ...) is invited to dinner” (we will denote them respectively by the letters $A$, $B$, $C$, ...).

Let us write conditions (1)–(8) of the problem in logical algebra symbols:

(1) $(B \Rightarrow \bar{A}) \land (C \Rightarrow \bar{A}) \land ((D \land E) \Rightarrow \bar{A})$;

(2) $D \Rightarrow E$;

(3) $B \Rightarrow E$;

(4) $F \Rightarrow G$;

(5) $(F \land \bar{A}) \Rightarrow \bar{H}$;

(6) $(\bar{F} \land E) \Rightarrow \bar{H}$;

(7) $G \Rightarrow (D \lor H)$;

(8) $(B \Rightarrow \bar{G}) \land (C \Rightarrow \bar{G}) \land ((E \land \bar{A}) \Rightarrow \bar{G})$.

The condition will always have the form of a conjunction of 12 implications:

$(B \Rightarrow \bar{A}) \land (C \Rightarrow \bar{A}) \land ((D \land E) \Rightarrow \bar{A}) \land (D \Rightarrow E) \land (B \Rightarrow \bar{E}) \land (F \Rightarrow G) \land ((F \land \bar{A}) \Rightarrow \bar{H}) \land ((\bar{F} \land E) \Rightarrow \bar{H}) \land ((\bar{D} \land \bar{H}) \Rightarrow \bar{G}) \land (B \Rightarrow \bar{G}) \land (C \Rightarrow \bar{G}) \land ((E \land \bar{A}) \Rightarrow \bar{G})$

In the heels of them, the premises are expressed by conjunctions of basic statements. It is impossible to simplify such implications, and their premises, for example with basic statements and their negations, will have to be represented by the vertices of the graph. We follow this as follows.
