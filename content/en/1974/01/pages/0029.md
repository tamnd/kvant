Electrical special processing of metals
I. I. Moroz

In recent years, electrical methods of metal processing have become increasingly used. What these methods are, and how they are used in comparison to other methods of metal processing, will be discussed in this article.

In 1834, Michael Faraday formed the basic laws of electrolysis.

According to Faraday's law, the amount of substances $m$ released or dissolved on the electrodes during time $t$ is directly proportional to the amount of electricity $q$ passing through the electrolyte:

$$m = kq = kIt = \frac{ItA}{Nev},$$

where $I$ is the current strength, $A$ is the atomic mass, $v$ is the valence of the substance, $N$ is Avogadro’s number, $e$ is the charge of the electron.

It is known that $I = jS$ (j is the current density, $S$ is the electrode area).

Just a few years after the discovery of the laws of electrolysis, in 1928, Soviet engineers V.N. Gusev and L.P. Rozhkov proposed using electrolysis for processing metals using common mechanical methods (turning, milling, cutting, grinding).

At first glance, this proposal seems uneducational. Indeed, all human experience would seem to teach that a tool must be a solid material to be processed. Even caveman used stone as a cutting tool. Then iron, steel, and, finally, hard and superhard alloy tools were created. But with the development of technology, especially specialized branches of mechanical engineering (automotive engineering, rocketry and some others), new materials appeared, the processing of which by mechanical methods is fraught with great difficulties, and in some cases, simply impossible. For example, there are hard and brittle alloys that are destroyed during mechanical processing. There was a need to create new methods of metal processing. This is where the proposal of Gusev and Rozhkov came in handy.

During electrolysis, metal electrodes are immersed in an electrolyte solution, an electrical voltage is applied to them, and current flows through the electrolyte solution (Fig. 1). Obviously, hydrogen is released on the metal surface, and then dissolution of the metal occurs, which is often accompanied by the release of oxygen.

In industrial practice, electrochemical etching and electrolytic polishing of metal have long been widely used. The first is for cleaning the metal surface from oxide films, the second is for improving the quality of the surface, giving it a grainy shine. Both of these processes are based on the fact that during electrolysis the metal is dissolved.
