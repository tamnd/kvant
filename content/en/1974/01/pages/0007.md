I have not the slightest doubt that the little that I managed to achieve in mathematics and teaching became possible thanks to constant sports activities. I can’t imagine my life without math or sports.

I picked up a tennis racket for the first time at the age of forty and was so carried away that five years later I added the first category. This passion has captured almost all the children of our houses on V. Olenei and my children as well. Fortunately, the hospitality courts of the Shakhtar sports club were located for use. Oh, if only all sports clubs, stadiums, and sports schools were so accessible to those looking for sports! Then many problems of youth sports would be solved without many years of discussions.

Together with me, professors P. A. Kitkin, V. M. Fokeev, V. A. Lurie, associate professor S. V. Boriskin and others “fell out” into tennis. P. A. Kitkin was the director of one of the institutes in Katseveli (in Crimea, near Siemens). There, together with other enthusiasts, he built a court, surrounded it with a fishing net and skipped training. We were his guests in the summer and slept under the trees near the court.

Years have passed. We all learned to play tennis decently, and that requires a lot of stamina. Olympic beta champion Peter Snegle wrote that, to his surprise, the load on this game turned out to be greater than the load on the beta. What helped us, no longer young people, to cope with these difficulties? Physical fitness and endurance developed in previous years by walking on flats, regular jogging, and, especially, swimming.

For several years now, a swimming section has been operating at the Moscow House of Scientists. Corresponding member of the USSR Academy of Sciences L. A. Lyusternik, academician S. L. Sobolev and others worked in it. Once a week, starting at seven in the morning, we successfully trained for an hour. Renewed and full of strength, they went to work. Further, many years later, S. L. Sobolev said that it was a wonderful time, when wonderful thinking and writing occurred. L.A. Lyusternik was surprised to discover that he had forgotten about the runny nose, but learned about the existence of such a time of day as six o’clock in the morning. P. A. Kitkin and V. M. Fokeev wrote their doctoral dissertations during these years, and A. I. Felzenbaum wrote his candidate’s theses. I myself must admit that for a number of years I was in fits and starts looking for a solution to some problems in general algebra. Hopes gave way to disappointments. When I got down to business in earnest, I created a strict regime: the first half of the day - work, lunch, then a three-hour game of tennis in the summer or lying down and jogging in the winter. And so on every year. In the evening - an absolutely fresh head, a sense of the canvas of life, joyful anticipation of work, assistance in thinking about the paths and details of evidence.
