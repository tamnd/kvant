Liquid helium is often called a "quantum liquid". When you first become acquainted with quantum physics, you may get the impression that its laws are important only for describing the properties of atoms, atomic nuclei, electrons and other microparticles. Liquid helium is one of the most striking examples of the fact that quantum physics can also describe the properties of macroscopic bodies.

In front of you is a liquid that has been poured into a vessel. You can observe its behavior, pour it into another vessel, and so on. And at the same time, the properties of liquid helium are completely unusual; it is radically different from all other liquids. Its behavior can only be explained by the laws of quantum physics.

What are the main features of the behavior of liquid helium? The first of them was discovered by Kamerlingh Onnes during an experiment during which liquid helium was first obtained. Onnes attempted to convert helium into a solid state. He began to reduce the pressure in the vessel containing the boiling liquid. At the same time, the temperature increased (its value became close to 1° K!), but no signs of helium solidification were found.

Kamerlingh Onnes subsequently repeatedly tried to obtain solid helium. The last such attempt was made by him at the end of his life in 1922. Pumping out steam above liquid helium with twelve new pumps of a special design (the pressure dropped to 0.013 mm rm. cm!), he reached a temperature of only 0.83 ° K. The message about this experiment was called: “On the lowest temperature obtained so far.” But even at this record low temperature for that time, helium remained liquid.

G. Kamerlingh Onnes (1853-1926)

It has now been firmly established that helium is the only substance in nature that does not solidify down to absolute zero (it can become solid only with additional strong slope). Solid helium was first obtained in 1926 by H. Keesom, the successor of Kamerlingh Onnes, who after him headed the work at the Leiden Laboratory.

From the point of view of classical physics, the unique behavior of liquid helium is completely incomprehensible. Indeed, as the temperature drops, the thermal vibrations of the lime part become weaker and weaker. The presence of mechanical adhesion forces should ultimately lead to the solidification of the substance.

The behavior of liquid helium has nothing in common with this picture. Helium would remain liquid even at absolute nULE, although there would be no thermal movement at all.
