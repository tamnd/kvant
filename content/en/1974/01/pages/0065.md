Problem 5. In a room with volume $V = 50 \text{ m}^3$ air humidity $f = 60\%$ at temperature $t = 20^\circ\text{C}$ and pressure $p = 10^5 \text{ h/m}^2$. What is the mass of $m$ moist air in the room? How will this mass change if the humidity increases by $\Delta f = 10\%$ with a change in temperature and pressure? Will this change the total number of moist air molecules in the room? Saturated vapor pressure at $t = 20^\circ\text{C}$ $p_H = 2,3 \cdot 10^3 \text{ h/m}^2$.

The mass of moist air $m$ is the sum of the mass of dry air $m_1$ and the mass of water vapor $m_2$:

$$m = m_1 + m_2.$$

From the Mendeleev-Clapeyron equation

$$m_1 = \frac{p_1V\mu_1}{RT} \quad \text{и} \quad m_2 = \frac{p_2V\mu_2}{RT},$$

where $\mu_1 = 29 \text{ ке/кмоль}$ and $\mu_2 = 18 \text{ ке/кмоль}$ are the molecular masses of air and water vapor, $p_1$ and $p_2$ correspond to partial pressures. According to the conditions of the problem $p_2 = \frac{f}{100\%} p_H$, and according to Dalton’s law $p_1 = p - p_2 = p - \frac{f}{100\%} p_H$.

Then we finally get

$$m = m_1 + m_2 = \frac{V}{RT} \left[ p\mu_1 - \right.$$

$$- \frac{f}{100\%} p_H \left( \mu_1 - \mu_2 \right] = \frac{50}{8,3 \cdot 10^3 \cdot 293} \times$$

$$\left[ 10^5 \cdot 29 - \frac{60}{100} \cdot 2,3 \cdot 10^3 \left(29 - 18\right) \right] = 58 \left(\kappa e\right).$$

As can be seen from the last formula, with an increase in humidity $f$ with constant $p$ and $T$, the mass of humid air in the room $m$ will be reduced to $(\mu_1 - \mu_2 > 0)$. The change in mass with an increase in humidity by $\Delta f$ will be equal to

$$\Delta m = \frac{p_HV}{RT} \left( \mu_1 - \mu_2 \right) \frac{\Delta f}{100\%} =$$

$$= \frac{2,3 \cdot 10^3 \cdot 50}{8,3 \cdot 10^3 \cdot 293} \left(29 - 18\right) \frac{10}{100} = 0,05 \left(\kappa e\right).$$

The decrease in the mass of moist air in a room with increasing humidity can be understood using Avogadro’s law: equal volumes at the same pressure and temperature contain the same number of molecules of any gas. As the air humidity in the room increases, it does not change, but only part of the air molecules are replaced by water molecules (see Fig. 3): the number of water vapor molecules increases and the number of dry air molecules decreases. Since $\mu_2 < \mu_1$, the mass of moist air will be mixed.

We can also say that the specific heat of vaporization $L$ is the amount of heat that must be consumed to convert a unit mass of liquid into steam when it boils.
