V. Z. Kresin
Near absolute zero
(historical sketch)

“... If we could place the Earth in some very cold region, then all our rivers and oceans would turn into mountains. The air would cease to be invisible and would turn into liquid. A transformation of this kind would open up the possibility of obtaining new liquids, about which we still have no idea.” These words, uttered in the middle of the 18th century, belong to the great French chemist Lavuzier.

In the 19th century, many gases were burned, but Lavouzier’s dream of obtaining new liquids was finally fulfilled only in 1908, when in the laboratory of the Dutch physicist Kamerlingh Onnes at Leiden University, the last of the gases, helium, was converted into liquid.

A few words about the main achievements on the path to absolute zero.

One of the main methods of burning gases is to burn them. In this case, the molecules come closer together, the role of adhesion forces returns, and it becomes possible to transition into a liquid state. However, if the gas is at a temperature above the critical temperature, tonics cannot be converted into liquid by combustion. In this case, transformational cooling of the gas is necessary.

By the seventies of the 19th century, a huge number of experiments had been carried out on the combustion of gases and the production of many new liquids (special mention should be made of the experiments of Faraday, who, along with skateium, which was previously used for the combustion of gases, was the first to use gas cooling). However, oxygen, nitrogen and hydrogen showed no signs of turning into liquid. Therefore, the belief that these three substances are “permanent gases” began to fade in physics. Only in 1877, the French scientist Calte was able to obtain liquid oxygen at a temperature of $T = 90,2^\circ$ K. Six years later, physicists Wroblewski and Olszewski were the first to see liquid nitrogen. The transition of nitrogen into liquid occurred at $T = 77,4^\circ$ K.

The path to obtaining liquid hydrogen was very difficult. It turns into a liquid at a temperature of only $20,4^\circ$ K. This problem was solved only in 1898 by the English physicist Dewar. Dewar used a vacuum vessel he invented to store liquid hydrogen, which he named and is used today. When Dewar worked on the problem of obtaining liquid hydrogen, he had no doubt that his work was the last successful one on the path to absolute zero. But Dewar was wrong. The boiling point of the liquid gel turned out to be even lower.
