Near absolute zero
(historical sketch)

“... If we could place the Earth in some very cold region, then all our rivers and oceans would turn into mountains. The air would cease to be invisible and would turn into liquid. A transformation of this kind would open up the possibility of obtaining new liquids, about which we still have no idea.” These words, uttered in the middle of the 18th century, belong to the great French chemist Lavuzier.

In the 19th century, many gases were burned, but Lavouzier’s dream of obtaining new liquids was finally fulfilled only in 1908, when in the laboratory of the Dutch physicist Kamerlingh Onnes at Leiden University, the last of the gases, helium, was converted into liquid.

A few words about the main achievements on the path to absolute zero.

One of the main methods of burning gases is to burn them. In this case, the molecules come closer together, the role of adhesion forces returns, and it becomes possible to transition into a liquid state. However, if the gas is at a temperature above the critical temperature, tonics cannot be converted into liquid by combustion. In this case, transformational cooling of the gas is necessary.

By the seventies of the 19th century, a huge number of experiments had been carried out on the combustion of gases and the production of many new liquids (special mention should be made of the experiments of Faraday, who, along with skateium, which was previously used for the combustion of gases, was the first to use gas cooling). However, oxygen, nitrogen and hydrogen showed no signs of turning into liquid. Therefore, the belief that these three substances are “permanent gases” began to fade in physics. Only in 1877, the French scientist Calte was able to obtain liquid oxygen at a temperature of $T = 90,2^\circ$ K. Six years later, physicists Wroblewski and Olszewski were the first to see liquid nitrogen. The transition of nitrogen into liquid occurred at $T = 77,4^\circ$ K.

The path to obtaining liquid hydrogen was very difficult. It turns into a liquid at a temperature of only $20,4^\circ$ K. This problem was solved only in 1898 by the English physicist Dewar. Dewar used a vacuum vessel he invented to store liquid hydrogen, which he named and is used today. When Dewar worked on the problem of obtaining liquid hydrogen, he had no doubt that his work was the last successful one on the path to absolute zero. But Dewar was wrong. Liquid helium is often called a "quantum liquid". When you first become acquainted with quantum physics, you may get the impression that its laws are important only for describing the properties of atoms, atomic nuclei, electrons and other microparticles. Liquid helium is one of the most striking examples of the fact that quantum physics can also describe the properties of macroscopic bodies.

In front of you is a liquid that has been poured into a vessel. You can observe its behavior, pour it into another vessel, and so on. And at the same time, the properties of liquid helium are completely unusual; it is radically different from all other liquids. Its behavior can only be explained by the laws of quantum physics.

What are the main features of the behavior of liquid helium? The first of them was discovered by Kamerlingh Onnes during an experiment during which liquid helium was first obtained. Onnes attempted to convert helium into a solid state. He began to reduce the pressure in the vessel containing the boiling liquid. At the same time, the temperature increased (its value became close to 1° K!), but no signs of helium solidification were found.

Kamerlingh Onnes subsequently repeatedly tried to obtain solid helium. The last such attempt was made by him at the end of his life in 1922. Pumping out steam above liquid helium with twelve new pumps of a special design (the pressure dropped to 0.013 mm rm. cm!), he reached a temperature of only 0.83 ° K. The message about this experiment was called: “On the lowest temperature obtained so far.” But even at this record low temperature for that time, helium remained liquid.

It has now been firmly established that helium is the only substance in nature that does not solidify down to absolute zero (it can become solid only with additional strong slope). Solid helium was first obtained in 1926 by H. Keesom, the successor of Kamerlingh Onnes, who after him headed the work at the Leiden Laboratory.

From the point of view of classical physics, the unique behavior of liquid helium is completely incomprehensible. Indeed, as the temperature drops, the thermal vibrations of the lime part become weaker and weaker. The presence of mechanical adhesion forces should ultimately lead to the solidification of the substance.

The behavior of liquid helium has nothing in common with this picture. Helium would remain liquid even at absolute nULE, although there would be no thermal movement at all.
