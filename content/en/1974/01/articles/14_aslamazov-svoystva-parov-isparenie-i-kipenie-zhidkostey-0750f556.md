Properties of vapors, evaporation and boiling of liquids

Steam equation of state

Under normal conditions, the vapor density is low, and the interaction between vapor molecules can be neglected. Such pairs, just like an ideal gas, describe the Mendeleev-Clapeyron equation:

$$pV = \frac{m}{\mu}RT,$$

where $p$, $V$ and $T$ are the pressure, volume and temperature of the vapor, respectively, $m$ is its mass, $\mu$ is the molecular mass, $R$ is the universal gas constant $^*$).

Task 1. Name the density of saturated water vapor and water at $t = 100^\circ$ C. Saturated vapor pressure of water at emulsification $p_H = 10^5 \text{ h/m}^2$.

Vapor density $\rho = m/V$, where $m$ is mass, $V$ is vapor volume. From the Mendeleev–Clapeyron equation we have

$$\rho = \frac{p_H \mu}{RT} = \frac{10^5 \cdot 18}{8,3 \cdot 10^3 \cdot 373} = 0,57(\text{кг/м}^3)$$

The ratio of this density to the density of water $p_B = 10^3 \text{ кг/м}^3$ is very small:

$$\frac{\rho}{\rho_B} = 5,7 \cdot 10^{-4}.$$

When using the Mendeleev-Clapeyron equation when solving problems on the properties of vapors, it should be remembered that the mass of vapor $m$ located in a closed vessel can change when parameters $p$, $V$ and $T$ change. The mass of steam decreases as a result of its condensation and, conversely, increases with the evaporation of the liquid in the vessel. For example, when making skatin, steam at a certain volume $V_0$ becomes saturated; with a further decrease in volume $V$, the mass of steam $m$ also decreases in proportion to the volume, so that the pressure of steam $p_0 = \frac{RT}{\mu} \frac{m}{V}$ remains constant (see Fig. 1, a and b).

Humidity
The presence of water vapor in the air can be described quantitatively using two characteristics - absolute and relative air humidity.
Absolute air humidity, as is known, is called the density of water vapor $\rho = m/V$, and relative humidity is the percentage ratio of the density of water vapor to the density of saturated vapor at the same temperature, that is, $f = \frac{\rho}{\rho_H} \cdot 100\%$. Relative air humidity can also be defined as the ratio of water vapor pressure to saturated vapor pressure at the same temperature. Indeed, at constant temperature the vapor density is proportional to

$$f_2 = \frac{\rho_2}{\rho_1} \cdot 100\% = \frac{3,4 \cdot 10^4}{10^5} \cdot 100\% = 34\%.$$

As the temperature changes, even with constant absolute humidity, the relative humidity changes. Thus, as moist air cools at a certain temperature $T_p$, its relative humidity reaches 100%, and the vapor contained in the air becomes saturated. With further cooling, the steam will condense. Temperature $T_p$ is called the dew point (at this temperature dew falls).

Problem 3. At what maximum relative air humidity $f_0$ in the room will we not fill a piece of milk taken out of the refrigerator? The temperature in the refrigerator is $t_1 = 5^\circ\text{C}$, and in the room $t_2 = 25^\circ\text{C}$. The pressure of saturated water at $5^\circ\text{C}$ $p_1 = 866\text{ h/m}^2$, and at $25^\circ\text{C}$ $p_2 = 3192\text{ h/m}^2$.

Near the bottle, the temperature of the humid air becomes $t_1 = 5^\circ\text{C}$. The bottle is not filled if the relative humidity at this temperature does not exceed 100%:

$$f = \frac{\rho}{\rho_1} \cdot 100\% \leq 100\%$$

where $\rho_1 = \frac{\rho_1\mu}{RT_1}$ is the density of saturated water vapor at $T_1 = 278^\circ\text{K}$, $\rho$ is the absolute humidity of the air in the room, independent of temperature. Therefore, the maximum absolute air humidity at which the bottle is not yet filled is $\rho_0 = \rho_1 = \frac{\rho_1\mu}{RT_1}$. The corresponding relative humidity at temperature $T_2 = 298^\circ\text{K}$ will be:

$$f_0 = \frac{\rho_0}{\rho_2} \cdot 100\% = \frac{\rho_1 T_2}{\rho_2 T_1} \cdot 100\% = \frac{866 \cdot 298}{3192 \cdot 278} \cdot 100\% \approx 30\%$$

Heat of vaporization. Boiling

The transformation of liquid into vapor, as is known, requires the supply of energy. The amount of heat that must be supplied in order to evaporate a unit mass of liquid at a changed temperature (isothermal) and external pressure equal to the pressure of its saturated vapor is called the specific heat of evaporation. We can also say that the specific heat of vaporization $L$ is the amount of heat that must be consumed to convert a unit mass of liquid into steam when it boils. Frequent energy goes to increase the potential energy of molecules (to work

against the forces of molecular attraction), and part - on the work of distribution against the forces of external pressure (since the specific volume *) of the vapor is greater than the specific volume of the liquid).

Task 6. Determine what part of the specific temperature of water vaporization goes to increase the internal energy of the system at $T = 373^\circ\text{K}$. $L = 2,3 \cdot 10^6$ j/kg.

From the law of conservation of energy we have

$$L = \Delta w + A,$$

where $\Delta w$ is the change in internal energy during the evaporation of 1 kcal, $A$ is the work of distribution. Since the distribution is isobaric, then

$$A = p_H (v_H - v_0),$$

where $v_H = \frac{RT}{p_H \mu}$ is the specific volume of saturated steam, and $v_0$ is the specific volume of water, which can be neglected in comparison with $v_H$ (see problem 1). Thus

$$\frac{\Delta w}{L} = \frac{L - A}{L} = 1 - \frac{RT}{\mu L} = 1 - \frac{8,3 \cdot 10^3 \cdot 373}{18 \cdot 2 \cdot 10^6} \approx 0,9.$$

That is, 90% of the supplied heat is spent on overcoming the forces of interaction between molecules during the evaporation of water at $373^\circ\text{K}$.

*) Let us recall that specific volume is the reciprocal of density, that is, the volume of a unit mass of a substance.

When solving the last problem, the question arises: will water boil in a vessel at $T_1 = 373^\circ$ K? As is well known, for boiling it is necessary that gas bubbles at the bottom of the vessel begin to grow due to the evaporation of the liquid inside them. Strictly speaking, this occurs when the saturated vapor pressure slightly exceeds external atmospheric pressure, namely, by the sum of the excess pressure values

$$p_1 = \frac{2 \sigma}{r},$$ where $\sigma$ is the coefficient of surface tension of the liquid, and $r$ is the radius of the bubble, and hydrostatic pressure $p_2 = \rho g h$, where $\rho$ is the density, and $h$ is the height of the liquid in the vessel. Therefore, boiling of a liquid at normal external pressure begins at a slightly higher temperature than $373^\circ$ K$^*$), and in the problem under consideration there will be no liquid. Obviously, the rule is that replacement overheating of the liquid does not occur, since the additional pressure is small compared to the external pressure. In order to overheat a liquid, it and the walls of the vessel must be thoroughly cleared of gases.
