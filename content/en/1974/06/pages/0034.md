If you somehow remove the matnik from its state of equilibrium by imparting some initial energy to it, it will perform periodic, repeating movements. A distinctive feature of oscillations in comparison with other types of periodic motion is that there is a special position - the equilibrium position, to which the periodic oscillations return, but do not remain in this position, but continue to move further by inertia.

Consequently, in a system that has inertia, there must be a so-called restoring force. In an external matnik this is the force of elasticity, in a mathematical one it is a component of the force of gravity. The restoring force is associated with the displacement of the matnik from the equilibrium position:

$$F_B = -kx.$$

This equality in form fills Hooke’s law for elastic deformations:

$$F_{ynp} = -k_{ynp}x,$$

where $x$ are the deformation values, $k_{ynp}$ is the elasticity coefficient. Therefore, the restoring force is often called the quasi-elastic force (the prefix “quasin” means “as if”), and $k$ is the quasi-elasticity coefficient. Coefficient $k$ depends on the nature of the restoring force.

Thus, the basic law of diamkick - Newton’s second law for harmonic vibrations is written as follows:

$$a = \frac{F}{m} = -\frac{k}{m}x. \quad (2)$$

The solution to this equation is expression (1).

The initial phase $\varphi_0$ depends, of course, only on the initial conditions. The cyclic frequency $\omega$ depends on $m$ and $k$, characterizing the inert and quaselastic properties of the system, as follows:

$$\omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}.$$

But if you have forgotten this formula, then simple physical messages and the method of dimensions will help you remember it.

Indeed, the greater the quasi-elasticity coefficient $k$, the greater the acceleration of the matnik at any time, the faster the matnik oscillates. The greater the mass of the mat, the less its acceleration, the slower the vibrations will occur.

Let us write the dependence of $\omega$ on $m$ and $k$ in the following form:

$$\omega = Ak^\alpha m^\beta,$$

where $A$ is the dimensionless proportionality coefficient, $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are dimensionless exponents. Telery complements the rules of dimensions, according to which the dimensions of the left and right sides of any equality must be the same, that is

$$[\omega] = [k^\alpha] [m^\beta].$$

In SI $$[\omega] = c^{-1}, [k] = \kappa \cdot c^{-2}, [m] = \kappa \cdot c^{-2\alpha} \kappa e^\beta.$$

Let us find for which $\alpha$ and $\beta$ this equality turns into an identity. To do this, we equate the exponents of identical bases on the left and right and get the system

$$\begin{cases} -1 = -2\alpha, \\ \alpha + \beta = 0, \end{cases}$$

where

$$\alpha = \frac{1}{2} \text{ и } \beta = -\frac{1}{2}.$$

Thus,

$$\omega = A \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}.$$

The coefficient $A$, which is actually equal to 1, cannot, of course, be found using the dimensional method.
