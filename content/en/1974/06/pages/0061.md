Now try to imagine the size of each facet of the eye. So - the molecule is still about 100,000 times smaller than such a facet!

Of course, such a particle cannot be seen with the naked eye. But maybe it can be seen in a microscope? What should the microscope magnification be for this?

A drop of water with a diameter of 0.5 cm, when magnified 2000 times, will be the size of a classroom, but even then it will not be possible to distinguish individual molecules in it. At another 2000x magnification, the drop will have a cross section of 20 km, and its “grainy” structure begins to emerge before our eyes. But you need to increase it another 250 times to finally see the structure of the water molecule, but... such an increase (a total of 10⁹ times) cannot be obtained.

Currently, with the help of special microscopes (electron microscopes), photographs are taken in which individual molecules can be distinguished, but only those that are large in size compared to water molecules.

In the photo (Fig. 2) you see protein molecules whose diameter is about 100 times larger than that of water. Knowing that the photograph was taken at 73,000x magnification, try to estimate the approximate diameter of the protein molecule.

And finally, we advise you to continue the experiment to determine the size of oil molecules yourself. Of course, it is difficult to repeat the Rayleigh experiment, since for this you need to have a special type of oil and make precise measurements. But try it anyway! Your reward will be that you have determined a value that is comparable to the size of molecules.
