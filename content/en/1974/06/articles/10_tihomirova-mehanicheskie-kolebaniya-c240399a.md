Mechanical vibrations
V. A. Tikhomirova

Harmonic oscillatory motion is a movement that occurs according to the law
$$x = x_0 \cos(\omega t + \varphi_0).$$
(1)

Here $x$ is the displacement of the body from the equilibrium position, $x_0$ is the amplitude of oscillations, $\omega t + \varphi_0 = \varphi$ is the phase of oscillations, $\omega$ is the cyclic frequency, which can be associated with the frequency of oscillations $v$ or the period of oscillations $T$ by correspondences $\omega = 2\pi v = \frac{2\pi}{T}$, and $\varphi_0$ is the initial phase. The greatest difficulty in studying these quantities is caused by the concept of oscillation phase. From equation (1) it is clear that, knowing the oscillation phase and amplitude, it is possible to determine not only the position of the body at a given moment in time, but also the direction of movement of the body. Therefore, when we say that the phase of oscillation characterizes the state of oscillatory motion at the moment being shaken. The initial phase shows at what stage the oscillatory process began. Let, for example, the mathematical matrix (Fig. 1, a) be connected with the initial phase $\varphi_0 = \frac{\pi}{2}$.

This means that it begins to move from its equilibrium position to the left. After a quarter of the period, when the oscillation phase is $\varphi = \omega t + \varphi_0 = \frac{2\pi}{T} \cdot \frac{T}{4} + \frac{\pi}{2} = \pi$, the matrix should reach the extreme goal position (Fig. 1, b), etc. The concepts of phase and initial phase become especially important when comparing or adding several oscillations.

Equation (1) is a kinematic law that describes a harmonic oscillation and relates the basic kinematic quantities that characterize this movement. Let us remember when the amplitude, frequency and initial phase of oscillations arise. The simplest and most obvious examples of oscillatory systems are the spring matrix (Fig. 2) and the mathematical matrix. We will talk only about the free (or natural) vibrations of these matrices *).

If you somehow remove the matnik from its state of equilibrium by imparting some initial energy to it, it will perform periodic, repeating movements. A distinctive feature of oscillations in comparison with other types of periodic motion is that there is a special position - the equilibrium position, to which the periodic oscillations return, but do not remain in this position, but continue to move further by inertia.

Consequently, in a system that has inertia, there must be a so-called restoring force. In an external matnik this is the force of elasticity, in a mathematical one it is a component of the force of gravity. The restoring force is associated with the displacement of the matnik from the equilibrium position:

$$F_B = -kx.$$

This equality in form fills Hooke’s law for elastic deformations:

$$F_{ynp} = -k_{ynp}x,$$

where $x$ are the deformation values, $k_{ynp}$ is the elasticity coefficient. Therefore, the restoring force is often called the quasi-elastic force (the prefix “quasin” means “as if”), and $k$ is the quasi-elasticity coefficient. Coefficient $k$ depends on the nature of the restoring force.

Thus, the basic law of diamkick - Newton’s second law for harmonic vibrations is written as follows:

$$a = \frac{F}{m} = -\frac{k}{m}x. \quad (2)$$

The solution to this equation is expression (1).

The initial phase $\varphi_0$ depends, of course, only on the initial conditions. The cyclic frequency $\omega$ depends on $m$ and $k$, characterizing the inert and quaselastic properties of the system, as follows:

$$\omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}.$$

But if you have forgotten this formula, then simple physical messages and the method of dimensions will help you remember it.

Indeed, the greater the quasi-elasticity coefficient $k$, the greater the acceleration of the matnik at any time, the faster the matnik oscillates. The greater the mass of the mat, the less its acceleration, the slower the vibrations will occur.

Let us write the dependence of $\omega$ on $m$ and $k$ in the following form:

$$\omega = Ak^\alpha m^\beta,$$

where $A$ is the dimensionless proportionality coefficient, $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are dimensionless exponents. Telery complements the rules of dimensions, according to which the dimensions of the left and right sides of any equality must be the same, that is

$$[\omega] = [k^\alpha] [m^\beta].$$

In SI $$[\omega] = c^{-1}, [k] = \kappa \cdot c^{-2}, [m] = \kappa \cdot c^{-2\alpha} \kappa e^\beta.$$

Let us find for which $\alpha$ and $\beta$ this equality turns into an identity. To do this, we equate the exponents of identical bases on the left and right and get the system

$$\begin{cases} -1 = -2\alpha, \\ \alpha + \beta = 0, \end{cases}$$

where

$$\alpha = \frac{1}{2} \text{ и } \beta = -\frac{1}{2}.$$

Thus,

$$\omega = A \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}.$$

The coefficient $A$, which is actually equal to 1, cannot, of course, be found using the dimensional method.

Telery about the amplitude of oscillations. Of course, it depends on the energy that was given to the matnik at the initial moment. Let's talk about this more specifically when solving the next problem.

Task 1. We perform a harmonic oscillation of a spring magnet with a cyclic frequency $\omega$ and amplitude $x_0$. Find the maximum values ​​of acceleration and speed of the magnet, as well as the magnitude of its mechanical energy.

According to Newton's second law for harmonic oscillations

$$a = -\omega^2 x.$$

Since the displacement $x$ changes according to the harmonic law, and $\omega^2$ is a constant value, then the acceleration of the magnet $a$ also changes according to the harmonic law, and its amplitude

$$a_0 = \omega^2 x_0.$$

The minus sign in the equation of motion indicates that the acceleration oscillations are opposite in phase to the mixing oscillations.

Since the displacement of the magnet and its acceleration change with time according to the harmonic law, it is natural to assume that the speed of the magnet changes according to the same law. The structure of the calculation actually confirms these assumptions. The amplitude of velocity fluctuations can be found using the law of conservation of energy.

The mechanical energy of the magnet $E$ consists of the kinetic energy of the ball $E_K = mv^2/2$ and the potential energy of elastic deformation of the arm $E_\pi = kx^2/2$.

$$E = E_K \max = \frac{mv^2}{2}.$$

$$E = E_\pi \max = \frac{kx^2}{2}.$$

Task 2. We place a weight of mass $m$ on the scales hanging on a spring from a height $h$ and remain on the scale (Fig. 5, a). The coefficient of simplifying the spring is $k$. The masses of the spring and cup are small compared to the mass of the load. Determine the amplitude and period of free oscillations of the cup with the load.

The system will indeed oscillate, since it is a spring pendulum located vertically. But, in addition to the elastic forces of the spring, the force of gravity also acts on the cup with the load. What is her role? This force turns out to change the equilibrium position of the system, shifting it down by values ​​$\Delta x$ (Fig. 5, b).

Let's consider two coordinate systems: $OX$ and $O'X'$. Let us write down the law of pendulum motion in the first system:

$$ma = -kx + mg.$$
But
$$x = x' + \Delta x.$$

The value $\Delta x$ can be found from the condition of equality of the forces of gravity and elasticity in the equilibrium position: $mg = k\Delta x$. That's why

$$ma = -kx' - k \frac{mg}{k} + mg,$$

that is

$$ma = -kx'.$$

Consequently, the acceleration of the system is reported only by elastic forces.

The oscillation period is

$$T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{m}{k}}.$$

Problem 4. A hydrometer of mass m is a ball filled with two and a cylindrical tube with a cross section S. It is placed in zinc with density $\rho$ (Fig. 7). The hydrometer adjusts the temperature slightly deeper than is necessary for equilibrium, and then releases it. The least free oscillations of a hydrometer.

In the equilibrium position, the gravity layer is balanced by the buoyant force. If the hydrometer is immersed deeper into the liquid, the buoyant layer becomes stronger than the forces of gravity, and an equilibrium force appears, directed upward. Having passed the equilibrium position by inertia, the hydrometer turns out to be immersed in the liquid less than is necessary for equilibrium, and an equilibrium force appears, directed downward. Thus, the change in the buoyant force acts as a restoring force:

$$\Delta F_{\text{выт}} = -\rho g \Delta V = -\rho g Sx$$

(the minus sign indicates that the change in buoyancy force is opposite to the change in the volume of the immersed part of the hydrometer). Therefore, in this case $k = \rho g S$, so

$$T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{m}{k}} = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{m}{\rho g S}}.$$

Problem 5. A mathematical magnet of length l is mounted on a cart that rolls without friction from an inclined plane with an angle of inclination $\alpha$. The most important equilibrium of a magnet and the period of its oscillations.

Since the magnet is on a cart rolling down an inclined plane with acceleration $a = g \sin \alpha$, its equilibrium position will be the position in which the magnet moves relative to the plane with the same acceleration $a$ as the cart.

Two forces act on the ball: the force of gravity $mg$ and the tension force of the thread $F_H$. The balance of these forces in the equilibrium position should impart acceleration $a$ to the ball:

$$F_p = ma = mg \sin \alpha.$$

From figure 8 it is clear that this is only possible if the magnet thread is perpendicular to the inclined plane.

So, the component of gravity along the plane, equal to $mg \sin \alpha$, should provide the magnet with accelerated movement along the inclined plane. The tension force, always perpendicular to the trajectory of the ball relative to the cart, also cannot act as a restoring force. Therefore, one layer remains - the gravity component, perpendicular to the plane and equal to $mg \cos \alpha$. We can say that the magnet oscillates as if in a different gravitational field with the acceleration of free fall $g' = g \cos \alpha$.

Task 6. The softener consists of a metal ball suspended on a loop thread. How will the period of its oscillations change if the ball is given a positive charge, and another negatively charged ball is placed below, on the same vertical with the magnet (Fig. 9, a)?

In addition to the force of gravity and the tension force of the thread, the charged magnetic ball is also subject to an electronic force of attraction to the oppositely charged ball located below. This force, like the force of gravity, will give a component to the direction of movement (Fig. 9, b), which will increase the returning force:

$$F_B = F'_B + F''_B.$$

In this case, the average value of the magnetic acceleration will increase, and the corresponding period will decrease. The equilibrium position of the magnet remains vertical.
