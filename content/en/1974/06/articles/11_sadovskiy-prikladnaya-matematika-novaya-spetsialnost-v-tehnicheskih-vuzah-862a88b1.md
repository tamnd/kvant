“Applied mathematics” is a new specialty in technical universities

Engineers of any specialty need to know fundamental disciplines that have developed during centuries of research into the laws of the world. The most important of these disciplines are mathematics and physics. Developing in the process of human activity, they themselves turn out to be an amazing force that contributes to the progress of knowledge and technology.

The basic facts obtained by exact science (theorems, physical laws, etc.) constitute scientific capital, which, unlike existing technology and technological production, almost does not age with time. A striking illustration of the stability and continuity of knowledge in the field of fundamental disciplines can be a comparison of parts of modern mathematical education. Here you can find “school” geometry created more than 2000 years ago and the even more ancient foundations of algebra. They are combined with differential and integral calculus, as well as analytical geometry and linear algebra. These sections of the standard higher mathematics course at universities have an approximate two-slot age. Finally, modern methods for solving optimization problems (to determine the largest and highest values ​​of functions) - including linear, nonlinear and range programming, maximum principles, etc. - are a little older than twenty years. At the same time, some of them are a successful synthesis of known methods of searching through various options and new messages that ensure the determination of the purposefulness (and, therefore, efficiency) of such searching. Optimization methods are included in the curricula of specialties with advanced mathematical training.

Older branches of science coexist with new ones and they are not canceled. Only the relative weight of individual parts, teaching methods, and assessment of the significance of certain sections change. So, for example, according to the new systematic secondary school program, the mathematics course is introduced a year earlier (in fourth grade). It introduces early set-theoretic concepts, algebraic symbols, modern terminology, the beginnings of geometry, and in high school - the beginnings of mathematical analysis and the study of elements of functions.

A similar process of restructuring the program of mathematical courses, shifting emphasis to new sections, and some reduction of the “traditional” sections that have lost their former meaning is taking place in higher education. In many ways, this perestroika turns out to coincide with the scientific and technological revolution, about which columnists write and write. There is no doubt that one of the most important conditions for this revolution is progress in the field of mathematical knowledge and computer technology. High-speed electronic digital computers (computers), created by mathematicians in collaboration with engineers, over the last thirty years have significantly expanded the possibilities of research (not only mathematical ones!), caused a revaluation of values, introduced fundamental new things into the sphere of economic management, into the management of production processes, into its automation, etc.

Computers present us with another example of the continuity and stability of mathematical ideas: we can trace two directions, the merger of which is distinguished by the paternal creation of computers. However, one of them comes from Blaise Pasque (1623-1662) and his calculating machine (see Quantum, 1973, no. 8, p. 10). The second is from Gottfried Leibniz (1646-1716); he wanted to develop a mathematical apparatus ready to carry out extensions and proofs of the theory, and to build on the basis of this apparatus a machine capable of carrying out proofs automatically.

The first line continued in the work on creating more and more advanced calculating machines: adding meters, automatic adding machines, etc. The second was supported by the work of George Boole (1815-1864), who laid the foundations of the algebra of logic, then by Frege and Russell, who created the actual theory of calculus.

Apparently, for the first time, both of these directions came together in the ideas of Charles Babage (1792-1871); the design of his analytical machine included the most important parts of a computer: memory cells for storing data, arithmetic devices, a device that controls the operation of the machine (program).

Modern computers are a synthesis of ideas dating back to Pascal and Leibniz. These ideas, together with the capabilities of modern social algebra, led to the creation of the theory of algebraic models of computers and theoretical programming.

Computers require proper mathematical support - and it will be created by people. According to one of the world's largest companies producing computer equipment, modern computers are only 40% hardware, while the remaining 60% is software (that is, programs for solving various problems, translators, time management for individual tasks, etc.). The company and its films employ 100,000 mathematicians. According to some estimates, the cost of creating a software program reaches 80% of the cost of a ten-year operation of a computer. Computers, originally intended for solving scientific (computational) problems, turned out to be irreplaceable in the field of management and information processing*).

It is curious that the so-called calculating machines**), designed for processing information (in particular, for accounting calculations), were subsequently (before the advent of computers) used to solve problems with data printed on special cards (punched cards) in the form of plugs (perforations). The set of machines includes: *mabulator* (summing machine), *mulpiller* (multiplying machine), *sorted automaton* (carries out the simplest logical operations - selecting card arrays based on certain criteria), *puncher* (applies data to cards) and other devices.

*) The concept of information is one of the initial ones in mathematics; it cannot be defined (like the concept of a set) with the help of other more elementary concepts. Here, the term “information” can be understood as a totality of facts or data that carry meaningful information about something. The concept of content is relative. So, for a person who has graduated from the secondary level, the message that the Mediterranean and Black Sea contain uses does not contain new information, while for a person who has not studied geography, this message contains something new.

To become an applied mathematician, it is not at all necessary to strive for universities or capital cities. This opportunity is open to lovers of mathematics, physics and their applications in many educational institutions of the country. All students specializing in applied mathematics receive excellent mathematical training and master the disciplines of social science, engineering, and computer science. In the final year, they become familiar with the latest computers, their mathematical software, and information processing processes. And the most important thing is that they master the methods of applied mathematics and master the worldview of an applied mathematician. This means that the development of mathematical thinking and its culture comes to the fore, and not the condition of a certain list of sieves, algorithms, programs; it is still impossible to stock up on all occasions: each new task is associated, as a rule, with a special situation in which old solutions, algorithms, methods no longer work. New ones are required - you need to be able to create them.

The acquired knowledge and ability to solve specific problems open up broad and attractive prospects for the graduate. Scientific and design institutes, design bureaus and laboratories, computer centers and automated control systems are rich in a wide range of interesting and complex problems that are waiting to be solved by mathematical engineers.
