HOW TO MEASURE A MOLECULE?
N. A. RODINA

Our basic knowledge of molecules can be expressed in very simple words:
1. All bodies are made up of tiny particles - atoms and molecules.
2. Atoms and molecules are in a state of continuous movement. This movement is eternal, never stopping under any circumstances.
3. Molecules of all bodies interact with each other. In different substances they interact differently, depending on the type of molecules and the distances between them.
These three provisions are called the basic provisions of the molecular-kinetic theory.
In science, perhaps, there are few areas of knowledge that have such a rapid and fruitful history of development as knowledge about molecules. Mention of the smallest particles of matter is found in manuscripts dating back to the 12th century BC. This means that this knowledge has been created over the course of 32 centuries, that is, 3200 years! Many great scientists contributed their work to the creation of molecular kinetic theory. In science, the structure of molecules and their behavior have been studied sufficiently fully to even rearrange molecules and create substances with a predetermined property. Artificial rubies, rubber, plastics, medicines and vitamins have been created. You've probably read about the difficulty with which these substances were added in their natural form in the past: rubies were added from underground, rubber from the sap of tropical trees, plastics were not known at all.

So, the molecules have been well studied, and they continue to be studied in our time. Moreover, the main directions of development of modern physics are associated with the application of knowledge about the structure of matter. This should be kept in mind by anyone who plans to study physics in the future.

Our story about molecules will be devoted to determining the sizes of molecules.

Can you imagine how big we are? Is it possible, for example, to show with your fingers at least the distance between air molecules, which is approximately 10 times greater than the diameter of the molecule itself?

The sizes of molecules have been determined in many experiments. Let's describe one of them; This experiment was tested about 60 years ago by the English scientist Robert Rayleigh.

A drop of olive oil was placed in a cleanly washed large vessel containing water and placed on its surface. The drop spread over the surface of the water and formed a round film. Gradually the area of ​​the film increased, but then the spreading stopped and the area stopped changing. Rayleigh proposed that the molecules were arranged in one row, that is, only the film became equal to the size of one molecule,

and decided to determine this thickness.
In this case, of course, it is necessary to take into account that the volume of the film is equal to the volume of the drop.

Using the data obtained in the Rayleigh experiment, we calculate the thickness of the film and find out what the linear size of the oil molecule is. The drop had a volume of 0.0009 $cm^3$, and the area of ​​the film formed from it was equal to 5500 $cm^2$. Hence the film thickness
$$d = \frac{V}{S} = \frac{0,0009}{5500} cm = 0,00000016 cm.$$

In Rayleigh's experiment, the volume of a drop was determined by its mass and the density of the oil. The mass of the drop was 0.0008 $e$, the oil density was 0.9 $e/cm^3$. Using these data, calculate the volume of the drop.

Numerous experiments have shown that the molecules of different substances differ in size. But when they want to estimate the diameter of molecules (if

understand that they have the shape of balls), takes values ​​0.0000001 $cm^*$).

Is it possible to imagine a particle that also has dimensions?

Look at the photograph of the head of a common housefly taken at high magnification (Fig. 1). On the sides of the head you can see the eyes of the fly; they consist of individual elements, the so-called facets. In the photograph, each facet has a diameter of approximately 1 mm. Now try to imagine the size of each facet of the eye. So - the molecule is still about 100,000 times smaller than such a facet!

Of course, such a particle cannot be seen with the naked eye. But maybe it can be seen in a microscope? What should the microscope magnification be for this?

A drop of water with a diameter of 0.5 cm, when magnified 2000 times, will be the size of a classroom, but even then it will not be possible to distinguish individual molecules in it. At another 2000x magnification, the drop will have a cross section of 20 km, and its “grainy” structure begins to emerge before our eyes. But you need to increase it another 250 times to finally see the structure of the water molecule, but... such an increase (a total of 10⁹ times) cannot be obtained.

Currently, with the help of special microscopes (electron microscopes), photographs are taken in which individual molecules can be distinguished, but only those that are large in size compared to water molecules.

In the photo (Fig. 2) you see protein molecules whose diameter is about 100 times larger than that of water. Knowing that the photograph was taken at 73,000x magnification, try to estimate the approximate diameter of the protein molecule.

And finally, we advise you to continue the experiment to determine the size of oil molecules yourself. Of course, it is difficult to repeat the Rayleigh experiment, since for this you need to have a special type of oil and make precise measurements. But try it anyway! Your reward will be that you have determined a value that is comparable to the size of molecules.
