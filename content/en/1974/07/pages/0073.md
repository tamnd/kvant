Consequently, we can rightfully compare the distribution of resin droplets in an emulsion with the distribution of molecules in the atmosphere and thus determine the masses of the molecules of gases contained in the air.

Let's use the results of one of Perrenn's experiments. The mass of one drop of resin turned out to be equal to $7,8 \cdot 10^{-15} \text{e}$. The height $h$, at which the number of such droplets was 2 times less than at the bottom, was equal to $3 \cdot 10^{-3} \text{cm}$.

Now let’s remember the data about our atmosphere: if it consisted only of oxygen, then at an altitude $H = 5 \text{km}$ the oxygen density would be 2 times less than at the Earth’s surface.

How to calculate the mass of oxygen molecules from these data? Let us denote the mass of molecules by $m$, and the mass of the drop by $M$ and write this ratio

$$\frac{m}{M} = \frac{h}{H},$$

where

$$m = \frac{Mh}{H} = \frac{7,8 \cdot 10^{-15} \text{e} \cdot 3 \cdot 10^{-3} \text{cm}}{5 \cdot 10^5 \text{cm}} = 47 \cdot 10^{-24} \text{e}.$$

Do a similar calculation for hydrogen yourself.

Currently, the masses of molecules are determined in many different ways, and the mass values ​​​​are refined. For example, the mass of oxygen molecules is now set to $54 \cdot 10^{-24} \text{e}$, and for hydrogen - $3,34 \cdot 10^{-24} \text{e}$.

How can we determine the masses of molecules of gases that are not part of the atmosphere? Here one law of nature comes to the rescue. It was discovered (this was done in 1811 by the Italian scientist A. Avogadro) that equal volumes of all gases under the same conditions contain the same number of molecules. This means that $1 \text{m}^3$ oxygen, nitrogen, neon, carbon dioxide, etc. at $0^\circ \text{C}$ and normal atmospheric pressure contain the same number of molecules as $1 \text{m}^3$ hydrogen. What then is the reason for the difference in the densities of these gases? Why, for example, is the density of neon, that is, its mass $1 \text{m}^3$, equal to $0,9 \text{кг/м}^3$, and the density of hydrogen is 10 times less - $0,09 \text{кг/м}^3$? Answer this question and determine the mass of neon molecules. You can also calculate the mass of molecules of other gases (the densities of gases are indicated in tables, for example, in the textbook “Physics 6”).

Having become acquainted with one of the methods for determining the mass of molecules, you could be convinced that these masses are very small. Is it possible to cock several molecules together? Of course not, although in modern science and technology a very sensual color is used. If you balance two pieces of paper on such a scale, and then mark a point on one of them with a pencil, then everyone will discover its mass. It would seem that it is not worth taking into account the mass of molecules. But all bodies consist of molecules, which means that the masses of bodies are made up of the masses of molecules. How many molecules are there in bodies? Let us find, for example, how many hydrogen molecules are contained in a child’s “balloon” if the mass of all hydrogen in it is equal to $3 \text{e}$. Then the number of molecules will be equal to $3 \text{e}: 3 \cdot 10^{-24} \text{e} = 10^{24}$, that is, $1 000 000 000 000 000 000$. We have now moved from unimaginably small to unimaginably large numbers!

To create at least some idea of ​​the magnitude of this number, let’s carry out one more (last!) calculation. If you make such a thin puncture in this rubber ball that $1 000 000$ molecules come out through it every second, then it will take about 30 billion years for all the molecules to come out of the ball.
