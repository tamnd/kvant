Hence the radius of curvature of the parabola at point $M_1$
$$R_1 = \frac{v_0^2}{g \cos^3 \alpha}.$$

So, the radius of curvature at any point of the parabola is determined by the magnitude of the square of the velocity $v_0$ and the magnitude of the cube of the cosine of the angle $\alpha$ formed by the vectors of the initial velocity $v_0$ and the total velocity $v$. The radius of curvature of the parabola increases (its curvature decreases) as the flying body moves along it.

Now, using formula (4), we determine the radius of curvature at the vertex of the parabola. In this case, the angle $\alpha$ is zero, since $v_0$ is the same as $v$. So, $R = \frac{v_0^2}{g}$.

The same result could be obtained immediately. At the initial moment of motion of the body, it experiences only centripetal acceleration
$$g = \frac{v_0^2}{R}.$$

Let us find the radius of curvature of the parabola in the vicinity of the parabia points of the body, where the speed tangent to the trajectory of motion is directed almost vertically. In this case, $R = \frac{v_0^2}{g \cos^3 \alpha} \rightarrow \infty$, since $\alpha \rightarrow \frac{\pi}{2}$, and $\cos \alpha \rightarrow 0$. Note that now the acceleration of gravity is directed almost tangentially to the trajectory, and the role of centripetal acceleration has been reduced to zero.

Let us now find the radius of curvature at a point $M_2$ of the parabola where the tangential velocity $v_2$ is twice the initial velocity $v_0$:
$$R_2 = \frac{v_0^2}{g \cos^3 \alpha} = \frac{v_0^2}{g} \frac{1}{\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^3} = \frac{8v_0^2}{g},$$

since the angle is $\alpha = 60^\circ$.

The second possibility, implemented in our funny example, actually has much more serious applications. Modern cinema and television operate on this principle.

A disk with slots (wipe) rotates in front of the lens of the film camera. Blackouts on the screen are necessary to hide the flickering of film frames. The overall frame changes 24 times per second (the wiper interrupts the light twice as often), and the angular movements of the bodies on the screen do not interrupt 0.03 rad/s relative to the viewer. Under these conditions, a familiar picture of continuous movement of an object on the screen is observed.

The electron beam of the television tube passes through the entire field of the screen line by line and returns to its original position in a time commensurate with the so-called critical small frequency (50-60 $2\mu$), at which the eye, due to the inertia of vision, does not distinguish an interrupted change in brightness from a continuous one.
