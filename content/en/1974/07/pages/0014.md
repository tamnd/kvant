12 years old (Fig. 7). But at the end of 1973, work by other astronomers was published, in which statistical analysis of photographic images of the star led to a different trajectory, for the explanation of which it is not necessary to assume the existence of planets.

Is it possible to overcome the errors of our observations and by searching for the trajectories of stars to reliably establish the existence of planets?

Observations of stars not from the earth’s surface, but from the lunar surface, can help with this - Lunya has no atmosphere, and the diameter of the images of stars on photographic plastic will be significantly smaller than when observed from the Earth. Changes from satellites located outside the Earth’s atmosphere can also help, with a sufficiently perfect system for stabilizing them in space.

A spin that isn't

Take a magazine with circles depicted on the cover in your hand and make several uniform rotational movements with your hand in the plane of the pages with the image. Figure “comes to life”: the circles begin to spin. It is not difficult to “twist” the circles in the other direction. How is this nillusion committed?

Our eye has the ability to retain a visual impression for a fraction of a second after the visible object has already disappeared from the field of view. The same applies to the part of the image that we observe. When we move our figure parts of the concentric circles, contrasting in brightness, move on the retina in time with the circular movement of the hands. We cannot distinguish one detail from another in a monochromatic circle, but each luminous element of the circle remains in our perception at a certain place somewhat longer than in reality. This is how the illusion of moving the elements of light circles arises, and when the speed approaches, a complete illusion of rotation of the light parts of the figure arises. At this stage, the effect is similar to that which we see when observing the spokes of a rapidly turning wheel fused together.

The basis of this illusion is the so-called strokosoppic effect. It consists, firstly, in the fact that during the first observation, each rapidly rotating object is motionless, and, secondly, in the fact that the rapid change of images of individual phases of body movement gives the effect of continuous movement.
