A friend of mine recently returned from a trip to London. A talented mathematician, he brilliantly completed his doctoral dissertation when he was not yet thirty years old. Despite his youth, he gained authority in science, his works were translated into many languages, and so he was offered to go to Angllud to meet and exchange experiences with foreign colleagues. On this trip, the Soviet delegation was accompanied by an interpreter, and the young scientist, although he did not speak English, felt confident, not even aware of the difficulties that lay ahead of him.

“I was like a deaf-mute there,” he lamented upon arrival, “there was no contact with foreign mathematicians.” I made my report with the help of a translator, and that was the end of it. Almost no one speaks Russian there, and I don’t speak English. I read literature in my specialty in three European languages, but I cannot speak any of them. Translator? The translator knows the language well, but not mathematics. I tried to conduct a scientific conversation through an interpreter, but it turned out to be very long and somehow helpless, since he usually could not even understand the essence of the problem. Is it really possible to require a translator to have knowledge of mathematics, physics and other sciences? But it would not hurt me to master English speaking skills at school, instruction and graduate school, where I studied this language. Then I did not attach any knowledge to him and, having “passed” him for thirteen years, we have actually misunderstood him. So I'm punished. The trip, from which I expected so much and which could have brought me so much benefit, turned out to be empty...”

So, another person who would, with self-satisfaction, flog himself for limiting himself.

Self-organization, by the way, being a rare phenomenon in essence, has become almost a pattern in the study of foreign languages. And it’s easy to agree on the reason for this pattern after reading Peter Hagboldt’s statement.

In the well-known collection “Physicists Prodolzhayutshit” (M., “Mir”, 1968) a short story was published about what representatives of a typical “non-linguistic” profession thought about the advisability of studying foreign languages.

They say that the great physicist Gibbs was a very reserved person and usually remained silent at meetings of the academic council of the university where he taught. But at one of the meetings, when the question of what to devote more space to in the new curricula - mathematics or foreign languages ​​- was being decided, he could not resist and made a speech. “Mathematics is a language!” - he said.

My friend also shared the same opinion. Until some time.

What language did you actually give your report in?

But is anyone trying to figure out how well to master a foreign language in order to use it in their work without getting into a funny situation? Nobody seems to be trying to do this.
