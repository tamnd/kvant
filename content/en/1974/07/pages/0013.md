The experimental difficulty of detecting planets is shown by the example of Barnard's star.

There was a star near our Sun that was very suitable for its planets. This is Barnard's Star, named after the amateur astronomer who discovered it. This star is faint, but it moves at a fairly high speed - 10.3″ per year (1/360 degree). Its distance from Earth is 5.9 light years. Closer to it is only a double star in the constellation Centaurus. Figure 6 shows the trajectory of Barnard's star modified by astronomer Vande Kamp and the orbits of its planets calculated from this trajectory.

30 years (from 1937 to 1967) were observed. Over the years, van de Kamp studied and measured over 3,000 photographs of the star with an accuracy of 0.01″, that is, up to 3 millionths of a degree. In the photographs, angular mixing also produced a linear mixing of 6 microns.

It was initially assumed that Barnard's Star has one planet with an orbital period of 25 years, the mass of this planet is about 1.5 times the mass of Jupiter. A more careful analysis of the star's trajectory led in 1969 to the hypothesis of two planets with orbital periods of 26 years and 12 years and masses approximately equal to the mass of Jupiter. The orbits of the planets are almost circular, the distances from the star are 2.8 and 4.7 astronomical units.
