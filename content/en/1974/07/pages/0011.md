⟦folio 9⟧

Icarus. figure 2 shows the orbit of Icarus: at perihelion it approaches the Sun at a distance of $28 \cdot 10^6$ km, that is, twice as close as Mercury, and at aphelion it goes beyond the orbit of Mars.

At perihelion and aphelion—the two extreme points of the orbit—the acceleration is perpendicular to the velocity. At these two moments (points 1 and 2 on Fig. 2), the force of gravity towards the Sun imparts to Icarus a centripetal acceleration $a = v^2/R$, but, on the other hand,

$$a = \gamma M/R^2.$$

Icarus perihelion passes very quickly and spends most of its time far from the Sun, so its average temperature is quite low.

The fact that satellites with a highly elongated orbit spend most of their time not in perihelion, not in close luminaries, but, on the contrary, in aphelion, far from it, is used when launching scientific satellites of the Earth. Their orbit is elongated, so the most interesting part of the path - far from the Earth - they pass slowly and give detailed readings about the properties of the environment.

However, the flight to Icarus has its own difficulties - these are the small size and high speed of the astroid relative to the Earth.

It is difficult to “get into” Icarus.

It is not necessary to study the matter of astroids in their “homeland” - beyond the orbit of Mars. Plans to artificially change the orbit are being discussed. One of these ways is an explosion on the astroid. Explosive gases will fly in one direction, and the astroid (according to the law of conservation of momentum) will fly in the other, while it will slightly change its orbit (Fig. 3), acquiring an additional speed $\Delta v$. The Earth, with its gravity, will complete the rest - the astroid will become a satellite of the Earth, convenient for study.

Planets of other stars

Planets of other stars are not visible in telescopes. Nevertheless, these planets were discovered.

The star is clearly visible, and the motion of the star is influenced by its gravity by its invisible satellite - the planet. Planets are detected by star deviations from linear motion. External forces on the star system - the planet - do not act (other stars are far away), so the center of mass moves clearly in a straight line and, moreover, evenly.
