IS IT POSSIBLE TO WEIGH
MOLECULE
N. A. RODINA

In this article we want to introduce you to one of the ways to determine the mass of molecules.

It is known that the Earth’s atmosphere consists of different gases - nitrogen, oxygen, hydrogen, carbon dioxide and others. The height of the atmosphere is high; It has been established that it extends up to 2000 km from the Earth's surface, but the bulk of the gas is below. The lower layer of the atmosphere, which is only 16 km high, contains 0.9 of its mass.

Why do molecules “settle”? The reason is that they are acted upon by a gravity force directed towards the Earth. But why then does it “fall” on the Earth and not form a thin layer on its surface? This is caused by the continuous random movement of air molecules. Thanks to this movement, the molecules would be divided throughout the entire space of the world, if there were no gravity force that pulls them down.

This means that the height of the atmosphere, its average density, and the distribution of molecules along the height depend on the masses of the gas molecules that make up the atmosphere - after all, the greater the mass of the molecules, the greater the gravity force acting on it. For example, if the atmosphere consisted only of oxygen, then at an altitude of 5 km its density would be half that of the Earth's surface, and if it consisted only of hydrogen, then this would happen at an altitude of 80 km.

Of course, the distribution of molecules also depends on temperature, since temperature is related to the speed of movement of molecules. How do you think the above numbers would change if the Earth began to move in an orbit closer to the Sun (for example, “switched places” with Venus)? What if the Earth moved away from the Sun (“switched places” with Mars)?

But what does our story about the atmosphere have to do with determining the masses of molecules? The fact is that the French scientist Jean Perrin created something similar to the earth’s atmosphere, in which the role of molecules was played by particles, although extremely small, but still such that they could be seen in a microscope and their sizes and masses determined.

To do this, the resin was mixed with water so that a mixture was formed containing individual microsoptic droplets of the resin. This mixture is called an emulsion (for example, cream in milk, until it settles, forms an emulsion).

Even before this experiment was carried out, scientists assumed that drops of resin in water would behave in the same way as molecules in the atmosphere. The trials that Perrin began in 1908 confirmed this assumption.

The resin droplets had the shape of balls, their diameter was changed and turned out (in one of the series of experiments) to be equal to 0.00005 cm (that is, 5·10⁻⁵ cm).
