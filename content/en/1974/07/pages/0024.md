What is the curvature of a planar Linney curve?

Let's consider this question using the example of a parabola described by a body, mostly horizontal from a certain height with an initial horizontal speed $v_0$ (see schematic figure). Let’s “complete” the left side of the parabola, which could be described by the body if it were given the initial horizontal velocity in the opposite direction ($-v_0$). This part of the parabola at figure is dotted. At the vertex of the parabola (point $M$, whose coordinates are (0, 0)) we select a small arc $AMB$ such that the middle of the arc coincides with the vertex of the parabola. Now let’s select a circle of such a radius that it passes through three points of the arc $AMB$, that is, through the middle of the arc and its ends. This can be done, since it is known that through three points that do not lie on the same straight line, it is always possible to check only one circle with iritis. The reciprocal of the radius of the resulting circle $1/R$ determines the approximation of the value of the curvature of the parabola at its vertex. This approximation will be more accurate, the shorter the length of the arc $AMB$. The curvature at a point is determined by the reciprocal of the radius of this “limit” circle.

Now we determine the curvature of the parabola at an arbitrary point $M_1$. To do this, we select a small arc of the parabola $A_1M_1B_1$ such that the point $M_1$ is the midpoint of this arc. We will again select a circle of such radius that it passes through the points $A_1$, $M_1$ and $B_1$. This circle has a larger radius than in the first case ($R_1 > R$). The curvature of the parabola at the point $M_1$ is approximately $1/R_1$. In the same way, you can determine the curvature of a parabola at any point. Consequently, a paraboly can be considered as a curved line consisting of an infinitely large number of infinitely small arcs of decreasing curvature along which the body moves.

The radius of curvature of any plane curve is found using differential calculus. As for the curvature of a parabola - the trajectory of a body’s movement, more precisely, its center of gravity under the influence of attraction to the earth, it can be determined using information from mechanics.

So, at some point in time $t$ the body is at point $M_1$ of the parabola (see Fig.). During its movement, only the force of gesture acts on the body *). The gestive force imparts to all bodies the same acceleration, directed vertically downward and equal to $g$. The acceleration of the force $g$ can be decomposed into two components: $g \sin \alpha$ - directed tangentially, that is, in the direction of the total speed of the body $v$ at a given moment of time $t$ (tangential) component, and $g \cos \alpha$ - normal (centripetal) component, where $\alpha$ is the angle formed by the initial speed $v_0$ and tangential velocity $v_1$ at point $M_1$. As the body moves along the parabola, the decomposition of the constant acceleration $g$ into components will occur in different ways, since the angle $\alpha$ increases from zero to $\pi/2$. The tangential component of acceleration will increase as the body moves along the parabola, and the normal component will decrease.

Let us determine the value of the centripetal component of the acceleration of the body at the moment of time $t$ when it moves along an infinite small arc of radius $R_1$:

$$a_{\text{ис}} = \frac{v_1^2}{R_1}, \text{ или } g \cos \alpha = \frac{v_1^2}{R_1},$$

but since $v_1 = \frac{v_0}{\cos \alpha}$, then

$$g \cos \alpha = \frac{v_0^2}{R_1 \cos^2 \alpha}.$$

*) It is assumed that the height from which the body is thrown is not very large, so the change in $g$ should not be taken into account.
