IS IT POSSIBLE TO WEIGH
MOLECULE
N. A. RODINA

In this article we want to introduce you to one of the ways to determine the mass of molecules.

It is known that the Earth’s atmosphere consists of different gases - nitrogen, oxygen, hydrogen, carbon dioxide and others. The height of the atmosphere is high; It has been established that it extends up to 2000 km from the Earth's surface, but the bulk of the gas is below. The lower layer of the atmosphere, which is only 16 km high, contains 0.9 of its mass.

Why do molecules “settle”? The reason is that they are acted upon by a gravity force directed towards the Earth. But why then does it “fall” on the Earth and not form a thin layer on its surface? This is caused by the continuous random movement of air molecules. Thanks to this movement, the molecules would be divided throughout the entire space of the world, if there were no gravity force that pulls them down.

This means that the height of the atmosphere, its average density, and the distribution of molecules along the height depend on the masses of the gas molecules that make up the atmosphere - after all, the greater the mass of the molecules, the greater the gravity force acting on it. For example, if the atmosphere consisted only of oxygen, then at an altitude of 5 km its density would be half that of the Earth's surface, and if it consisted only of hydrogen, then this would happen at an altitude of 80 km.

Of course, the distribution of molecules also depends on temperature, since temperature is related to the speed of movement of molecules. How do you think the above numbers would change if the Earth began to move in an orbit closer to the Sun (for example, “switched places” with Venus)? What if the Earth moved away from the Sun (“switched places” with Mars)?

But what does our story about the atmosphere have to do with determining the masses of molecules? The fact is that the French scientist Jean Perrin created something similar to the earth’s atmosphere, in which the role of molecules was played by particles, although extremely small, but still such that they could be seen in a microscope and their sizes and masses determined.

To do this, the resin was mixed with water so that a mixture was formed containing individual microsoptic droplets of the resin. This mixture is called an emulsion (for example, cream in milk, until it settles, forms an emulsion).

Even before this experiment was carried out, scientists assumed that drops of resin in water would behave in the same way as molecules in the atmosphere. The trials that Perrin began in 1908 confirmed this assumption.

The resin droplets had the shape of balls, their diameter was changed and turned out (in one of the series of experiments) to be equal to 0.00005 cm (that is, 5·10⁻⁵ cm). As you can see, their diameter is very small, but still much larger than the diameter of the molecules (remember that the diameter can

The accuracy and conscientiousness with which these experiments were carried out is remarkable. Droplets of different sizes can be separated from each other several times to obtain homogeneous emulsions. Several series of measurements were carried out, using both “large” drops - with a diameter of 5 decimal parts of a millimeter, and small ones - with a diameter of 14 thousandths of a millimeter.

Droplet sizes were determined in different ways. One of them - it is called the method of rows - can be understood from the above photograph (Fig. 1). It shows drops of emulsion that have settled on the surface of the vessel. In some places, the drops were located in a straight line, forming a row (for example, under point “a”). By measuring the length of the row, counting the number of drops in it and knowing the magnification with which it was made, the average diameter of the drop was determined. It is known that such measurements give more accurate results than measurements of a single particle.

Find out such rows and determine the diameter of a drop of this emulsion (magnification is 5000).

When the emulsion was formed, droplets began to settle to the bottom of the vessel and settled for a long time - several months, until equilibrium was achieved. Did they all settle down around the day itself? No, the result was a picture reminiscent of the Earth’s atmosphere: near the day itself, the number of droplets was the widest, and then it gradually decreased with height. The reason for this distribution of droplets in complex emulsions is the same as for air molecules in the atmosphere! The only difference is that the resin droplets do not move on their own; they are free from all sides only from surrounding water molecules.

The entire emulsion layer, this entire little “atmosphere,” was 0.01 cm thick.

To count the number of droplets in different layers, we proceeded as follows. The emulsion was placed under a microsop (Fig. 2). First, the microscope was formed so that the lowest layer (approximately 0.0001 cm thick) was clearly visible, and the number of resin droplets in it was counted. Then the microsop was formed onto the next layer, etc. It was impossible to count the drops: they were mixed all the time, their number was huge (tens of thousands of pieces). In one experiment, Perrin counted 13,000 drops on four levels.

In Perrin's experiments, it was firmly established that droplets of resin in an emulsion are distributed under the influence of gravity exactly according to the same law as molecules in the Earth's atmosphere. Many experiments have been done

times, in different versions: they changed the resin, added glycerin to the water, and the results of the experiment remained the same. Consequently, we can rightfully compare the distribution of resin droplets in an emulsion with the distribution of molecules in the atmosphere and thus determine the masses of the molecules of gases contained in the air.

Let's use the results of one of Perrenn's experiments. The mass of one drop of resin turned out to be equal to $7,8 \cdot 10^{-15} \text{e}$. The height $h$, at which the number of such droplets was 2 times less than at the bottom, was equal to $3 \cdot 10^{-3} \text{cm}$.

Now let’s remember the data about our atmosphere: if it consisted only of oxygen, then at an altitude $H = 5 \text{km}$ the oxygen density would be 2 times less than at the Earth’s surface.

How to calculate the mass of oxygen molecules from these data? Let us denote the mass of molecules by $m$, and the mass of the drop by $M$ and write this ratio

$$\frac{m}{M} = \frac{h}{H},$$

where

$$m = \frac{Mh}{H} = \frac{7,8 \cdot 10^{-15} \text{e} \cdot 3 \cdot 10^{-3} \text{cm}}{5 \cdot 10^5 \text{cm}} = 47 \cdot 10^{-24} \text{e}.$$

Do a similar calculation for hydrogen yourself.

Currently, the masses of molecules are determined in many different ways, and the mass values ​​​​are refined. For example, the mass of oxygen molecules is now set to $54 \cdot 10^{-24} \text{e}$, and for hydrogen - $3,34 \cdot 10^{-24} \text{e}$.

How can we determine the masses of molecules of gases that are not part of the atmosphere? Here one law of nature comes to the rescue. It was discovered (this was done in 1811 by the Italian scientist A. Avogadro) that equal volumes of all gases under the same conditions contain the same number of molecules. This means that $1 \text{m}^3$ oxygen, nitrogen, neon, carbon dioxide, etc. at $0^\circ \text{C}$ and normal atmospheric pressure contain the same number of molecules as $1 \text{m}^3$ hydrogen. What then is the reason for the difference in the densities of these gases? Why, for example, is the density of neon, that is, its mass $1 \text{m}^3$, equal to $0,9 \text{кг/м}^3$, and the density of hydrogen is 10 times less - $0,09 \text{кг/м}^3$? Answer this question and determine the mass of neon molecules. You can also calculate the mass of molecules of other gases (the densities of gases are indicated in tables, for example, in the textbook “Physics 6”).

Having become acquainted with one of the methods for determining the mass of molecules, you could be convinced that these masses are very small. Is it possible to cock several molecules together? Of course not, although in modern science and technology a very sensual color is used. If you balance two pieces of paper on such a scale, and then mark a point on one of them with a pencil, then everyone will discover its mass. It would seem that it is not worth taking into account the mass of molecules. But all bodies consist of molecules, which means that the masses of bodies are made up of the masses of molecules. How many molecules are there in bodies? Let us find, for example, how many hydrogen molecules are contained in a child’s “balloon” if the mass of all hydrogen in it is equal to $3 \text{e}$. Then the number of molecules will be equal to $3 \text{e}: 3 \cdot 10^{-24} \text{e} = 10^{24}$, that is, $1 000 000 000 000 000 000$. We have now moved from unimaginably small to unimaginably large numbers!

To create at least some idea of ​​the magnitude of this number, let’s carry out one more (last!) calculation. If you make such a thin puncture in this rubber ball that $1 000 000$ molecules come out through it every second, then it will take about 30 billion years for all the molecules to come out of the ball.
