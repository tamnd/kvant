Language is both a science and a means of communication. As a science, only linguists are interested in language. But any person, regardless of his inclinations, always uses language as a means of communication. And as a means of communication, language is undoubtedly accessible to everyone, regardless of their abilities.

The opinion that every educated person must be fluent in a foreign language to one degree or another has become firmly established in our lives. And no one is trying to refute this opinion, so as not to be compromised and not to be exposed to the ignorant. Moreover, if previously a student heard about using the language (and at the same time learned it) from the fifth grade, now all this often begins already in kindergarten and does not end even with the completion of education. Thus, the number of years allocated for studying one foreign language turns out to be two parts. But this fact, undoubtedly positive, does not fully comply with the law of transition with quality. A friend of mine recently returned from a trip to London. A talented mathematician, he brilliantly completed his doctoral dissertation when he was not yet thirty years old. Despite his youth, he gained authority in science, his works were translated into many languages, and so he was offered to go to Angllud to meet and exchange experiences with foreign colleagues. On this trip, the Soviet delegation was accompanied by an interpreter, and the young scientist, although he did not speak English, felt confident, not even aware of the difficulties that lay ahead of him.

“I was like a deaf-mute there,” he lamented upon arrival, “there was no contact with foreign mathematicians.” I made my report with the help of a translator, and that was the end of it. The translator knows the language well, but not mathematics. I tried to conduct a scientific conversation through an interpreter, but it turned out to be very long and somehow helpless, since he usually could not even understand the essence of the problem. Is it really possible to require a translator to have knowledge of mathematics, physics and other sciences? So, another person who would, with self-satisfaction, flog himself for limiting himself.

The success of any activity, undoubtedly, largely depends on what methods are used to achieve it. This fully applies to the study of foreign languages. In terms of the number of methods used to study them, foreign languages ​​occupy one of the first places. True, the quality of these methods is far from equal, but each of them always finds its own fans.

Thus, the famous French method Guzn, possessing a phenomenal memory, took only a German dictionary and, contrary to Vitka L.’s main statement that this was impossible, learned all the words from it by heart. In addition, he thoroughly mastered German grammar. Believing that he had fully mastered the language, Guzn went to Germany to listen to lectures at the university.

Great, however, was his disappointment when he discovered that, despite the “baggage” he had acquired, he not only did not understand German speech, but he himself could not say anything. It turned out that he had not learned to speak and understand.

But what's the matter? Why, despite the work invested, did the “word-cut” method not bring success?

First of all, apparently, because any word lives a full life

only in a phrase. What is taken is separated, it is dead. Guzn’s entire “vocabulary” turned out to be dead.

In addition, in different sentences the same word can have not only different, but even opposite meanings. For example, the English word odds is translated as change, discord, advantage, chances.

It is quite obvious that the meaning of one of the meanings of this word does not even make it possible to guess its other meanings.

The word odds is no exception. Most words in a language have not one, but many meanings, which are derived from a variety of phrases through linguistic experience. From one sentence you can find out only one meaning of a word.

Thus, it is the language experience that reads us, not the dictionary. The dictionary is only an intermediary during the first acquaintance, and should not be abused.

What does language experience consist of and how is it acquired?

If we turn to the explanatory dictionary for the meaning of the word “experience”, we will get a specific one, which will undoubtedly help us answer the question posed. According to the explanatory dictionary, experience is a set of practical conditions of knowledge, skills, and abilities.

This means that in order to accumulate linguistic experience, it is necessary, first of all, to practical application of the acquired knowledge. In other words, in order to learn to speak and understand, write and read in a foreign language, you should do this as often as possible, using all the information available for this, therefore, when choosing books in a foreign language, it is advisable to combine business with pleasure and read what particularly interests you - be it classics, adventure literature or detective stories. In addition, high school students are recommended to read articles that interest them from foreign popular science magazines, as this prepares them for reading foreign literature on their chosen specialization.

It is also important to follow the following rules: read systematically, read at least one page of foreign text every day.

avoid translation. Translation is often compared to a crutch that we don't need when we can walk without it. “Walking” in this case means reading in such a way as to grasp the content without remaining stuck on the form. Full understanding of reading comes only with practice.

The same applies to going crazy “by ear”. “Teaching” your ear to perceive foreign speech is no less important than learning to speak, write and read in this language. Often, even people who have a good command of the language, when they find themselves abroad for the first time, feel overwhelmingly confused, not understanding anything of what is being said around them. They even have the impression that they are hearing a tape recording being played back at increased speed. However, after some time, the assessment of helplessness goes away, as the ear gradually adapts to the unusual tempo of speech.

Learning the concept “by ear” is one of the main tasks when learning a foreign language. This requires constant practice. In other words, to learn to hear, you need to listen as much as possible.

It is best to start with records, listening to the same text until a complete understanding is achieved. When choosing records in a foreign language, you should adhere to the same principle as when choosing reading materials: “from simple to complex.”

When the concept of records does not cause difficulties, you can start listening to radio broadcasts in a foreign language. Perceiving natural speech is, of course, much more difficult than working with a record or tape. But there is no need to check in and cause so many difficulties: the concept of radio broadcasting in a foreign language comes gradually. Therefore, just like reading, it is advisable to listen to speech in a foreign language every day, removing fifteen minutes from this.

Isn’t it true, it’s hard to imagine a driver who has a perfect understanding of street traffic, all the techniques for driving a car, how the car works, but... wouldn’t know how to drive it? It is no less difficult to imagine a mathematician who knows many formulas, but does not know how to use them in solving the simplest problems. But for some reason, no one cares about the fact that, having studied a foreign language for many years, having thoroughly mastered its grammar and having also learned a lot of words, many cannot bring themselves to utter a phrase in it. What's the matter?

When learning to perform mathematical operations, we make mistakes and are not ashamed of it. When learning to drive a car, the driver also inevitably makes mistakes somewhere, but continues on his way. But many of those who study a foreign language are so afraid (or embarrassed) of their side effects that they prefer to “properly” remain silent. The result speaks for itself: no one has yet learned to speak while maintaining silence.

This is also evidenced by the high extraction of the polyglot, member of the Academy of Sciences of the Estonian USSR, Professor Paul Arnetz:

“... What I love most and value most about languages ​​is the opportunity to communicate, to have a live conversation. I write my scientific papers in 15 languages ​​and speak 20 languages. These “scissors” are explained by the fact that I start learning a new language from the spoken part.

... I have been teaching foreign languages ​​at the University of Tartu for more than 30 years. My main principle is to get students to “speak” as soon as possible... Those who are shy, afraid of making a mistake in emphasis, incorrectly using this or that phrase, will never learn to speak freely. Courage, as in any other endeavor, is the key to success when learning languages.”
