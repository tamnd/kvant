Now we can move on to solving problems.

Problem 1. Is it possible to connect ten cities with non-intersecting roads so that from each city there is a path of roads leading to the path of other cities?

Solution. Let's assume that cities can be connected by roads as indicated in the problem. In this case, if any two cities are not directly connected by a road, then there will be a third city that will already be directly connected to each of them. Selecting cities as points on the plane and roads as arcs, we find that any two points are connected by a chain of arcs. Since five arcs converge at each point, the total number of arcs is $\frac{5 \cdot 10}{2} = 25$. According to Euler's theorem, these arcs divide the plane into $2 + 25 - 10 = 17$ regions. Each of these seventeenths of the area is limited by at least three arcs, since otherwise there would be two cities directly connected by at least two roads, and this contradicts the conditions of the problem. Therefore, the number of arcs is at least $\frac{3 \cdot 17}{2} = 25,5$. Thus, the initial assumption leads to a contradiction, and the cities cannot be connected to each other in the way required in the problem.

Problem 2. Three quarreling neighbors have three common wells. Is it possible to check for non-intersecting roads from each house to each well?

Solution. Let's assume that this can be done.

Having chosen the houses as blue, and the wells as black dots, and each blue point we connect another with each black dot so that the two resulting arcs do not intersect in pairs. Then any two points representing houses or wells will be connected by a chain of arcs, and by virtue of Euler’s theorem, these arcs will divide the plane into $9 - 6 + 2 = 5$ regions. Each of these seventeenths of the area is limited by at least three arcs, since according to the conditions of the problem, one of the roads should not be used to connect two houses or two wells. Therefore, the number of arcs must be at least $\frac{5 \cdot 4}{2} = 10$, and, therefore, our assumption is incorrect.

Problem 3. Prove that on every map there is a country that borders with no more than five countries.

Solution. If the number of countries on the map does not exceed six, then the statement of the problem is obvious. We will prove that on a map with more than six countries, there are even four countries, each of which borders on no more than a fifth of the countries. Let's outline the vertices and arcs of the original map in black, and mark one point in each country with red. Any two marked points lying in connecting countries (that is, countries that have a common boundary arc), we connect another within these countries so that the red arcs do not intersect in pairs. Then every two red points will be connected by a chain of arcs, and since no two constructed arcs will connect the same points, then each country on the map, consisting of a point and another red color, will be limited by no less than tri-arcs.
