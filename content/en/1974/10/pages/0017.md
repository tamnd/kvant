But what forces cause hot water to be a fountain?

The first explanations of the geyser's operation were based on the hypothesis of the existence of a large underground pool, only partially filled with water. The water in the underground pool is heated by the internal body of the Earth and boils. The vapors that form enter the tube, which is filled with water from underground channels. The steam from the pool rises through the tube, carries with it the water in the tube, and causes it to splash upward in a fountain. However, experimental studies have not confirmed this.

The mechanism of action of the geyser was first explained in the 19th century by G. Bunsen, who studied geysers in Iceland. It turns out that an underground pool is not needed at all. The tube created by the source is quite large enough for a geyser to erupt.

Bunsen measured the temperature of the water in the geyser by lowering a thermometer into the tube and adjusting the depth of the thermometer each time. figure 1 shows his results. It can be seen that as you move deeper into the geyser tube, the temperature increases. At the top of the geyser the temperature was 85.5°C, at the base it increased to 126°C.

Why doesn’t the water in the geyser boil, despite the fact that its temperature exceeds 100 degrees?

It is known that the boiling point of water depends on pressure. As the pressure decreases, the boiling point decreases, and as the pressure increases, it increases. At the top of a high mountain, water boils at about 80°C. In the geyser tube, on the contrary, the boiling point is above 100°C, because the pressure there is greater than atmospheric pressure by the pressure of the water column in the tube. This excess pressure prevents the water from boiling. At no point in the tube does the temperature of the water reach the boiling point corresponding to the pressure at that point.

Before the eruption, explosions are heard from time to time, and each explosion is accompanied by strong excitement in the pool. Apparently, this occurs because hot water and superheated steam enter the geyser tube through the side channels (see Fig. 1). The steam, mixing with colder water in the tube, separates its body. Some amount of water may evaporate, then due to the sudden expansion of the steam an explosion occurs. Frequent water, overheating from the tube into the pool, causes excitement in it.

In the intervals between eruptions, the temperature of the water in the tube rises, but even just before the eruption it never reaches boiling point.

How does an eruption occur?

The key to unraveling this phenomenon is provided by Bunsen's observations. He was able to measure the water temperature a few minutes before the eruption.

It turned out that the water temperature was slightly lower than the boiling point at this point in the tube. Thus, the actual water temperature 10 meters from the bottom of the geyser was 122°C, and the boiling point temperature, taking into account the water pressure in this place, was 124°C.
