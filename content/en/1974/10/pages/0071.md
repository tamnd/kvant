The sequence is especially simple

$$\Pi_{11} = (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, \dots),$$

called a sequence of natural numbers, in which each subsequent term is 1 more than the previous one.

This sequence can be compared with any other infinite sequence - each member of the sequence is assigned its serial number: the first member is the number 1; the second term is the number 2, ... the $k$th term, which is often called the general term, is the number $k$. These members themselves are usually designated as follows:

$$(a_1, a_2, a_3, \dots, a_k, \dots).$$

So, for example, in the sequence $\Pi_1$ the second term is $a_2 = 4$; third term $a_3 = 6$, $k$th term $a_h = 2k$. Thus, a number sequence is a set of ordered pairs of numbers $[k, a_k]$, in which the first number (number) is a natural number, and the second (the term itself) is a real number. In addition, there is a one-to-one mapping of nature that is nothing more than a function. Therefore, number sequences are a special class of function. The domain of definition of such functions is the set of $[1, 2, 3, 4, 5, \dots, k, \dots]$ natural numbers for infinite sequences and mns-

$\{1, 2, 3, \dots, n\}$ is for finite ones (here $n$ is the number of the last member of the finite sequence). For example, the sequence $\Pi_7$ consists of six terms, for it $n = 6$.

Now it is not difficult for us to give a clear definition of the concept of “numeric sequence”.

Numerical sequences are a function, the domain of definition of a componkh is the set of natural numbers $\{1, 2, 3, \dots, k, \dots\}$ or a subset of its first $n$ elements $\{1, 2, 3, \dots, n\}$, and the domain of change is the set of natural numbers.

Expressing the $k$th term through its number $k$: $a_k = f(k)$ allows you to specify the entire sequence.

This method of specifying a sequence is called analytical.

As an example, note that the common term of the sequence $\Pi_6 = (1, 4, 9, 16, 25, \dots)$ is $a_k = k^2$.

The analytical sequence is very similar to the analytical way of specifying a function - only when writing a function, the independent variable is denoted by $x$, and the dependent variable by $y$. Let's say the formula $y = 2x$ is an analytical notation for a linear function. If the domain of its definition is considered to be the set of all real numbers, then the graph of the function $y = 2x$ will be a straight line (Fig. 1). The equality $a_h = 2k$ is an analytical assignment of a sequence of four numbers (the independent variable $k$ takes only natural values).
