If $m \parallel n$, then there are no solutions. If the angle between lines $m$ and $n$ is equal to $60^\circ$, then lines $m''$ and $n''$ can coincide, and in this case a whole family of solutions is obtained. This will happen only when the parallel to the frame formed by the straight lines $m$, $n$, $m'$ and $n'',$ is a rhombus with an angle $60^\circ$ at the vertex $O$.

Finally, if the point $O$ coincides with the intersection point of the lines $m$ and $n$, then the triangle either degenerates into a point, or the solution is a whole family of regular triangles, in which case the angle between the lines $m$ and $n$ is equal to $60^\circ$.

Let us note in conclusion that if we slightly change the formulation of the problem—assuming that the lines $m$ and $n$ contain not the altitudes, but the bisectors of the triangle—then we get a problem that is, in some senses, dual to the solution. **M247.** Square $6 \times 6$ needs to be filled with 12 flat ones, of which $k$ has the shape of a wall, and the remaining 12 - $k$ a straight wall (Fig. 1). At what $k$ is this possible?

If $k$ is even, then the square can be filled with ueolks and the straight line with ueolniks (Fig. 3. Fig. 4. Fig. 5.)

It remains to analyze the case of $k = 1$ and $k = 3$. It turns out that in these cases installation is impossible.

1) Let $k = 1$. Let us enclose the rows of the square (Fig.4) and assign to each cell of the rectangle (or square) the number of the row in which it is located. It is easy to notice that the sum of the row numbers (in cells) for any rectangle is divisible by 3, but for any corner it is not divisible. The sum of the row numbers of the square (by cells) is divided by 3, which means that with $k = 1$ stacking is impossible.

2) Let $k = 3$. Let us outline the rows and columns of the square (Fig. 5).

Let us associate two numbers with each of the figures: the sum of the row numbers and the sum of the column numbers in which the cells of this figure are located. For rectangles, both these numbers are divisible by 3, but for angles they are not divisible. The remainders obtained when dividing the numbers corresponding to the corners by 3 are determined only by the type of corner and do not change if you move the corner around the square without turning it over (see figure 5 and 6; figure 6 shows an example of such a move; the numbers of the rows and columns in which the cells lie are shown in the cells).

The sum of the row numbers and the sum of the column numbers (in cells) for the entire square is divided by 3; Consequently, the third corner of a square with the corners and nine corners of a rectangle is possible only when all three corners are of the same type. Due to the symmetry of the square, we will assume that all the corners are the same as on figure 6.
