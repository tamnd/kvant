Geysers *), hot springs, mineral springs - all these are echoes of volcanic activity.

Geysers are hot springs in which boiling water erupts at certain intervals. The eruption of a large geyser is a very beautiful sight. With an explosion and thunder, a column of boiling water, shrouded in steam, flies up like a fountain, crumbling at a great height. The fountain will last for some time, then the water disappears, the steam dissipates, and everything calms down. Usually there is a pool around the geyser, sometimes several meters in diameter. The ground near the geysers is warm or even hot.

Geysers and hot springs are located in places of active volcanic activity that has occurred or has recently ceased. They are found in Kamchatka (about 100 geysers), in Spain, in New Zealand, in North America (Yellowston National Park), in Japan, in China. In Spain, in the valleys of almost all rivers, steam rising from boiling water and geysers is visible. The heat from hot springs is used there to heat liquids and greenhouses. There is even a river in Kamchatka called Geysernaya. In the valley of this river there are about 20 large and many small geysers. The largest geyser in Kamchatka, the Giant, throws out a stream of water up to 40 m high, and steam up to several hundred meters. Small geysers raise water just a few centimeters above the ground.

The activity of geysers, as a rule, is periodic, but the time intervals between eruptions vary from geyser to geyser. The eruption of some geysers occurs like a blast

a few minutes, others erupt 1-2 times a month. The interval between eruptions may be constant or may vary. The Old Sluzhaka geyser (Jelewston National Park) has a fairly strict periodicity; the period of its eruptions ranges from 53 to 70 minutes. The stream of water from Old Servants rises to a height of up to 40 m. The eruptions rise to a height, remain for some time and disappear. Before the steam has time to dissipate, a new column of boiling water flies out, followed by another and another. The fountain of the Great Geyser reaches a height of 30 m.

If you look into the geyser basin after the eruption, you will see that there is no water in it, and there is a hole in the bottom - a channel going into the depths. Before the eruption begins, water rises through the channel, fills the pool, boils, splashes out, and then a fountain of boiling water appears. When the eruption stops, the water flows back into the geyser tube. For example, the Great Geyser has a tube diameter of 3 m and a depth of 23 m. On the surface of the earth, the tube ends in a pool 16 m wide and 18 m long.

The inner surface of the tube and the basin is covered with a very even layer of silica, so hard that it cannot be broken by hammer blows. Silica deposits are called geysers. Near some geysers, cones of geyserite form from a few centimeters to several meters in height.

How did this amazing geizka form? How did the lining of the geyska and the pool come about?

It can be assumed that this is due to deposits of substances contained in the water. However, the geyser water does not produce sediment; Once poured into a bottle, it remains clean for years.

And only if you evaporate water in an evaporation cup, then at the edges, where evaporation occurs faster, silica will be deposited and a siliceous ring will form. In the middle, even after prolonged boiling, the water may only become cloudy.

Now imagine a warm spring, the water of which flows down a small slope. Spreading, the water quickly evaporates and the silica settles. This sediment is gradually returned to the spoon through which the water flows. In this way, the geysin of the spring well will increase until, finally, a geyzka and a pool are formed.

But what forces cause hot water to be a fountain?

The first explanations of the geyser's operation were based on the hypothesis of the existence of a large underground pool, only partially filled with water. The water in the underground pool is heated by the internal body of the Earth and boils. The vapors that form enter the tube, which is filled with water from underground channels. The steam from the pool rises through the tube, carries with it the water in the tube, and causes it to splash upward in a fountain. However, experimental studies have not confirmed this.

The mechanism of action of the geyser was first explained in the 19th century by G. Bunsen, who studied geysers in Iceland. It turns out that an underground pool is not needed at all. The tube created by the source is quite large enough for a geyser to erupt.

Bunsen measured the temperature of the water in the geyser by lowering a thermometer into the tube and adjusting the depth of the thermometer each time. figure 1 shows his results. It can be seen that as you move deeper into the geyser tube, the temperature increases. At the top of the geyser the temperature was 85.5°C, at the base it increased to 126°C.

Why doesn’t the water in the geyser boil, despite the fact that its temperature exceeds 100 degrees?

It is known that the boiling point of water depends on pressure. As the pressure decreases, the boiling point decreases, and as the pressure increases, it increases. At the top of a high mountain, water boils at about 80°C. In the geyser tube, on the contrary, the boiling point is above 100°C, because the pressure there is greater than atmospheric pressure by the pressure of the water column in the tube. This excess pressure prevents the water from boiling. At no point in the tube does the temperature of the water reach the boiling point corresponding to the pressure at that point.

Before the eruption, explosions are heard from time to time, and each explosion is accompanied by strong excitement in the pool. Apparently, this occurs because hot water and superheated steam enter the geyser tube through the side channels (see Fig. 1). The steam, mixing with colder water in the tube, separates its body. Some amount of water may evaporate, then due to the sudden expansion of the steam an explosion occurs. Frequent water, overheating from the tube into the pool, causes excitement in it.

In the intervals between eruptions, the temperature of the water in the tube rises, but even just before the eruption it never reaches boiling point.

How does an eruption occur?

The key to unraveling this phenomenon is provided by Bunsen's observations. He was able to measure the water temperature a few minutes before the eruption.

It turned out that the water temperature was slightly lower than the boiling point at this point in the tube. Thus, the actual water temperature 10 meters from the bottom of the geyser was 122°C, and the boiling point temperature, taking into account the water pressure in this place, was 124°C.

We have already said that before the eruption, explosions are observed, caused by the flow of steam from underground channels into the geyser tube and accompanied by a rising water column. The boiling point at reduced pressure will be 121°C. Therefore, the actual temperature of water at level A (122°C) is already one degree higher than the boiling point. The water in the tube immediately begins to boil, steam is formed, which raises the water even higher and freezes it to float into the loading pool. The pressure on the lower layers of water decreases even more noticeably, the boiling becomes more and more violent, and more and more vapors are formed. Suddenly, the entire mass of water located at the very bottom of the tube begins to boil; Then, at the top, the elephants throw water up with enormous speed, and a geyser erupts.

The ejected water falls back into the pool, cools and after a while begins to flow back into the tube and gradually fills. The eruption is over. From time to time, explosions are heard and water surges are visible, but the next emission begins only when the water in the tube reaches a temperature close to the boiling point.

The eruption of a natural geyser cannot continue indefinitely. When the tube reaches such a length that the temperature of the water in the lower layers, due to too much pressure, can no longer reach the boiling point, the eruption should stop. The spring nevertheless continues to deposit silica, and a beautiful reservoir is formed. The depth of some of these reservoirs is from 10 to 12 m.

We have already said that the temperature of the water in the geyser tube during “rest” never reaches the boiling point. Therefore, steam, spontaneously exploding and raising the water column, must form in the side channels feeding the tube. But a geyser can “work” without these channels if there is an additional atok tella slightly below the middle of its tube. This can be shown on a working model of a geyser, which is not difficult to do yourself. A general view of the installation of such an artificial geyser is shown in figure 2.

You need to take a two-meter metal tube and install it vertically. A vessel should be secured at the top of the tube, large enough so that the hot water does not splash out, but is collected in the vessel and can flow back into the tube. The entire structure must be securely closed. Since it deals with hot water and steam, it is necessary to be very careful when creating an artificial geyser. To operate such a geyser, you need two tell sources: one at the bottom, and the second slightly below the center of the tube. As sources of body, you can take large tin cans filled with hot coals. You need to cut a hole in two jars, slightly smaller in diameter than the diameter of the tube, and, after heating the jar without coals, fit it tightly onto the tube. If the responsible is not too large, and the jar is well heated, then when the jar and tube are heated at the same time, the jar will firmly hold on to the place of the tube where it was inserted. The eruption of such a geyser occurs in about 5-6 minutes.
