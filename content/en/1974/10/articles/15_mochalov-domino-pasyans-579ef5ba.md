⟦folio 62⟧

take the segment $CA_1 = D_1B_1$ and connect the point $A_1$ with the point $B_1$. Quadrilateral $A_1B_1D_1C$ is a square with diagonal $B_1C$.

The process of constructing squares can be continued. In this case we get an infinite sequence of segments

$$CD_1 > CD_2 > CD_3 > CD_4 > \dots > 0;$$

each of them is the difference between the diagonal and the side of the same square; it is easy to find expressions for their length:

$$CD_1 = CB - AB, CD_2 = CB_1 - A_1B_1, \dots$$

Now let the diagonal and the side of the original square be commensurate, that is, there is such a segment $e$ - a unit of length - which fits an integer number of times both in the diagonal $BC$ and in the side $AB$ of the square, that is, the lengths of the segments $BC$ and $AB$ are expressed in integers. In this case, both the length of the segment $CD_1 = CB - AB$ is expressed as an integer, and the length of the diagonal $B_1C$ of the square $A_1CD_1B_1$, equal to the difference $AC - AB_1 = AB - CD_1$, is an integer.

So, we have built an infinite sequence of killing positive integers

$$CD > CD_1 > CD_2 > \dots > CD_h > \dots > 0$$

(here $CD_i$ is the length of the segment $CD_i$), although it cannot be infinite. The resulting contradiction proves the inexhaustibility of the diagonal of a square and its sides.
