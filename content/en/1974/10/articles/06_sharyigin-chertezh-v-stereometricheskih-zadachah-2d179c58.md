Drawing in stereometric problems
I. F. Sharytsin

When checking written work submitted to calculation training assignments, the following is often discovered: in pure geometric tasks, a drawing is accompanied quite well, and what is depicted in the draft can only be called a drawing conditionally. And one can only wonder how, with the help of such a “drawing”, the applicants still managed to solve the problem. It is no secret that a good drawing can provide significant assistance in solving geometric problems, especially problems in stereometry. Of course, learning to make such drawings as the artists «Kvant» do is hardly possible for an ordinary school student. However, if you pay attention to the quality of the drawing when solving each geometric problem, then in the end you will learn to do it quite well. Make it a rule not to start solving a problem until you have done a good drawing. Do not use paper, make your drawing large, draw invisible lines with dotted lines, do not draw auxiliary lines until you are sure of their necessity. Very often, stereometric problems will be reduced to one or several problems in planometrics. Make a separate drawing for each task. Balls, as a rule, should not be depicted; It is enough to indicate their center, points of contact with a straight line, plane or other balls. The fact that two balls are touching means that the distance between their centers is equal to the sum of the radii if the touch is external, and the same distance is equal to the difference of the radii if the touch is internal.

Let's move on to examples.

Problem 1 (MSU, Faculty of Chemistry, 1968). Inside the sphere of space there are four balls of radius r. Which of these balls touches the other three and the surface of the sphere. Determine the radius of the sphere.

Solution. Let A, B, C and D be the centers of balls of radius r, and O be the center of the sphere (Fig. 1). From the fact that the balls touch each other in pairs externally, it follows that ABCD is a regular tetrahedron with edge 2r. All balls touch internally with center O, therefore the distances AO, BO, CO, DO are equal to each other and equal to R - r, where R is the desired radius of the sphere. Problem 2 (MIPT, 1966). The edge of a regular tetrahedron ABCD is equal to a. A sphere is built on edge AB, as on a diameter. Find the radius of a ball circumscribed by a trihedral angle of a tetrahedron with its vertex at point A and tangent to the constructed sphere.

Solution. Let $O$ be the midpoint, $AB$ be the center of the given sphere, $O_1$ be the center of the desired ball. It is easy to report that $O_1$ lies at the height AM of the tetrahedron ABCD (Fig. 2).

Let us denote by $x$ the radius of the ball with center at point $O_1$. Since this ball is inscribed in a trihedral angle A, it follows that $AO_1 = 3x$. In fact, for any ball circumscribed by a trihedral angle A, the ratio of the distance from its center to the vertex A to the radius is a constant. Consider a ball described in a regular tetrahedron ABCD; its center, as is known, coincides with the center of the circumscribed sphere; the ratio of the radii of the circumscribed and circumscribed spheres for a regular tetrahedron is easily calculated and equals 3.

Let's depict $\triangle AMB$ on a separate drawing ($\times M$ - straight line) (Fig. 3). Consider $\triangle AOO_1$.

We have

$$AO_1 = 3x, \quad AO = \frac{a}{2},$$

$$O_1O = \frac{a}{2} \pm x.$$

(At figure 3 the case of an internal tangency is depicted. In the case of an external tangency, the point $O_1$ must be on the continuation of AM beyond the point M.)

From the right triangle AMB we find cos $\angle O_1AO$, and then, by writing the cosine theorem for $\triangle OAO_1$, we find x.

Answer: $\frac{a}{8} \left( \sqrt{6} \pm 1 \right)$.

Problem 5 (the formulation of this problem is a re-phasing of the problem given in 1973 at the Olympiad at MIPT). The axes of three equal, pairwise touching cylindrical surfaces are mutually perpendicular. The radius of the smallest ball touching all three surfaces if the radius of each of them is equal to $r$.

Solution. Consider the cube $ABCDA_1B_1C_1D_1$ with edge $2r$. We can assume that edge $AA_1$ lies on the axis of one cylinder, edge $DC$ on the axis of another, and, finally, edge $B_1C_1$ on the axis of the third (Fig. 7).

Let us prove that the center of the desired ball coincides with the center $O$ of the cube.

Indeed, consider three cylinders, concentric and passing through the point $O$. Then $O$ is the only point lying inside or on the surface of all these three cylinders, and therefore, for any point other than $O$, the distance to one of the three lines $AA_1$, $DC$ and $B_1C_1$ is greater than the distances from $O$ to these lines.

Answer: $r(\sqrt{2} - 1)$.

Task 7. We fall a light beam onto a flat mirror at an angle α. The mirror rotates through an angle β around the projection of liu onto the mirror. At what angle will the reflected line deviate?

Let $A$ be a certain point on the liu, $B$ the point at which the liu falls on the mirror, $A_1$ the point symmetrical to $A$ with respect to the given mirror, and $A_2$ the point symmetrical to $A$ with respect to the surface of the surface $A$ with respect to the surface $A_1$ and $A_2$ from point $A$ to these mirrors (Fig. 9). Then $BA_1$ and $BA_2$ are continuations of the reflected rays and, therefore, the desired angle is equal to $\not\prec A_1BA_2$.

In the tetrahedron $AA_1A_2B$ (see Fig. 9), $D$ and $C$ are the midpoints of the edges $AA_1$ and $AA_2$, respectively; plane BDC (surface plane $A_2$) is perpendicular to edge $AA_2$, $AB = A_1B = A_2B$, $\not\prec ABA_1 = 2\alpha$, $\not\prec A_1AA_2 = \beta$. From here we easily find:

$$A_1A_2 = 2DC = 2AD \sin \beta = 2AB \sin \alpha \sin \beta.$$

Now from $\Delta A_1BA_2$ we find the required angle.

Answer: 2 arcsin ($\sin \alpha \sin \beta$).

To illustrate a rather difficult stereometric problem with a simple drawing, you must have a well-developed spatial imagination. This is achieved through practice and only practice.
