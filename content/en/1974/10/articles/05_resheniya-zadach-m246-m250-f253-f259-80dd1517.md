M246. On the plane there are two straight lines $m$ and $n$ and a point $O$. Construction of a triangle whose two altitudes lie on the given lines $m$ and $n$, and the center of the circumscribed circle is at point $O$.

In order not to consider various cases of relative positions of straight lines $m$, $n$ and points $O$, we will solve the problem without a drawing.

Let us first assume that the point $O$ does not coincide with the intersection point of the lines $m$ and $n$. Let's note the following:

1) If through the point $O$ we draw lines $m'$ and $n'$, parallel lines $m$ and $n$, respectively, then we should obtain perpendiculars passing through the midpoints of the side of the desired triangle (this immediately follows from the fact that $O$ is the center of the circumscribed circle).

2) The straight line $m''$, symmetrical to the straight line $n'$, must not pass through the vertex of the desired triangle (say, through the point $C$). Similarly, line $n''$, symmetrical to line $n$ relative to line $m'$, must also not pass through point $C$. (Both of these statements follow from the first remark.)

3) If the lines $m''$ and $n''$ intersect (and they may not intersect; that’s why we wrote the word up to false on a) everywhere in 1) and 2), then the point of their intersection is the vertex $C$ of the desired triangle.

Once vertex $C$ is found, vertices $A$ and $B$ are easy to find: we know the directions of sides $CA$ and $CB$ and that they intersect with lines $m$ and $n$ at points $A$ and $B$, respectively.

So, we have described the construction process. However, it remains unclear whether this construction always leads to a goal; in other words, is it possible to arbitrarily define the lines $m$, $n$ and the point $O$? From our construction it follows that this is not so: if the lines $m''$ and $n''$ are parallel, then the vertex $C$ cannot be found. Check that lines $m''$ will be parallel to $n''$ if and only if either the acute angle between lines $m$ and $n$ is equal to $60^\circ$, or when $m \parallel n$.

Fig. 1. Fig. 2.

If $m \parallel n$, then there are no solutions. If the angle between lines $m$ and $n$ is equal to $60^\circ$, then lines $m''$ and $n''$ can coincide, and in this case a whole family of solutions is obtained. This will happen only when the parallel to the frame formed by the straight lines $m$, $n$, $m'$ and $n'',$ is a rhombus with an angle $60^\circ$ at the vertex $O$.

Finally, if the point $O$ coincides with the intersection point of the lines $m$ and $n$, then the triangle either degenerates into a point, or the solution is a whole family of regular triangles, in which case the angle between the lines $m$ and $n$ is equal to $60^\circ$.

Let us note in conclusion that if we slightly change the formulation of the problem—assuming that the lines $m$ and $n$ contain not the altitudes, but the bisectors of the triangle—then we get a problem that is, in some senses, dual to the solution. (However, it can be easily solved without using this duality.) Try to solve this problem and understand what the duality consists of.

($i=1, 2, 3$).

Prove that the quantity $\phi$ is two-edged at the edge $B'D$ of the metrahedron $B'DKM$ and does not depend on the choice of points $K$ and $M$. Find this quantity $\phi$.

Let us present the shortest solution to this problem, using only the symmetry message.

Let us denote the point of contact of the ball with the KDM plane by $L$, and the diagonal of the cube $B'D$ by $d$. Let us draw through $d$ six planes $\alpha, \chi, \lambda, \mu, \gamma$ and $\beta$, passing the corresponding points $A'$, $K, L, M, C'$ and $B$.

⟦folio 26⟧

The dihedral angle $\Rightarrow \alpha d\gamma$ (the angle between planes $\alpha$ and $\gamma$) is equal to $120^\circ$, since when the cube is rotated $120^\circ$ around the diagonal $d$, the angles $\Rightarrow \alpha d\gamma$, $\Rightarrow \gamma d\beta$ and $\Rightarrow \beta d\alpha$ are aligned with each other.

But $\Rightarrow \alpha d\chi = \Rightarrow xd\lambda$: the points at which two planes - KDM and KDA' - passing through the line KD, touch the ball, are located, symmetrical to each other relative to the plane KB'D, passing through the center of the ball, and therefore the planes $\alpha$ and $\lambda$ are also symmetrical to each other relative to the plane $x$. Likewise, $\Rightarrow \lambda d\mu = \Rightarrow \mu d\gamma$.

Since the sum of the angles $\Rightarrow \alpha d\lambda + \Rightarrow \lambda d\gamma = \Rightarrow \alpha d\gamma$ is $120^\circ$, the sum of their halves $\varphi = \Rightarrow xd\lambda + \Rightarrow \lambda d\mu = \Rightarrow xd\mu$ is $60^\circ$.

Most readers, like the author N.F. Shartsein, solved this problem by calculation and obtained the following interesting interval result: the sum of the length $|KD'| + |D'M|$ is always equal to the length of the edge of the cube (that is, just like the value of the angle $\varphi$, it does not depend on the position of the point $L$).

M250. a) When two of King Arthur *) knights gathered. Some of them are at enmity with each other, but each knight has at least $\frac{n}{2}$ others among those gathered. Let us prove that Merlin, King Arthur’s advisor, can seat a knight at a round table so that the edges of the cube (that is, just like the size of the angle $\varphi$, do not depend on the position of the point $L$).

Problem M250 b) is given here in a precise formulation. This is a very difficult task. The article “Problems about counts and the fairy tale “Ivan Tsarevich and the Gray Wolf” will be devoted to its solution, which can be read in the next issue of our magazine.

Let us present the solution M250 a).

When seating knights at a round table or on the same rock (that is, in a row), we will always mean that they are neighbors as friends. We will need such a fact.

Lemma. Let $k$ knights sit on one rock, and let each of the two outermost knights have more than half of their friends. Then these $k$ knights can be seated at a round table.

*) See Mark Twain, A Yankee with King Arthur's Two.

Proof. Let's denote the knights from left to right in the order they sit by $B_1, B_2, \dots, B_k$.

If $B_1$ and $B_k$ are friends, then the statement of the lemma is obvious. Consider all those knights to the left of whom sits the friend of the knight $B_k$. There are at least $k/2$ of them. Therefore, there will be a friend of the knight $B_1$. Let's denote it $B_s$ (then $B_{s-1}$ is a friend of $B_k$). Let's move the knights in this order:

$B_1, B_2, \dots, B_{s-1}, B_k, B_{k-1}, \dots, B_s$.

Since $B_1$ and $B_s$ are friends, the knights can already be seated at the table.

The statement of the lemma is proven. Telery proves that $n$ knight is always on the same rock. Suppose we have already managed to seat $r$ knights ($r < n$) on the rock; Let us show that then one more knight can be placed on the rock.

Two cases are possible: 1) the extreme knights have all their friends already sitting on the rock; 2) the extreme knights have friends who are not sitting on the rock. In the second case, it is obvious that another knight can be placed on the edge.

If the first case occurs, then $r$ the knights sitting on the rock, according to the lemma, can also be seated at the round table. Let us denote the knights already sitting at the table in order by $B_1, B_2, \dots, B_r$. Let's take knight $A$ from among $n - r$ unseated knights, who is familiar with some knight sitting at the table, for example with $B_m(m < r)$ (there is one, since $r \leq \frac{n}{2}$), or $n - r \leq \frac{n}{2}$). Telery $r + 1$ knights can be seated on the rock like this:

$A, B_m, B_{m+1}, \dots, B_r, B_1, B_2, \dots, B_m-1$.

Reasoning similarly, we find that it is gradually possible to seat all $n$ knights on one rock. Applying the lemma to $n$ knights sitting on the rock, we point them to the round table.

V. L. Gutenkhacher

F253. The ball, depicted as a solid dielectric, was placed in a single-voltage electric field with a voltage of $E$. In this case, the dielectric turns out to be polarized. Using the principle of superposition, find the electric field voltage at the center of the ball and at points at a distance of $r$ from the center of the ball ($r$ less than the radius of the ball).

Dielectric molecules can be represented as occupancies of length $l$ with charges $+q$ and $-q$ at the ends. The number of molecules per unit volume is $n$.

⟦folio 27⟧

Fig. 18.

In an external electric field, all dumbbells are oriented along the field (Fig. 18, a), and charges appear on the ball, distributed in a certain way over its surface. Inside the ball, where the densities of positive and negative charges are equal, the charges cancel each other.

If you look at the given figure, you can imagine that a ball of a fully polarized dielectric is equivalent to two balls laid out inside each other. One ball is charged only negatively, the other - only positively (Fig. 18, b, and f). The centers of these balls are displaced relative to each other by a distance of $l$ (the length of the dumbbell), and relative to the center of the real ball they are displaced by a distance of $\frac{l}{2}$ (Fig. 19).

Thus, the field at any point consists of the external field and the fields created by positively and negatively charged balls. Therefore, first we will solve an auxiliary problem: we will find the direction of the field inside a uniformly charged ball at a distance $r$ from its center.

As is known *), the direction of the field inside a uniformly charged sphere is zero, and outside the sphere the field is the same as if it were created by an exact charge located in the center of the sphere. The volume of a uniformly charged ball is divided into thin spherical elephants, the thickness of which is much less than the ball. According to the superrosicini principle, the field direction at a distance $r$ from the center of the ball is equal to the sum of the field directions of the charges of such spherical elephants. But the direction of the field created by the charges of those elephants whose radii are greater than $r$ is equal to zero, and the direction of the layer whose radii are less than $r$ is the same as if the charges of these layers were in the center of the ball. Consequently, the direction of the field at a distance $r$ from the center of the ball is $E_r = \frac{Q_r}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}$, where $Q_r = qn \frac{4}{3} \pi r^3$ and $E_r = \frac{qn r}{3\varepsilon_0}$.

Using the results obtained, it is not difficult to find the direction of the field at any point on a dielectric ball.

Consider the center of the ball. the directionality at this point is equal to the sum of three vectors (Fig. 20): vector $E$ of the directionality of the external field, vector $E_1$ of the directionality of the field created at this point by a positively charged ball, and vector $E_2$ of the directionality of the field created by a negatively charged ball. Since the center of the ball is located at a distance of $\frac{l}{2}$ st centers is positive and away from

negative charged balls, then

ABC. Angles $\angle ABC$ and $\angle O_1AO_2$ are equal, and the following follows:

F258. A ring made of monk wire breaks if it is charged with charge Q. The diameter of the ring and the diameter of the wire are increased by 3 times. At what charge will this new ring break?

Under the influence of electric repulsion forces, the ring is deformed, and at any place in the ring the resulting elastic force is equal to the force of the electrical interaction of the selected elementary section with the rest of the ring: $F_{эл} = F_{упр}$.

The force of interaction between two exact charges $Q_1$ and $Q_2$ is at a distance of $r$ from each other, according to Coulomb's law it is equal to $\frac{Q_1 Q_2}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0 r^2}$. This is apparently the only combination of $Q_1, Q_2$ and $r$ that has a force dimension. Therefore, the electric force in the ring must be proportional to $\frac{Q^2}{R^2}$ (R is the radius of the ring):

$$F_{эл} = \alpha \frac{Q^2}{R^2},$$

where $\alpha$ is the proportionality coefficient.

At the moment of rupture, the elastic force per unit cross-sectional area of ​​the wiring is equal to the limiting force $\sigma$, which depends only on the materials of the wiring, therefore

$$F_{упр} = \sigma S$$

(S is the cross-sectional area of ​​the wiring).

Thus,

$$\alpha \frac{Q^2}{R^2} = \sigma S. \tag{1}$$

$$\alpha \frac{Q_1^2}{(3R)^2} = \sigma S_1.$$
