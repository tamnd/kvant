“Space” tasks for entrance exams
E. P. Kuznetsov

In this article we will talk about the laws of motion of bodies in a gravitational field and illustrate their tasks offered at the entrance exams to the Moscow Institute of Physics and Technology.

Just as all electrostatics is described, essentially, by Coulomb's law, all the complexity, beauty and bullet-like tannoy of the motion of celestial bodies is contained in Newton's simple and elegant law of universal gravitation. What is this law? Every object in the Universe pulls toward every other object with a force proportional to the masses of the objects and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them:

$$F = \gamma \frac{m_1 m_2}{R^2}.$$

In addition, it must be remembered that a force applied to a body causes an acceleration in the direction of this force, in magnitude inversely proportional to the mass of the body.

The force of mutual attraction between two bodies is described by a formula completely similar to Coulomb’s law. Therefore, the expression for potential energy will have the same form as in electrostatics:

$$W = -\gamma \frac{m_1 m_2}{R}.$$

We put a minus sign in front of the formula. Let's see what this means.

To move two bodies apart a certain distance (for example, to lift an object above the surface of the Earth), it is necessary to overcome the force of their mutual attraction (gravity), that is, to do work. Consequently, the potential energy of the body increases. On the other hand, if the bodies are separated by an infinitely large distance, the force of their interaction will be zero. It is reasonable to assume that the potential energy will also be zero. But only a negative value can increase, tending to zero in the limit.

Now we will study the movement of planets in circular orbits around the Sun, the movement of artificial Earth satellites, and in general the movement under the influence of the forces of mutual attraction of two bodies, the mass $M$ of one of which is significantly greater than the mass $m$ of the other. In this case, the large body can be considered motionless.

The motion of such bodies is described by Kepler's laws. We present the formulas of Kepler's laws without derivation.

I. All bodies in the Solar System move along curves that are finite sections (ellipse, parabola, hyperbola), at one of the foci of which the Sun is located.

II. The radius vector from the Sun to the planet is swept over equal areas in equal time intervals (Fig. 1).

III. The squares of the planets' orbital times are proportional to the cubes of large half-seine orbits.

A special case of an ellipse is a circle. For her the third law

Let's consider solving specific problems.

Problem 1. An artificial satellite, using a meteorological communication system, is launched in the plane of the earth's equator so that we are always in the Zenome of one and the same point on the globe. How many times is the radius of the satellite's orbit greater than the radius of the Earth $R_0 = 6370$ km? Acceleration of free flow at the Earth's surface $g = 9,8 \text{ m/c}^2$.

The acceleration caused by the satellite's attraction to the Earth is centripetal acceleration:
$$\frac{\gamma M}{R^2} = \omega^2 R = \frac{4\pi^2}{T^2} R.$$

In our case, the satellite constantly “hangs” over the same point on the Earth (the so-called Stationary satellite), and therefore, the period of revolution is equal to the period of revolution of the Earth around its axis, that is, one day. Let's transform the left side of our equality:
$$\frac{\gamma M}{R^2} = \frac{\gamma M}{R_0^2} \left( \frac{R_0}{R} \right)^2 = g \left( \frac{R_0}{R} \right)^2.$$

Thus,
$$\frac{4\pi^2}{T^2} R = g \left( \frac{R_0}{R} \right)^2.$$

Multiply both sides by $R_0/R$; Then:
$$\left( \frac{R}{R_0} \right)^3 = \frac{g T^2}{4\pi^2 R_0}.$$

Substituting numerical values, we get
$$\frac{R}{R_0} = \sqrt[3]{\frac{9,8 (24 \cdot 3600)^2}{4\pi^2 6370 \cdot 10^3}} = \sqrt[3]{290} = 6,6.$$

The problem can be solved in another way using Kepler's third law:
$$\left( \frac{R}{R_0} \right)^3 = \left( \frac{T}{T_0} \right)^2.$$

The value $T$ is known to us, and $T_0$ is the period of revolution of the satellite in the immediate vicinity of the Earth’s surface. He, very/no, is equal
$$T_0 = \frac{2\pi R_0}{v_1} = \frac{2\pi R_0}{\sqrt{g R_0}} = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{R_0}{g}},$$

where $v_1$ is the first escape velocity. Then
$$\left( \frac{R}{R_0} \right)^3 = \frac{T^2 g}{4\pi^2 R_0}.$$

Problem 2. The satellite is orbiting around the Earth in a circular orbit of radius $R$. As a result of the short-term effect of the frost, the speed of the satellite decreased so that it began to move in an elliptical orbit touching the surface of the Earth (Fig. 2). How long after emodo will the satellite land? The radius of the Earth is $R_0$. The trenets in the atmosphere are pretenbrets.

The origin changes due to the instantaneous action of the braking system, and the further movement of the satellite will again occur according to the laws of celestial mechanics. After braking, the satellite begins to move along

elliptical orbit, the major half-axis of which is $a = (R + R_0)/2$. According to Kepler's third law

$$\left(\frac{T_3}{T_K}\right)^2 = \left(\frac{a}{R}\right)^3,$$

where $T_3$ is the period of revolution of the satellite in an elliptical orbit, and $T_K$ in a circular one. The period of revolution of a satellite in a circular orbit is equal to

$$T_K = \frac{2\pi R}{v} = \frac{2\pi R}{\sqrt{\gamma M}/R} = 2\pi R \sqrt{\frac{R}{\gamma M}}.$$

Then its period of revolution in an elliptical orbit is equal to

$$T_3 = 2\pi R \sqrt{\frac{R}{\gamma M}} \left(\frac{R + R_0}{2R}\right)^{3/2}.$$

From the moment of braking to landing, the satellite will travel just half of the orbit (see Fig. 2). That's why

$$t = \frac{T_3}{2} = \pi R \sqrt{\frac{R}{\gamma M}} \left(\frac{R + R_0}{2R}\right)^{3/2} = \frac{\pi}{\sqrt{\gamma M}} \left(\frac{R + R_0}{2}\right)^{3/2}.$$

If the distance from the satellite to the Earth's surface is not great, that is, $R \approx R_0$, the formula can be passed:

$$t = \frac{\pi}{R_0 \sqrt{g}} \left(\frac{R_0 + R}{2}\right)^{3/2}.$$

Until now we have neglected friction in the atmosphere. Let us now consider the deceleration of a satellite in the upper layers of the atmosphere. We will assume that the initial orbit of the satellite is circular, and the braking table is not

so large that any particular turn can be considered a circle with a sufficient degree of accuracy.

The total energy of a satellite is equal to the sum of its kinetic and potential energy. The potential energy of the satellite is $-Mm\gamma/R$, and the kinetic energy is $mv^2/2$. But the speed of the satellite in orbit is $v = \sqrt{\gamma M/R}$. Then the kinetic energy of the satellite

$$W_{\text{кин}} = \gamma \frac{mM}{2R},$$

that is, it is determined only by the radius of the orbit and is always half as much (in absolute value) as the potential energy. The total energy of the satellite is negative:

$$W_{\text{пол}} = -\frac{\gamma}{2} \frac{mM}{R}.$$

Let the satellite fly in an orbit of radius $R_1$ before braking and have a speed

$$v_1 = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma M}{R_1}}.$$

After braking in the upper layers of the atmosphere, the satellite moved into an orbit with a radius of $R_2$, less than $R_1$ (it couldn’t fly higher as a result of braking), and its kinetic energy became equal to $W_2 = \gamma mM/2R_2$; therefore, its speed is given by

$$v_2 = \sqrt{\frac{2W_2}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma M}{R_2}}.$$

If we compare expressions (3) and (2), we can see that as a result of braking, the speed of the satellite increased ($R_2 < R_1$!) But this is already a regularity of orbital motion, due to the above-mentioned proportional distribution of total energy between kinetic and potential. The total energy, as follows from formula (1), decreases as the orbital radius decreases (it is negative), so the law of conservation of energy is in no way

Problem 3. A satellite launched into a steep orbit at an altitude of $H = 500$ km above the Earth's surface freezes in the upper layers of the atmosphere. The lateral acceleration of the satellite is $\beta = 3 \cdot 10^{-13}$ rad/s$^2$. What altitude will the satellite be at in a month?

The magnitude of the angular acceleration is low, and therefore each orbit of the satellite can be considered a circle. Then, in each specific orbit, we can neglect friction and apply the laws of celestial mechanics to the satellite. If we denote the radius and period of revolution for the first analogous orbit by $R_1$ and $T_1$, and for the orbit in which the satellite ends up in a month, by $R_2$ and $T_2$, then, according to Kepler’s third law, we can write

$$\frac{T_1^2}{R_1^3} = \frac{T_2^2}{R_2^3} = \frac{(T_1 + \Delta T)^2}{(R_1 + \Delta R)^3} = \frac{T_1^2 + 2T_1\Delta T + (\Delta T)^2}{R_1^3 + 3R_1^2(\Delta R) + 3R_1(\Delta R)^2 + (\Delta R)^3}.$$

It is obvious that $\Delta R \ll R$ and $\Delta T \ll T$ ($\Delta R$ in principle cannot be greater than the orbital height, while the radius of the Earth is 6370 km, and you can now estimate the maximum value $\Delta T$ yourself, using Kepler’s third law).

But then, without any loss of accuracy, we can neglect the terms $(\Delta R)^2, (\Delta R)^3, (\Delta T)^2$:

$$\frac{T_1^2}{R_1^3} = \frac{T_1^2 + 2T_1\Delta T}{R_1^3 + 3R_1^2\Delta R}.$$

From here

$$\frac{R_1^3 + 3R_1^2\Delta R}{R_1^3} = \frac{T_1^2 + 2T_1\Delta T}{T_1^2}.$$

Hence,

$$\frac{\Delta R}{R_1} = \frac{2}{3} \frac{\Delta T}{T_1}.$$

But $\Delta T/T_1 = \Delta \omega/\omega_1$, where $\omega_1$ is the angular velocity of the satellite. By analogy with rectilinear uniformly accelerated motion, we define the angular acceleration by the equality

$$\beta = \frac{\Delta \omega}{t},$$

where $t$ is time (in our case, one month). So we get

$$\Delta R = \frac{2}{3} R_1 \frac{\beta t}{\omega_1} = \frac{2}{3} (R_0 + H)\beta t = \frac{2}{3} (R_0 + H)^2 \frac{\beta t}{v}.$$

Here, as usual,

$$v = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma M}{(R_0 + H)}}.$$

But, since $H \ll R_0$, we can set the speed $v$ equal to the first cosmic one. Substituting numerical values, we get

$$\Delta R = \frac{2}{3} (6370 + 500)^2 \times \frac{3 \cdot 10^{13} \cdot 30 \cdot 24 \cdot 3600}{8} = 3 (\text{km}),$$

$$H' = H - \Delta R = 497 \text{ km}.$$

As we see, the values ​​acquired at school are sufficient for the distances of various problems in applied astronomy and astronautics.

Exercises

2. Halley's Comet is expected to appear in 1986. The last time it appears is in 1910, and its minimum distance from the Sun is then 0.6 of the distance from the Earth to the Sun (0.6 astronomical units - a.e.). How further does it move away from the Sun?

The average distance of the Moon from the Earth is $R = 3,84 \cdot 10^5 \text{ km}$, the angular speed of rotation of the Moon around the Earth is $\omega = 2,56 \cdot 10^{-6} \text{ rad/c}$.
