Fisheye camera
V. Drachev,
A. Mazur

In the 9th issue of our magazine for 1974, we talked about the scientific conference of students of physics and mathematics integrative schools, which takes place in March of this year in Tbilisi. At this conference, V. Drachev presented a report on the design and operation of a device for obtaining photographs with a viewing angle close to 180°. This device was designed by a group of students from integrative school No. 165 in Novosibirsk. This article explains how to make such a camera yourself.

Let's imagine a swimmer who dives under the water without a mask. What picture will open to him if he looks up through the calm surface of a pond?

Provided that the body of water is relatively small, the swimmer will see the following. On the dark ceiling (the surface of the water) there is a bright round window, in which the entire sky is “sandwiched” from horizon to horizon. All objects surrounding the pond stand at the edges of this window, with their “tops” directed towards its center. Moreover, it will be possible to see even part of the coastal day.

All this can be easily explained on the basis of the phenomenon of total internal reflection of light. It is known that when moving from an optically denser to an optically less dense medium, there is a limiting angle of incidence (angle $\alpha_0$ by figure 1), starting from which light will not escape from the first medium to the second. In this case the light is said to experience total internal reflection. For example, for the transition water - air $\alpha_0 = 49^\circ$.

Now let’s imagine that all the rays go in the reverse order, that is, from air to water. Then, if the useful angle from which light will not exit from the first medium to the second is $98^\circ$ (that is, two limiting angles $\alpha_0$), then the viewing angle in the air will be $180^\circ$. The field of view becomes much wider.

This is exactly how fish see, for example (Fig. 2). True, objects placed at the edges of the air hemisphere turn out to be highly distorted and, of course, displaced from their position.

The thing is that with total internal reflection, the surface of the water becomes a good mirror, since the light reflection coefficient increases sharply. For angles of incidence close to zero, the reflection coefficient of water is approximately 2%, and for angles greater than the limit, it is almost 100%. figure shows that the mirror image of the fish is visible from a smaller viewing angle than the fish itself. Of course, the image will be good if the surface of the reservoir is glassy.

To simulate this phenomenon, we made a camera called a “Fisheye”. The camera (Fig. 3) consists of a rigid round box (18 cm in diameter, 4 cm in height) - a body, plexiglass, foil with a hole - a diaphragm, a circle of flat glass *) and a photographic plate.

The refraction of light occurs on the upper surface of the plexiglass, that is, the plexiglass here plays the same role as water in the experiments described above.

The glass, which is a transparent lid covering the diaphragm on top, is a thin plane-parallel plate, so it does not provide any significant additional effects.

The box must be filled with a liquid with a refractive index approximately the same as that of plexiglass (we use a mixture of water and glycerin), otherwise the light coming out of the glass will take a direction parallel to the original one, and the expected image on the photographic plate will not work. (Similarly: a swimmer diving with a mask will not see the above picture.)

When calculating the aperture, the following factor must be taken into account. The image on a photographic plate is obtained using a hole camera, so the diameter of the hole must be small compared to the distance to the photographic plate. But as the diameter of the aperture decreases, the aperture of light becomes more and more noticeable. This results in poor image quality. Theoretical calculation shows that the optimal value of the hole diameter should be of the order of $d = \sqrt{\lambda h}$, where $\lambda$ is the wavelength of light, $h$ is the distance from the hole to the photographic plate.

We obtained better quality signals on a sunny day with an aperture diameter of 0.3 mm; The sensitivity of the photographic plate was 180 units.
