At the end of the university course, N.N. Luzin invited Pavel Samuilovich to “stay at the university” (that is, enter graduate school) under his leadership. This proposal, accepted by P. S. Urson, ultimately determined his choice of mathematical specialization.

The postgraduate period in the life of P. S. Urson, which lasted from 1919 to 1921, fell entirely into the independent first years of the formation of a consistent Soviet mathematical school. There were years of extraordinary change and fascination with new creative possibilities that had opened up, years of preparatory flowering for many young people who for the first time tasted the joy of creative contact with science in those new, unprecedented conditions that the Revolution opened up. In the history of mathematical science there are few periods of such long-lasting enthusiasm as the beginning of those years at Moscow University, when in a very short period of time, literally in a few years, a whole large scientific school arose, largely determining the further development of mathematics in our country and immediately producing a number of new outstanding scientists.

P. S. Urson immediately found himself in the very center of this community of young mathematicians - first of all, of course, due to his unusually bright mathematical talent, but also due to his passion for science, his joy of life, his ebullient temperament and the obligatory properties of his character, open, friendly, completely alien to any pettiness. He was loved in a commercial environment, as people are loved in whom the genuine movement of a great human personality is felt, not only in the sense of abilities for one particular specialization, but also across the entire range of emotional, ethical and social properties of a person.

The mathematical creativity of P. S. Urson developed rapidly and variedly. Already in his student years, he mastered the mathematics of his time, and his postgraduate reports were long remembered among Moscow mathematicians: almost every report, even in specialties far from the main mathematical interests of Pavel Samuilovich, contained one or another new result or, in extreme cases, some significant requested or learned evidence. And how many different errors and impurities Pavel Samuilovich found on this matter in the mathematical literature!

But that's not all. From the postgraduate reports of P. S. Urson, some of his research on mathematical analysis arose (in particular, work on nonlinear interral equations), which later became significant and “classical” in the full sense of the word. However, his name was largely secured by topological research and, first of all, the theory of dimension he created, which was one of the significant achievements of mathematical thought in the first decades of the current century.

The theory of dimensionality was developed by P. S. Urson during the 1921/22 academic year. By this time, he had already completed his graduate studies and in June 1921 was approved as an assistant professor at Moscow University. That same summer, D. F. Egorov set a task for P. S.
