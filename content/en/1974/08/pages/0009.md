If these sets are sufficiently small in diameter (namely, if their diameters are smaller than the edge of the cube), then there is at least one point belonging to at least n+1 sets from among the given set $\Phi_1, \Phi_2, \ldots, \Phi_s$.

Thus, we arrive at the following new definition of dimension, which is formulated for bounded closed sets lying in eclidean spaces of any number of dimensions (although this definition can easily be generalized to any metric and even to even more generalized spaces).

Let's say that the dimension dim X of a space X does not exceed n if this space admits an arbitrarily small covering of closed sets with multiplicity no more than n+1.

The depth of the equivalence theorem proved by Uryson lies in the fact that it establishes the equivalence of two geometric properties that are not at all similar to each other, two definitions that are completely different in their formulations, which, as it turns out, are definitions of the same concept, namely the concept of the dimension of a geometric figure, that this concept can be characterized in this way with completeness of sides. It is also the comparisons of seemingly completely different approaches to such phenomena that turn out to be the most fruitful in science.

As a consequence of his “equivalence theorem,” Urysohn proved that an n-dimensional Eclidean space in the elementary geometric sense has multiplicity n in the sense of the general inductive definition of dimension given above. But the significance of this theorem was not limited to this; it served as a turning point in the development of the theory of multiplicity and a significant part of topology in general.
