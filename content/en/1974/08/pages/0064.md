Is there any other proof?
V. K. Smyshlyaev, A. P. Savin

If you ask a person who graduated from high school a long time ago whether he remembers the Pythagorean theorem, then in the overwhelming majority of people the answer will be: “But of course! In a right-angled triangle, the sum of the squares of the legs is equal to the square of the hypotenuse.” Other theorems are forgotten, but this one is not. She gives birth to us with her beauty and grace and remains in our memory for a long time.

But not everyone can remember the proof of this theorem, and if they do, it will not be the proof from Kiselev’s textbook, which belonged to Euclid, but some more elegant one, for example, the Indian one, consisting of figure (Fig. 1) and the signature “look”.

The proof is also very elegant, based on the theorem about a secant and tangent drawn to a circle from one point (Fig. 2). By the mentioned theorem $AB^2 = AC \cdot AD$ or $a^2 = (c + b)(c - b)$, that is $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$.

There are many proofs of the Pythagorean theorem. Back in 1876, Yu. F. Vipper’s book “Forty-five proofs of the Pythagorean theorem” was published in Moscow. Of course, it is already a bibliographic rarity, but W. Litzman’s book “The Pythagorean Theorem” (Moscow, Fizmatgiz, 1960) is still not too difficult to find. It contains a fairly complete collection of various proofs of this theorem. And, of course, we recommend looking at the article by V.N. Berezin “Pythagorean Theorem”, «Kvant», 1972, No. 3, and an unambiguous note in «Kvant» No. 7 for 1974.

Is the Pythagorean theorem unique in terms of the abundance of its proofs? It turns out not. A lot of fundamental theorems are discovered in dozens of ways.

What causes this? Why do mathematicians reprove already known facts? In our opinion, three main principles can be distinguished.
