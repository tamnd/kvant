It is known that the basic laws of motion - Newton's laws - are valid only in inertial reporting systems. Therefore, it is natural to expect that upon transition to non-inertial systems, the equations of motion should change. However, if we limit ourselves only to classical mechanics, that is, do not consider movements with speeds comparable to the speed of light, then it turns out that the basic equation of motion can be written in exactly the same form as Newton’s second law, if we introduce a new concept - the concept of inertial forces.

When the driver brakes the bus, he sees that the passenger on roller skates (that is, in the absence of tremors) receives acceleration relative to the bus, directed in the direction in which the bus was moving before braking. Or, which is the same thing, the acceleration of the passenger is opposite in direction to the acceleration that the bus receives.

This acceleration is not directly related to the action of some body on the passenger, but is observed in an accelerated coordinate system. Trying to describe the movement from the point of view of the usual laws of mechanics, one will have to say: if there is acceleration, then it must be attributed to the action of some force. Such forces, introduced in non-inertial reporting systems in order to describe the accelerated motion of free bodies, are called inertial forces.

Inertial forces differ from ordinary forces, for example, from electrical or gravitational forces, primarily in that they are caused not by the interaction of bodies, but by the accelerated movement of the reporting system itself. Inertial forces disappear upon transition to an inertial reporting system (observe on the sidewalk), while ordinary forces do not change upon transition to any other reporting system. Ordinary forces are said to be invariant with respect to a transition in another coordinate system, if only the speed of the new frame of reference is relative to the original residual multimensha of the speed of light *). Noting such a fundamental property of ordinary forces, sometimes inertial forces are called pseudo-forces (“as if” forces) or fictitious forces.

Another important difference between inertial forces is that from the non-inertial point of view, these forces are observed to act at all points of the system, wherever the body under study is located, and in the immediate vicinity of the body it is impossible to indicate the source of these forces.
