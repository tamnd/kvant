At the same time, Pavel Samulovich taught a course at Moscow University entitled “Topology of Continuums,” in which all these results were presented, often immediately after their proof. This course, the first topological course taught not only at Moscow University, but in our country in general, was undoubtedly one of the most remarkable mathematical courses ever taught within the walls of Moscow University, precisely because of its entirely creative nature.

The summer of 1922, spent by P. S. Urson with me again in the vicinity of Bolshev (in the so-called Stary Gorki), was the period of our joint research on the theory of topological spaces.

During this summer, results were obtained that were included in our general “Metoire on Compact Spaces,” the third edition of which was recently (in 1972) published as a separate monograph.

In the same summer and part of the next winter, Pavel Samulovich did his work on measure theory. In the winter of 1922/23, Pavel Samulovich continued to study mainly the theory of topological spaces. At this time, our joint theorem on the necessary and sufficiently conditional conditions for a topological space to be geometric to a metric one (the so-called “general metrization theorem”) was proved, and P. S. Urson also proved the remarkable theorem that all metric spaces with countable dense sets are also homomorphic to sets lying in a Silbert space. The proof of the emergence of topologically including in a global space any normal space with a countable base*), that is, the proof that the normal is necessary and sufficiently conditional for the metrizability of spaces with a countable base, was obtained by Pavel Samulovich only a little later, in 1924. During the winter of 1922/23, P. S. Urson finally edited the topic on the theory of dimension, and I edited our general topic on the theory of compact spaces. That same summer, Pavel Samulovich studied the theory of relativity with great enthusiasm, so that the next year (that is, in the winter of 1923/24) he would give a course of lectures on this subject at the university. The summer of 1923 was spent on a trip to Germany, where Pavel Samulovich gave reports at the Göttingen Mathematical Society with great success and immediately attracted the attention of Hilbert to his topological work**). As a separate activity, there was a walking trip around Norway. Returning to Moscow in the fall of 1923, Pavel Samulovich began to engage in new departments of topology with his characteristic passion: he studied the topics of Poincaré and Brouwer.
