After this, the determination of the dimension of metric spaces (or at least sets lying in included spaces of any number of dimensions) is carried out by induction as follows.

Let the set be assigned dimension -1.

Let us assume that we have already defined sets of dimension not exceeding n - 1. Let us add that the set $X$ (metric space) has dimension at most n if between any two lectures in $X$ closed sets without common lobes there is a partition of dimension at most n—1. After this, we naturally say that the dimension of the set $X$ is equal to n if $X$ is included in the class of sets with dimension at most n, and is not included in the class of sets with dimension at most n-1.

If we are only interested in the so-called “separable” metric spaces (that is, those having a countable dense subset of points)—and only they were interested in them at the beginning of the construction of the theory of dimension—then the definition of dimension equivalent to that given above can be formulated in the following way.

The dimension of the empty set is assumed to be equal to -1.

Assuming that sets of dimension no more than n-1 have already been defined, we say that the dimension of the set $X$ does not exceed n if, whatever the point $x \in X$ is and not with

the closed set $F$ holding it between $x$ and $F$ there is a partition of dimension at most n—1.

After which the statement is in turn equivalent to this.

Whatever the point $x \in X$ and its neighborhood $O(x)$, there are smaller neighborhoods $O_1(x)$ between the points, implying that the boundary of the neighborhood $O_1(x)$ has dimension at most n−1.

Now the reader can easily prove that every finite set (on a line, on a plane) and in general every metric space consisting of a finite number of points has dimension 0; that the straight line and the circle, as well as all the “elementary curves” known to the reader (parabola, ellipse, sinusoid and others) have dimension 1. By inductive definition, it follows that the plane and the sphere have dimension no more than 2. After this, again using induction, we prove that three-dimensional space has dimension no more than 3.
