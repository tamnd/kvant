The second reason is the desire to establish a connection between this theorem and other known facts, to determine its position in the complex structure called mathematics. An example of this is the connection we discovered between the tangent and secant theorem with the Pythagorean theorem.

And the third reason is the discovery of new facts, for which this theorem is a very important consequence.

We will look at one such example at the end of the article. Now let’s look at some proofs of other, not so much signs of the theorem.

Let $M$ be a point lying on a circle circumscribed around the geometric triangle $ABC$. Then the length of the largest of the segments $MA, MB, MC$ is equal to the sum of the lengths of the other two.

Let us present a rather elegant geometric solution. Namely, let $MA$ be the largest of the indicated segments (Fig. 3). Let us plot the segment $MD = MB$ from the point $M$. Next, we note that the angle $AMB$ is equal to $60^\circ$, as based on the same arc as the angle $ACB$. It follows that the triangle $BMD$ is equilateral, after which it is not at all difficult to establish that the triangles $ABD$ and $BMC$ are equal, and therefore $MA = AD + DM = MC + MB$.

Here's the trigonometric proof. Let us denote the length of the side $ABC$ of the triangle by $a$, and the angle $BAM$ by $\alpha$. Let us express the indicated segments in terms of $a$ and $\alpha$ using the sine theorem:

$$MB = \frac{a \sin \alpha}{\sin 60^\circ}, \quad MC = \frac{a \sin(60^\circ - \alpha)}{\sin 60^\circ},$$

$$MA = \frac{a \sin(60^\circ + \alpha)}{\sin 60^\circ}.$$

Let's find the sum of $MB$ and $MC$:

$$MB + MC = \frac{a}{\sin 60^\circ} \times (\sin \alpha + \sin(60^\circ - \alpha)) = \frac{a \cos(30^\circ - \alpha)}{\sin 60^\circ} = \frac{a \sin(\alpha + 60^\circ)}{\sin 60^\circ}.$$

Comparing the obtained result with the value $MA$, we obtain the statement of the theorem.

Note that trigonometry is a very powerful tool for solving geometric problems, and its use transfers the problem from geometric to algebraic.
