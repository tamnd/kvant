Listen on the bus
Let's start with an ordinary incident. The bus braked sharply at a traffic light, passengers were pulled forward. Someone stepped on the person standing forward and, of course, wriggled around. From the point of view of the rules of conduct, everything is in order. But for a physicist, the end of the story must seem incongruous. The passenger, of course, is not to blame: it’s probably the arrogant driver who is to blame - he should be interested in the science of behavior, but let’s just ask ourselves, what, strictly speaking, is the driver’s fault? How was the driver able to push the passengers? After all, he sits far away and cannot even reach them. We can say that the force (of course, not from the driver, but from the motor or, perhaps, from the brakes) was transmitted through the floor. This answer also cannot be considered satisfactory: after all, the horas remain standing in place - the passenger seems to be pulled, rather, by the head...

If he is not deliberately trying to confuse the matter, then everything is explained very simply. The driver braked the bus; the passengers' legs (due to the high static friction of the soles on the floor) also slowed down, and the torso and head continued to move at the same speed, obeying the first law. Newton's law of inertia. If there were no friction, for example, all the passengers stood on perfect rollers (which you can’t do for the sake of science!), then they would not immediately feel any impact, and one after another they would hit the wall of the driver’s cabin that suddenly stopped in front of them.

Such a description will be given by any reasonable observer who is in an inertial coordinate system, for example, this scene is observed while standing on the sidewalk.

But it may happen that another person wants to analyze the same story, say, the driver of the bus, who was sitting tightly in his cross. He, of course, knew that the autobus had slowed down, that is, it was accelerating relative to the ground, and therefore its reporting system would cease to be inertial. It is known that the basic laws of motion - Newton's laws - are valid only in inertial reporting systems. Therefore, it is natural to expect that upon transition to non-inertial systems, the equations of motion should change. However, if we limit ourselves only to classical mechanics, that is, do not consider movements with speeds comparable to the speed of light, then it turns out that the basic equation of motion can be written in exactly the same form as Newton’s second law, if we introduce a new concept - the concept of inertial forces.

When the driver brakes the bus, he sees that the passenger on roller skates (that is, in the absence of tremors) receives acceleration relative to the bus, directed in the direction in which the bus was moving before braking. Or, which is the same thing, the acceleration of the passenger is opposite in direction to the acceleration that the bus receives.

This acceleration is not directly related to the action of some body on the passenger, but is observed in an accelerated coordinate system. Trying to describe the movement from the point of view of the usual laws of mechanics, one will have to say: if there is acceleration, then it must be attributed to the action of some force. Such forces, introduced in non-inertial reporting systems in order to describe the accelerated motion of free bodies, are called inertial forces.

Inertial forces differ from ordinary forces, for example, from electrical or gravitational forces, primarily in that they are caused not by the interaction of bodies, but by the accelerated movement of the reporting system itself. Inertial forces disappear upon transition to an inertial reporting system (observe on the sidewalk), while ordinary forces do not change upon transition to any other reporting system. Ordinary forces are said to be invariant with respect to a transition in another coordinate system, if only the speed of the new frame of reference is relative to the original residual multimensha of the speed of light *). Noting such a fundamental property of ordinary forces, sometimes inertial forces are called pseudo-forces (“as if” forces) or fictitious forces.

Another important difference between inertial forces is that from the non-inertial point of view, these forces are observed to act at all points of the system, wherever the body under study is located, and in the immediate vicinity of the body it is impossible to indicate the source of these forces. If one passenger stood on roller skates, only another, then they would drive away from each other in different directions, freaking out about Newton's third law.

After protecting the bus, all the passengers moved forward, and no one went back. In this sense, the action of inertial forces resembles the action of electric or magnetic forces that are transmitted through an electromagnetic field and for which it is also not so easy to understand what counteraction is. Therefore, in the problem of inertial forces one can use a similar concept - the field of inertial forces.

Forward movement of the reporting system

The acceleration of this body in the system $K'$ is denoted by $a'$. Let us describe the movements of the selected body from the point of view of two observers: one is associated with the system $K'$ (we will call it a moving observer), the other is in the system $K$ (we will call it a stationary observer).

For a stationary observer (in an inertial reporting system), the body participates in two movements (Fig. 1): with acceleration $a'$ relative to system $K'$ and with acceleration $a_0$ relative to system $K$, therefore its total acceleration

Centrifugal force of inertia

Let us now consider a reporting system that rotates with a constant angular velocity $\omega$ around a fixed axis. Such a system could be, for example, a rotating disk. We have already said that in the case of developing a reporting system, it is necessary to take into account two forces of inertia - centrifugal and rooted. Let us limit ourselves to considering only the centrifugal force of inertia. This force depends on the coordinate of the body and does not depend on whether it is at rest or moving relative to the grown reporting system, therefore, for simplicity, we will assume that the body is motionless.

Let there be a body on the main rotating disk, which is held on the disk by a spring at a distance $r$ from the rotation axis. Let us describe his behavior from the point of view of the stationary and mobile observable.

A stationary observer (associated with an inertial reporting system) will say that since a body moves in a circle, it has an acceleration called centripetal and equal in magnitude

$$a_\mu = \omega^2 r.$$

This acceleration of the body is imparted by the elastic force of the stretched spring $F_y$, since no other bodies in the horizontal plane act on the body (Fig. 5). According to Newton's second law

$$m a_\mu = F_y.$$

From the point of view of the moving observable (associated with the rotating inertial system of the report), the body will end, therefore, the sum of all forces acting on the body is equal to zero. Therefore, this observable must say that there is a force that balances the elastic force (Fig. 6). This is the centrifugal inertial force $F_{и.ц}$, equal to

$$F_{и.ц} = -F_y = -m a_\mu.$$

The minus sign indicates that this force is directed opposite to the centripetal acceleration, that is, along the radius from the axis of rotation, and is equal in magnitude to $F_{и.ц} = m\omega^2 r$.
