Fermi was born in Rome on September 29, 1901, into the family of an employee. If we can talk about driving a vocation, then, undoubtedly, Fermi was born a physicist. Although nothing in his family encouraged him to pursue science, he showed an exceptional interest in mathematics and physics from childhood. The intellectual development of the boy, who later became a brilliant scientist, is of great interest, and I would like to dwell on this in more detail.

It is not known exactly when Fermi first became interested in science, but we have some information thanks to the testimony of Enrico Persico, professor of physics at the University of Rome and close friend of Fermi since they were 14 years old; his wife, Laura Fermi, and a number of his collaborators and friends, especially Franco Rasetti and Emillo Serre, with whom Fermi shared his contributions. Serres, for example, recounts the following episode: when Fermi was only ten years old, he was able to understand why a circle describes the equation $x^2 + y^2 = R^2$, although this required intense intellectual effort on his part.

Later, the thirteen-year-old Fermi was greatly helped in finding the right path in the scientific labyrinth by the engineer Amideus, a kind old man, a friend of the Fermi family, who can rightfully be proud that, having discovered Fermi’s exceptional abilities, he had a greater, and perhaps even decisive, influence on him. Engineer Amidei was a very neat person. When, after Fermi’s death, Serge asked Amideus to talk about Enrico’s first steps in science, he was able to provide (41 years later!) extremely accurate and valuable information for the history of science, allowing us to understand some elements in the formation of Fermi’s Tian personality. Below is a full letter from engineer Amidei to Professor Serra, telling about the period of Fermi’s life from the autumn of 1914 to the autumn of 1918.

They usually return home together. Almost always we are accompanied by Enrico Fermi, the son of my colleague. The boy always met his father after work. Having learned that I was serious about mathematics and physics, Enrico began asking me questions. At that time he was 13 years old and I was 37.

I remember his first question well:
— Is it true that there is a branch of geometry in which important geometric properties are revealed without using the idea of ​​mere?

I replied that this is absolutely fair and that this section is called design geometry.

“But how are these properties used in practice by engineers?” he asked.

This question seemed completely reasonable to me. Having told the boy about some of the properties, finding a successful application, I promised to bring him the next day - which I did - a book on design geometry.

A few days later, Enrico told me that he had already studied the first three lessons, and promised to return the book as soon as he had read it. About two months later the book was returned. To my question whether he encountered any difficulties, the boy replied: “None,” and added that he proved all the theorems and easily solved all the problems (there were more than 200 of them in the book).

I was amazed: after all, I knew that among these tasks there were some that I was forced to refuse to solve, because it would take a lot of time to complete the list. But I was convinced that Enrico coped with these tasks. It was absolutely amazing that in the free hours left from preparing school assignments, the boy perfectly studied design geometry and easily solved complex problems. I became convinced that Enrico was exceptionally gifted, at least in the field of geometry. When I told his father about this, he replied that at school Enrico was considered a good student, but nothing more.

Later I learned that Enrico studied mathematics and physics from random books that he bought in bookstores in the Campo de' Fiori market. He hoped, in particular, to find in these books a theory requiring the movement of wolves and gyroscopes. He never found an explanation. But, returning to this problem of words and words, the boy independently came closer to explaining the nature of the task movement of the wolf. Nevertheless, I told him that precise scientific understanding can be approached only by mastering theoretical mechanics. But to study it you will need knowledge of trigonometry, algebra, geometry and differential calculus... Enrico agreed with me, and I began to get him books that could give him new ideas and read the mathematical basis...

Enrico found vector analysis very interesting, useful and uncomplicated. From September 1917 to July 1918, he studied some aspects of engineering from books that I got for him.

The École Normale Supérieure in Pishcheba was created in 1813 by Napoleon, modeled after the École Normale Supérieure in Paris; it was the only free higher education institution in Italy. Although officially the school was intended to graduate from both the humanities and the natural sciences, careers and researchers were chosen and became famous, which raised the prestige of the school. In particular, almost all famous Italian mathematicians from Bianchi and Castelnuovo to Volterra and Levi-Civita were graduates.

In a competitive school entrance exam, Fermi was required to present his knowledge on the topic “The Nature and Causes of Sounds.” His “essay” gives an idea of ​​the level of knowledge in classical physics achieved by Fermi at the age of 17. Suffice it to say that not all graduates of physical universities (and not just high school) would be able to write such an essay in which the Fourier method is used to solve the differential equation of a vibrating rod. Fermi himself said that the examiner was surprised by the essay and said that he had never seen anything like it in his practice.

I would like to comment a little on the letter from engineer Amidei. It seems to me that it should be of interest not only to physicists and historians of science, but also to wider circles of readers, especially schoolchildren who are beginning to become interested in science, as well as teachers. Perhaps, thanks to the engineer Amidei, the gifted boy became a genius. Of course, Fermi was a trained physicist, but who can say what his fate would have been if the engineer Amideus had treated him differently, if he had answered the boy’s questions, for example, like this: “This is still too difficult for you. Here is what Enrico Persico wrote: “...Fermi’s exceptional abilities in the exact sciences appeared very early; when I met him (he was 14 years old), I was surprised to discover that my friend was not only a “doctor” in science, as they say in school jargon, but also a friend whose form of mind was completely different from that typical of all the “smart” boys and brilliant students with whom I knew... In the field of mathematics and physics, he showed knowledge in a much larger number of sections than was taught at school, and the knowledge was not school, and he operated with it in the north effortlessly.
