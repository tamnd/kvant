Enrico Fermi - physicist

Take any monograph on physics published in the last 20-30 years, and you will almost certainly find Enrico Fermi in it - the theory he developed, the research method he proposed, or at least a physical term. Fermi made enormous contributions to theoretical physics, atomic physics, nuclear physics and elementary particles.

More than 15 years ago, a Russian translation of Laura Fermi’s book “Atoms at Our Home,” a biography of this scientist written by his wife, was published. It talks about Fermi’s life path, reveals the image of Fermi as a man, but, naturally, almost does not cover his scientific activities. And finally, almost simultaneously, two books about Fermi, the scientist, appeared. However, of them *) belongs to the pen of Enrico Serra, a student, student and friend of Fermi, the second**) was compiled by another student of Fermi, the Soviet academician B. M. Let's turn the pages of these books.

...1915. Market day in Rome. A short, stocky fourteen-year-old boy makes his way through the counters of second-hand bookstores, looking for books on physics. Italian physics, which gave the world Hallel and Volta, had long since lost its former glory, the teaching of this science in schools and universities was carried out at a rather low level, and Enrico Fermi, who was in love with physics, had only one thing left to do - to draw knowledge from books.

...Having defended Dillom in Food, Fermi returned to Rome and immediately came under the tutelage of Corbino, who was the director of the physics laboratory at the University of Rome. Noticing Fermi's outstanding talent, Corbino helped him get (at the age of 26!) a professorship at the University of Rome - the first department of theoretical physics specifically established for this servant, and most importantly, to help around the young professor a group of equally young enthusiasts: Persico, Rasetti, Serre, Amaldi, Pontecorvo, Russia. This is how the Fermi school arose. Subsequently, it was precisely the names of those who grouped around Fermi during his life in the USA (Anderson, Gellman, Holberger, Feld, Orir, Rosenfeld, Chu, Young) that became known to every physicist.

...Like a magnet, Fermi always attracted talented people to himself. How did he achieve this? First of all, of course, unlimited knowledge of physics and a deep understanding of it. Behind the complex mathematical constructions, behind the clutter of formulas, Fermi was able to smooth out the essence of physical processes and phenomena. Here is what Hans Bethe, one of the outstanding physicists of our time, says:

“Fermi’s method of working on theoretical problems is of the greatest order of magnitude in its simplicity. He could penetrate into the existing essence of a task, no matter how difficult it seemed. He stripped away the veil of mathematical conditions and unnecessary formalism. With this approach, he could often solve a very complex physical problem in no more than half an hour. Of course, this did not provide a mathematically complete solution, but when you parted with Fermi after one of these discussions, it was already clear how a mathematical solution could be obtained.”

At the same time, Fermi himself was a good mathematician, capable, if necessary, of making any calculations. Exactly - if you really need it. But in his daily scientific activities, he experimented with approximate, estimated calculations and did not part with a slide rule. When electronic sending machines appeared, he immediately appreciated their power and was the first among physicists to actively use them.

The range of Fermi's scientific interests was colossal. Bruno Maksimovich Pontecorvo recalled that at the University of Rome Fermi lectured on quantum mechanics, atomic physics, mathematical physics, thermodynamics, but his favorite course was...geophysics. How did he manage to understand—and not amateurishly, but deeply—the table of various areas of physics? In addition to his outstanding talent, Fermi has two more important qualities:

Tea hard work and systematicity. Both manifestations already occur in youth. During the summer student holidays of 1919, he filled with pencil a notebook with a leather cover: on its 102 pages there was a summary of all physics from the analytical dynamics of Hamilton and Jacobi to Lorentz's electronic theory of matter, Planck's theory of quantum and the fundamentals of radioactivity of Rutherford. He wrote his last notes, table of contents and bibliographic lists on September 29, his 18th birthday. It is interesting that he took this notebook with him to America, where he kept it along with other scientific notes until the end of his life.

Without relying on his phenomenal memory, he compiled an extensive, well-systematized card file (“my artificial memory,” Fermin joked), from which, having first looked into the key pad, he could easily extract any necessary information: equations, parameters and CONSTANTS, bibliographic references, critical summaries of other people’s scientific works and reports.

A few words about Fermin’s performance, which later became an unaccepted standard for every physicist. In March 1934, he led research into the irradiation of atoms of various elements with recently discovered neutrons. Two months before the summer holidays, almost all elements were tested. Then Fermin went to give a series of lectures in South America, and returned and resumed work, testing the effect of various materials on the behavior of neutrons. By the end of 1934, the work was practically completed (if it had not been so integrative, it would have taken more than one year). Fermin was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1938 for his neutron research.

In December 1938, Fermin, with his wife and two children, went to Stockholm to receive the Nobel Prize, and from there went to America, deciding not to return to fascist Italy.

...Back in 1934, while studying the interaction of neutrons with uranium, Fermin discovered previously unknown radioactive substances and, albeit in a cautious manner, concluded that these were transran elements. The conclusion turned out to be erroneous, and Fermin could not forgive himself for this mistake until the end of his life. In fact, the radioactive substances he discovered, as the German scientists Hahn, Strassmann and Meitner showed four years later, were fission products of uranium nuclei. As soon as this was proven, in the minds of many scientists, including Fermin, the idea of ​​a nuclear reaction was born, as a result of which a huge amount of energy would be released.

...When war broke out in Europe, and especially after the United States entered the war in December 1941, hundreds of scientists working in the United States, mostly immigrants from various European countries occupied by Hitler's Germany, concentrated their services on the creation of the atomic bomb. And here Fermin turned out to be indispensable. Further, such outstanding scientists as Bohr, Oplenheimer, Kompon, Weiskoff, Feynman, Bethe, he was considered an “oracle”; they were turned to in the most difficult cases.

...After the end of the war, Fermin completely moved away from atomic research and became interested in elementary particle physics. As before, he devoted a lot of time to pedagogical activities: he lectured at the University of Chicago, led a theoretical seminar, studied with a group of graduate students, from whom many actively working scientists emerged, and wrote textbooks. In the summer of 1954, he became seriously ill, doctors made a terrible diagnosis - stomach cancer with metastases, and Fermin realized that he had little time to live. But even in this difficult situation, he remained calm and poor in spirit, and even in a large bed he continued to write a course on quantum mechanics.

Fermin died on November 29, 1954, after exactly two months of separation...

This is how Enrico Fermin is presented to us with a page from these two books. Of course, in a short story it is possible to give a complete picture of Fermin - a person and a scientist. To do this you need to read about books.

The appendices to Segre’s book are read with great interest: Fermin’s letters to her friend and mother-in-law Enrico Persicco, covering the period from 1917 to 1926; the speech delivered by Fermin when he was awarded the Nobel Prize and containing a popular presentation of work on artificial radioactivity resulting from neutron bombardment; a recording of Fermin's last speech, in which ZA tells a lively and non-presidential story, with great help, about the history of nuclear research up to the creation of the first nuclear reactor, and, finally, a more rigorous report on the same topic. All these materials, as well as most of the main text of both books, are quite accessible to high school students who are interested in physics. After reading these books, they will become acquainted with an outstanding scientist who is at the forefront of the emerging physics of the 20th century, as well as with one of the remarkable and dramatic pages in the history of physics.
