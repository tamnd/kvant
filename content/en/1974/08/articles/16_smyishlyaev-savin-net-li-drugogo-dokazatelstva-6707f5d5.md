Is there any other proof?
V. K. Smyshlyaev, A. P. Savin

If you ask a person who graduated from high school a long time ago whether he remembers the Pythagorean theorem, then in the overwhelming majority of people the answer will be: “But of course! In a right-angled triangle, the sum of the squares of the legs is equal to the square of the hypotenuse.” Other theorems are forgotten, but this one is not. She gives birth to us with her beauty and grace and remains in our memory for a long time.

But not everyone can remember the proof of this theorem, and if they do, it will not be the proof from Kiselev’s textbook, which belonged to Euclid, but some more elegant one, for example, the Indian one, consisting of figure (Fig. 1) and the signature “look”.

The proof is also very elegant, based on the theorem about a secant and tangent drawn to a circle from one point (Fig. 2). By the mentioned theorem $AB^2 = AC \cdot AD$ or $a^2 = (c + b)(c - b)$, that is $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$.

There are many proofs of the Pythagorean theorem. Back in 1876, Yu. F. Vipper’s book “Forty-five proofs of the Pythagorean theorem” was published in Moscow. Of course, it is already a bibliographic rarity, but W. Litzman’s book “The Pythagorean Theorem” (Moscow, Fizmatgiz, 1960) is still not too difficult to find. It contains a fairly complete collection of various proofs of this theorem. And, of course, we recommend looking at the article by V.N. Berezin “Pythagorean Theorem”, «Kvant», 1972, No. 3, and an unambiguous note in «Kvant» No. 7 for 1974.

Is the Pythagorean theorem unique in terms of the abundance of its proofs? It turns out not. A lot of fundamental theorems are discovered in dozens of ways.

What causes this? Why do mathematicians reprove already known facts? In our opinion, three main principles can be distinguished.

The first is pedagogical. In general, the first proofs of theorems are grammatical and clumsy, but then, when it became known that this fact is true, new, shorter and more elegant proofs appear that are easier to digest and more visual.

The second reason is the desire to establish a connection between this theorem and other known facts, to determine its position in the complex structure called mathematics. An example of this is the connection we discovered between the tangent and secant theorem with the Pythagorean theorem.

And the third reason is the discovery of new facts, for which this theorem is a very important consequence.

We will look at one such example at the end of the article. Now let’s look at some proofs of other, not so much signs of the theorem.

Let $M$ be a point lying on a circle circumscribed around the geometric triangle $ABC$. Then the length of the largest of the segments $MA, MB, MC$ is equal to the sum of the lengths of the other two.

Let us present a rather elegant geometric solution. Namely, let $MA$ be the largest of the indicated segments (Fig. 3). Let us plot the segment $MD = MB$ from the point $M$. Next, we note that the angle $AMB$ is equal to $60^\circ$, as based on the same arc as the angle $ACB$. It follows that the triangle $BMD$ is equilateral, after which it is not at all difficult to establish that the triangles $ABD$ and $BMC$ are equal, and therefore $MA = AD + DM = MC + MB$.

Here's the trigonometric proof. Let us denote the length of the side $ABC$ of the triangle by $a$, and the angle $BAM$ by $\alpha$. Let us express the indicated segments in terms of $a$ and $\alpha$ using the sine theorem:

$$MB = \frac{a \sin \alpha}{\sin 60^\circ}, \quad MC = \frac{a \sin(60^\circ - \alpha)}{\sin 60^\circ},$$

$$MA = \frac{a \sin(60^\circ + \alpha)}{\sin 60^\circ}.$$

Let's find the sum of $MB$ and $MC$:

$$MB + MC = \frac{a}{\sin 60^\circ} \times (\sin \alpha + \sin(60^\circ - \alpha)) = \frac{a \cos(30^\circ - \alpha)}{\sin 60^\circ} = \frac{a \sin(\alpha + 60^\circ)}{\sin 60^\circ}.$$

Comparing the obtained result with the value $MA$, we obtain the statement of the theorem.

Note that trigonometry is a very powerful tool for solving geometric problems, and its use transfers the problem from geometric to algebraic.

And one more note. The apparatus of trigonometry is based on a number of geometric theorems, therefore it is illegal to prove such theorems using trigonometry. For example, the “proof” of the Pythian theorem

mountain: $a = c \sin \alpha, b = c \cos \alpha$, from where $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$ ($\sin^2 \alpha + \cos^2 \alpha) = c^2$, cannot be recognized as a proof, since the identity $\sin^2 \alpha + \cos^2 \alpha = 1$ itself proves using the Pythagorean theorem.

Let us give one more proof of our theorem, based on Ptolemy’s theorem: the product of the diagonals of an inscribed saddle pad is equal to the sum of the product of the opposite sides of the inscribed saddle pad.

Let us apply this theorem to the quadrilateral $ABMC$. Then we have: $MA \times BC = MB \cdot AC + MC \cdot AB$, and, since $AB = BC = AC$, then $MA = MB + MC$.

In conclusion, we repeat: to prove a theorem, think about whether there is another proof. This approach will help systematize your mathematical knowledge, will help you look for different approaches to solving a problem, and find the most natural and productive among them. And, believe me, you will experience great satisfaction when you receive a beautiful and original proof. This is where mathematics comes into close contact with art.
