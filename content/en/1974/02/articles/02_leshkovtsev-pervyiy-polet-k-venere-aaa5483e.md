First flight
to Venus

Venus is the closest planet in the solar system to Earth. Until quite recently, our ideas about the structure and properties of Venus were modest and approximate. The dense atmosphere of the planet did not allow any details of its surface to be seen, and scientists
they wondered whether the surface of Venus was deserted or covered with an ocean. There was no accurate data about the period of Venus’s own rotation, the chemical composition of its atmosphere, the temperature and pressure of gases at various altitudes, etc.

The use of automatic mechanical stations allows us to study the planets of the solar system in detail and obtain a huge amount of accurate information about their characteristics. However, the creation of such stations is associated with enormous technical difficulties.

Thirteen years ago, on February 12, 1961, the Soviet Union successfully launched a controlled automatic station from an artificial satellite onto an international route for the first time. The Venera-1 station, having covered 270 million kilometers, passed close to the planet.

The total weight of the station was 643.5 kg. At the station there is scientific equipment designed to study cosmic rays, measure magnetic fields, record micrometeors and loaded particles filling outer space.

The launch of Venera 1 opened a new chapter in space exploration. In the following year, the USSR and the USA more than once sent automatic stations to the nearest planets.

Postage stamps were issued in the Soviet Union and a number of other countries.

dedicated to the launch of Venera 1. We present here stamps from the USSR, Hungary and Cuba dedicated to this event. The Soviet 10-kopeck stamp reproduces the general view of the station and the diagram of its convergence with Venus. Two Hungarian stamps show the moment the automatic station separates from the body of the launch vehicle.

V. A. Leshkovtsev
