For some reason, everyone thinks that gravity cannot be weakened. It turns out that this is very easy to do. Imagine that two identical massive balls are mounted on a light, long rigid rod. When the balls are brought together and are at a distance of $R$ from the center of the Earth, they are attached to the Earth with a force of $F$. Let's expand the balls, keeping the distance from them to the center of the Earth the same; each ball is now subject to a force equal to $\frac{F}{2}$ (Fig. 1). But these forces are directed towards the center of the Earth, that is, at a certain angle $\alpha$ to each other! This means the resulting force is $F_p = F \cos \frac{\alpha}{2}$. The dumbbell bends weaker than a material point of the same mass.

Of course, it does not produce any weakening of gravitational forces. The point is that the equivalent of the gravitational forces applied to the particles that make up the body is not equal to the arithmetic sum of these forces. A body of mass $m$ in a gravitational field can be considered a material point of the same mass only if it is homogeneous and spherical. Then the force acting on a material point placed at the center of such a ball is equal to the force acting on the body. Let the parts of the ship move in orbit around the Earth expand and shift so that the angle α is kept constant (Fig. 2).
(In order to maintain a given angle α, it is necessary to specifically control the orientation of the ship, and not just the displacement and expansion of its part.) Then the force acting on the dumbbell throughout the entire orbit differs from the force acting on a material point of the same mass by a constant factor $\cos \frac{\alpha}{2}$. In other words, for a dumbbell the tension force to the Earth is not $\gamma \frac{mM}{R^2}$, but $\gamma' \frac{mM}{R^2}$, where $\gamma' = \gamma \cos \frac{\alpha}{2}$. Here $\gamma$ is the gravitational constant, $m$ is the mass of the dumbbell, $M$ is the mass of the Earth, $R$ is the distance to the center of the Earth.

The dimensions of the ship are still small compared to the distance $R$, so the angle α is quite small, that is, the value $\cos \frac{\alpha}{2}$ is close to unity. All physical laws that are valid for a material point in the Earth’s gravitational field are also valid for our ship; it is only necessary to replace the gravitational constant $\gamma$ with $\gamma'$ everywhere. Thus, the potential energy of a dumbbell in the Earth's gravitational field will be equal to $\gamma' \frac{mM}{R}$.

In our case, all Kepler's laws are also satisfied. In particular, the spacecraft's orbit must be an ellipse, with the Earth at one of its foci.

Let our ship move in orbit with the balls pressed against each other. What happens if you quickly expand the balls through an angle α in perigree *)? The gravitational constant will change by $\cos \frac{\alpha}{2}$ times, and the potential energy of the ship will also decrease. This means that its kinetic energy will increase. The ship will be placed in a new orbit, also along an ellipse, but a little larger. Since $\gamma'$ still differs extremely little from $\gamma$, then at apogee it will also move extremely slightly away from the Earth, and at perigree it will return to the same point.

To choose the right tactics for leaving orbit, let’s figure out how the speed changes when moving in orbit. In the first half of the ellipse, from perigree to apogee (Fig. 3), the tension force is directed at an obtuse angle to the speed, so the speed decreases in magnitude. From apogee to perigee, the speed increases all the time, because the tension force is directed at an acute angle to the speed. On one half of the ellipse, the gravitational force slows down the ship, on the other, it accelerates it, and the work on acceleration and braking is equal in magnitude.

*) Perigee is a point in the orbit close to the Earth. Apogee is the farthest point in the orbit from the Earth.

This means that during one revolution around the Earth, the speed increases by a constant amount $w - w'$.

Now it is not difficult to construct graphs of the dependence of velocities and distances at perigree and apogee on the number of revolutions $n$ (Fig. 5, a, b).

After $n$ revolutions

$$v = v_0 + n(w - w')$$

$$V = V_0 - n(w - w')$$

$$r = \frac{v}{v_0 + n(w - w')},$$

$$R = \frac{v}{V_0 - n(w - w')}.$$

From the analysis of these dependencies it is clear that at the moment when the speed at perigree exceeds $w'$, our ship will no longer return to Earth.

What is this speed $v = w'$? In perigree $w' = 2\gamma' \frac{M}{v}$. Let's substitute $v = rv$. We get $v = 2\gamma' \frac{M}{rv}$, whence $v = \sqrt{\frac{2\gamma' M}{r}}$. This means that $v = w'$ is the second escape velocity at a distance $r$ from the center of the Earth. Now the meaning of $w$ and $w'$ has become clear - these are the second cosmic velocities for $y$ and $y'$ at a given angular momentum $L = mv$.

Now let's discuss how realistic such a spaceship is. If it has length $l$ in an orbit of radius $R$, then $\alpha \approx \frac{l}{R}$.

Then, as for small $\alpha$ cos$\frac{\alpha}{2} \approx 1 - \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)^2$, we obtain the relative difference of the attractive forces $\frac{\gamma - \gamma'}{\gamma} \approx \frac{l^2}{8R^2}$. For a ship about 20 m long in an orbit with a radius of about 10,000 km, the relative weakening of gravity is only $0,5 \cdot 10^{-12}$. But for a ship about 200 km long, the efficiency is already significant - $0,5 \cdot 10^{-4}$. Such a ship needs a little more than a year to leave Earth.

Interestingly, the smaller the orbital radius, the faster the ship will leave the Earth. After all, in the same time it will have time to make a large number of revolutions, and starting at a higher speed. The stronger the ship's pull, the faster it goes away. According to calculations by V.V. Beletsky, at a distance of twenty thousand kilometers from the center of the super-dense star Sirius B, a spaceship 200 km long could leave in just an hour and a half.
