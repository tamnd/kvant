A magnetic needle placed in a vessel with water served as a traveler for orientation at sea back in the Middle Ages. For a long time it was believed that the arrow pointed to the north, or more precisely to the Polar Region. However, observations during long sea voyages and on land showed that this was not true. Therefore, it is especially important for seafarers to know:

Where does the compass arrow point?

Christopher Columbus, on his way to the New World, drew attention to the important property of the magnetic needle. Soon after sailing (in September 1492), pushing west from the shores of the Iberian Peninsula, he noticed that the magnetic needle was increasingly deviating from the north-south direction. Observations on the Sun showed that in four days the end of the needle was swept away by a whole division (the compass scale consisted of 32 divisions, so that each corresponding angle was $11^{1/4}$). Many sailors perceived this unexpected and incomprehensible phenomenon as a shallow omen. It was even said that the captain, in order to avoid conflicts on board, had to tell the crew that the Polar Ice had been swept away, causing the needle to shift. However, as we approached America, the mixing began to decrease, and gradually the needle approached its usual position.

Thus, two important facts were established: 1) the magnetic needle can deviate from the north-south direction; 2) the magnitude of the deviation varies depending on the observation location.

The magnitude of the angle between the geographic meridian and the direction of the arrow (in the horizontal plane) is called declination.

The idea that the magnetic needle was pointing due north was dealt another blow: scientists discovered that the needle was not aligned horizontally; more precisely, it was noticed that in the northern hemisphere its northern end deviates downward. The smallest angle between the direction of the arrow and the horizontal plane is called inclination. The inclination also turns out to be different in different places, and the distribution of the magnitude of declination and inclination across the Earth's surface looked extremely complex. After this, a natural assumption arose that the reason for the deviation of the needle was somehow connected with the Earth. This assumption was developed in the famous book of the English natural spectral Gilbert “On the Magnet, Magnetic Bodies and the Great Magnet of the Earth,” which was published in 1600. To explain the behavior of the magnetic needle, the author likens the globe to a bar-shaped magnet, already familiar to scientists at that time. This model remains largely relevant today. Magnetic dipole

The electric field of two charges separated by a distance $l$ is called the field of an electric dipole. Although there are no magnetic charges in nature, there are magnetic fields, the pattern of field lines of which is similar to the pattern of force lines of the electric field of a dipole. This is, for example, the field of a magnetized rod at sufficiently large distances from it. By analogy with an electric field, such a field is called a magnetic dipole field. When describing such a field, it can be considered (although in fact this is not so) that it is created by two “magnetic masses” placed at the ends of a magnetized rod (see figure). For many years, scientists reasoned this way, likening magnetic masses to electric charges.

Let us first find the induction at the “far” point $A$ on the dipole axis such that

$$B_1 = -\frac{kM}{(L+a)^2}.$$

For the electric charge field it would be $E = \frac{k'Q}{(L+a)^2}$. Here $k$ and $k'$ are coefficients depending on the choice of system of units. At the same point, the induction of the “magnetic mass” field $M$ is equal to

$$B_2 = \frac{kM}{(L-a)^2}.$$

Using the superposition principle, we find the resulting value of induction at point $A$:

$$B_\parallel = B_1 + B_2 = 4 \frac{kMaL}{(L^2-a^2)^2}.$$

Since $L \gg a$, we can assume that

$$B_\parallel = 4 \frac{kaM}{L^3}.$$

The quantity $2kMa$ has a special name - the magnetic moment of the dipole; let us denote this important quantity by $p$, then

$$B_\parallel = \frac{2p}{L^3}.$$

Let us now find the induction in the plane perpendicular to the dipole axis. The field induction is created in a stripe - $M$, numerically equal to $B_3$, and directed in a straight line $DF$. Projecting the induction vectors $B_3$ and $B_4$ on the axis $OF$ and $CD$, we see that the sum of the projections on the $FO$ axis is equal to 0. The resulting projection on the $CD$ axis is

$$B_\perp = 2B_3\sin \alpha = 2 \frac{kaM}{(FC)^2FC}.$$

A comparison of formulas (1) and (2) shows that the dipole fields are more complex than the field of an exact charge: the magnitude of the induction depends not only on the distance to the field source, but also on the angle of the radius vector of the point at which the induction is measured with the dipole axis. When this angle changes from 0 to $\frac{\pi}{2}$, the field induction (at the same distance $L$) is halved.

Now let's apply the magnetic dipole model to describe the Earth's magnetic field. Let's imagine that inside the globe there are two “magnetic masses” that form a dipole. The dipole axis is extended so that it intersects the Earth's surface. The intersection points are called magnetic poles. The magnitude of the magnetic field induction near the stripes is twice as large as at the magnetic equator. Direct measurements show that near the magnetic stripes $B = 0,7 \cdot 10^{-4} \text{ m/l}$, and near the equator $B = 0,4 \cdot 10^{-4} \text{ m/l}$. If you are not specifically interested in areas near the stripe or the equator, then to evaluate the principles the average value is $B = 0,5 \cdot 10^{-4} \text{ m/l}$.

Using formulas (1) or (2), you can find out the dipole moment corresponding to a certain induction value. Assuming that the radius of the Earth is equal to $R_0 = 6400 \text{ km}$, we obtain that the value of the Earth’s magnetic moment is

$$p = 8 \cdot 10^{15} \text{ m/l} \cdot \text{m}^3.$$

Variations and anomalies of the magnetic field

One of the first points where external magnetic declination began to be systematically measured was London. Here are the declination values ​​by year: in 1540 - 7.2°; in 1560 - 9.6°; at 1580 - 10.9°. It turns out that the inclination changes over time, and the rate of this change is not constant. After 1580, the declination begins to decrease. Around 1660, the value of this value passed through zero and became negative, that is, the needle began to deviate not to the east, but to the west. The angle of deviation increased and in 1820 it was about 24°. Then the western slope began to decrease and is now about 16°.

Now the values ​​of the declination change on Earth by several decimal points. The results of the changes are recorded on so-called magnetic cards. On these maps, lines are drawn connecting points with the same declination value (just as on geographic maps - points that lie at the same height above sea level).

Thus, due to changes in declination over time - the so-called varnaces - magnetic maps lose value and need to be compiled again after a few years. The declination of the magnetic needle cannot be explained using the idea of ​​a permanent magnetic dipole located in the center of the Earth. Such concepts describe only the average behavior of the magnetic field. There are many magnetic anomalies on Earth, characterized by a significant deviation in the magnitude and direction of the magnetic field from the dipole model. Thus, in the southern tip of South America, the magnetic field induction drops to 2.5 \cdot 10^{-5} \text{ m/l} - less than at the magnetic equator. Some anomalies are associated with the occurrence of magnetic materials. Such, for example, are the values ​​of the Kursk magnetic anomaly, where the magnetic field induction exceeds 2 \cdot 10^{-4} \text{ m/l}. This is greater than the induction value near the Earth's magnetic stripes.

Earth's magnetosphere
and solar wind

So far we have been talking about the magnetic field on the Earth's surface. However, the Earth's magnetic field plays an important role in physical processes occurring at high altitudes, where the solar wind “blows”. The solar wind is a gas flow that extends radially in all directions away from the Sun. The solar wind gas is completely inactivated and consists of protons and electrons. In this state, it conducts electricity well.

The solar wind does not reach the Earth; this is due to the correspondence of the Earth's magnetic field. The flow of particles from the Sun is deflected and flows around the Earth in a region called the magnetosphere. The magnetosphere is located at a great distance from the earth's surface, much greater than the radius of the Earth. Therefore, the magnetic field here is significantly weaker than on the Earth's surface. Nevertheless, the magnetosphere still plays the role of a free “shield”, covering the Earth from penetration from space. Calculations show that the flow of the part located in the plane of the magnetic equator will slow down far from the Earth’s surface, at a distance of approximately 8-9 Earth radii.

On the other hand, the solar wind flow, moving along the Earth's magnetic axis in the direction near the magnetic pole, encounters on its path only a very weak, tending to 0, horizontal component of the Earth's magnetic field. Therefore, such part flows come much closer to the Earth's surface and cause strong possibilities in the Earth's ionosphere.

Geomagnetic dynamics

Recently, thanks to the advancements of astronautics, the possibility of an unknown magnetic field meter on the surface of the planets of the solar system close to us has become possible. It turned out that the Luns either does not have its own magnetic field at all, or its induction is, in any case, less than one tenth of the induction of the Earth’s magnetic field *). After this, the question is about the principles of the existence of the Earth's magnetic field and its changes in the coastal system. The Earth's magnetic field can be roughly described by assuming that it is induced by some electric current flowing deep within the Earth. Indeed, the picture of the electric field lines of a flask with current is similar to the picture of the magnetic field lines of the magnetized structure, which we replaced with the Earth's magnetic field. The physical reasons leading to the existence of such a current have not yet been definitively established. There are several theories trying to explain the origin of this current. Recently, the so-called “geomagnetic diamo theory” or, in short, “dynamo theory” has become popular among geophysicists. We will tell you what the physical basis of this theory is.

The results of geological research suggest that in the center of the Earth there is a liquid, electrically conductive core. The radius of the core is about 3900 km. According to the “dynamo theory”, currents in the core are formed due to electromagnetic induction by the rotational movement of a conducting core in a magnetic field. The magnetic field is created by the same currents. Thus, the magnetic field maintains itself, just as it happens in a diamo machine (hence the name of the theory). However, even before the start of such a machine, the tolerances of a significant “seed” field.
