9. In front of the mirror

Let's put a box with a “normal” arrangement of checkers in front of the mirror (Fig. 11). Let's draw a broken line on the box so that its mirror image looks the same as in figure 3. The location of $R$, which we will see in the mirror, corresponds to the even permutation

4, 3, 2, 1, 5, 6, 7, 8, 12, 11, 10, 9, 13, 14, 15

(twelve disorders). This means that the arrangement $R$ is flat.

Let $X$ be any good location. Let us prove that the arrangement $Y$, which is its mirror image, is planar. By sequentially moving the checkers, we will transfer the location $X$ to “normal” in front of the mirror. Then in the mirror the location $Y$ will turn into $R$. The location of $R$ is flat, which means that $Y$ is flat.

Since, in addition, every good disposition is reflected in the mirror in its entirety, it is thereby proven that there are as many good dispositions as there are bad ones.

At the same time, it was proven that there are as many even permutations as there are odd ones.

1. In textbooks on higher algebra

The parity of the number of four permutations to the number of odd ones is generally established using the concept of “transposition”. Below we consider permutations not of 15, but of $n$ numbers: 1, . . . , $n$. The evenness and oddness of such permutations are determined in the same way as in paragraph 2.

About the limit. If in some permutation we interchange any two numbers (not necessarily adjacent ones), then we get a new permutation. This transformation of a permutation is called *transposition*.

a) From any permutation of the numbers 1, . . . , $n$ one can go to any other permutation of the same numbers using several transpositions. Check it out.

b) Prove that every transposition changes the parity of the permutation (transforms an even permutation into an odd one, and an odd one into an even one).

b) Deduce from this that for $n \geq 2$ even permutations of the numbers 1, . . . , $n$ the same as there are odd ones.

2. Prove that the number of all permutations of the numbers is 1, . . . , $n$ is equal to the product $1 \cdot 2 \cdot \dots \cdot n$ (this product stands for $n!$; read: “en-factorial”).

As $n$ increases, the number $n!$ returns unusually quickly, for example, 15! = 1,307,674,368,000.
