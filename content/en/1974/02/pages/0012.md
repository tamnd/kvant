Using the dipole moment $p = 2kMa$ and replacing that at large distances FC ($FC \gg 2a$) we can approximately set FC = L, we write the formula for the magnitude of magnetic induction in the plane perpendicular to the dipole axis:

$$B_{\perp} = \frac{p}{L^3}.$$

(2)

A comparison of formulas (1) and (2) shows that the dipole fields are more complex than the field of an exact charge: the magnitude of the induction depends not only on the distance to the field source, but also on the angle of the radius vector of the point at which the induction is measured with the dipole axis. When this angle changes from 0 to $\frac{\pi}{2}$, the field induction (at the same distance $L$) is halved.

Now let's apply the magnetic dipole model to describe the Earth's magnetic field. Let's imagine that inside the globe there are two “magnetic masses” that form a dipole. The dipole axis is extended so that it intersects the Earth's surface. The intersection points are called magnetic poles. The magnitude of the magnetic field induction near the stripes is twice as large as at the magnetic equator. Direct measurements show that near the magnetic stripes $B = 0,7 \cdot 10^{-4} \text{ m/l}$, and near the equator $B = 0,4 \cdot 10^{-4} \text{ m/l}$. If you are not specifically interested in areas near the stripe or the equator, then to evaluate the principles the average value is $B = 0,5 \cdot 10^{-4} \text{ m/l}$.

Using formulas (1) or (2), you can find out the dipole moment corresponding to a certain induction value. Assuming that the radius of the Earth is equal to $R_0 = 6400 \text{ km}$, we obtain that the value of the Earth’s magnetic moment is

$$p = 8 \cdot 10^{15} \text{ m/l} \cdot \text{m}^3.$$

Variations and anomalies of the magnetic field

One of the first points where external magnetic declination began to be systematically measured was London. Here are the declination values ​​by year: in 1540 - 7.2°; in 1560 - 9.6°; at 1580 - 10.9°. It turns out that the inclination changes over time, and the rate of this change is not constant. After 1580, the declination begins to decrease. Around 1660, the value of this value passed through zero and became negative, that is, the needle began to deviate not to the east, but to the west. The angle of deviation increased and in 1820 it was about 24°. Then the western slope began to decrease and is now about 16°.

Now the values ​​of the declination change on Earth by several decimal points. The results of the changes are recorded on so-called magnetic cards. On these maps, lines are drawn connecting points with the same declination value (just as on geographic maps - points that lie at the same height above sea level).

Thus, due to changes in declination over time - the so-called varnaces - magnetic maps lose value and need to be compiled again after a few years. The declination of the magnetic needle cannot be explained using the idea of ​​a permanent magnetic dipole located in the center of the Earth. Such concepts describe only the average behavior of the magnetic field. There are many magnetic anomalies on Earth, characterized by a significant deviation in the magnitude and direction of the magnetic field from the dipole model. Thus, in the southern tip of South America, the magnetic field induction drops to 2.5 \cdot 10^{-5} \text{ m/l} - less than at the magnetic equator. Some anomalies are associated with the occurrence of magnetic materials. Such, for example, are the values ​​of the Kursk magnetic anomaly, where the magnetic field induction exceeds 2 \cdot 10^{-4} \text{ m/l}. This is greater than the induction value near the Earth's magnetic stripes.
