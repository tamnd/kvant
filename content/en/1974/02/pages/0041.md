Let the triangle $B$ be located angularly in the plane of the triangle $A$. Let us draw through the vertices $A_1, A_2$ and $A_3$ the lines $x_1, x_2, x_3$, respectively, the parallel lines $b_1, b_2$ and $b_3$. It is clear that triangles $X$ and $B$ are similar, since they are respectively parallel (Fig. 2, 3). Thus, we have constructed a triangle $X$, similar to triangle $B$, whose sides or their extensions pass through the vertices $A_1, A_2, A_3$ of triangle $A$. (Many readers have felt that a triangle like $B$ should be described around $A$ in the natural, "narrow" sense of the word, that is, points $A_1, A_2, A_3$ are not allowed to lie on the extensions of sides $x_1, x_2, x_3$, but only on the sides themselves, as on figure 2, but not on 3. If we rotate the straight line $x_1$ at the same angular velocity around its point $A_1, x_2$ - around $A_2$ and $x_3$ - around $A_3$, then we will get a whole family of triangles $X$. We will denote this entire family with the letter $S$. It is clear that each triangle $X$ of the family $S$ is similar to $B$. At figure 4 you can see how the triangle $X$ changes when turning the straight line $x_1, x_2, x_3$; not only what happens at the “critical” moment, when the triangle $X$ becomes very small; we will see soon that at some moments the lines $x_1, x_2, x_3$ pass through one point, so that the triangle $X$ degenerates to this point; then the straight lines $x_1, x_2, x_3$ approach their original positions (this happens when each of them rotates through the angle $180^\circ$).

Let's find out more precisely how the device $S$ is. Note that when two lines rotate uniformly around two points $A_1$ and $A_2$ (Fig. 5), their intersection point $P$ describes a circle with center $O$, for which $OA_1A_2 = OA_2A_1 = \frac{\pi}{2} - \varphi$, where $\varphi$ is the constant angle between the lines, $0 \leqslant \varphi \leqslant \frac{\pi}{2}$.

Moreover, for the point $P$ on one side of the straight line $A_1A_2$ the angle $A_1PA_2$ is equal to $\varphi$, and on the other ($\pi - \varphi$). It follows that each of the points $X_1, X_2, X_3$ of the vertices of the triangles $X$ circumscribe a circle. We denote the centers of these circles by $O_1, O_2, O_3$ (a circle with center $O_1$ passes through $A_2$ and $A_3$, etc.).
