F219. In the upper cylinder there is a model of an ideal monatomic gas. The cylinder is closed on top by a piston of mass $M$ and area $S$. Initially, the piston is held motionless, the gas in the cylinder occupies $V_0$ and has a temperature of $T_0$. Then the piston was released, and after several vibrations it remained at rest. Neglecting in all calculations the forces of tension, as well as the mechanical forces of the piston and cylinder, find the temperature and volume of the gas at the new position of the piston.

The entire system is heat-solided. Atmospheric pressure is $p_a$.

At the initial moment (immediately after the piston is released), three forces act on the piston: the force of gravity and the force of atmospheric pressure, directed downward, and the force of gas pressure in the cylinder, directed upward. If the equal force of all forces is directed downwards, then the piston begins to move downwards, but if the equal force is directed upwards, the piston will rise up.

To determine this, we will assume that the piston moves downwards. Let us write the equation of state of the gas in the cylinder at the equilibrium position of the piston:

$$pV = nRT.$$

From the piston equilibrium condition

$$p = p_a + \frac{Mg}{S}.$$

Posetom

$$\left( p_a + \frac{Mg}{S} \right) V = nRT.$$

We have obtained an equation relating $V$ and $T$. However, in order to find $V$ and $T$, you need to have one more equation. It can be obtained using the law of conservation of energy. Since the vessel is heat-solided, and the tension forces and heat capacity of the piston and cylinder are negligible, the work done on the gas is equal to the change in its internal energy:

$$A = \Delta U.$$

As is known, the internal energy of one mole of an ideal monatomic gas is equal to

$$U = \frac{3}{2} RT,$$

where $R$ is the universal gas constant. The internal energy of $n$ moles is equal to $\frac{3}{2} nRT$, and the change in the internal energy of a gas when its temperature changes from $T_0$ to $T$ is equal to

$$\Delta U = \frac{3}{2} nR(T - T_0).$$

Now let's find work on the gas slope. This work is done by the force of atmospheric pressure and gravity. Therefore it is equal

$$A = \left( p_a S + Mg \right) h,$$

where $h = \frac{V_0 - V}{S}$ is the distance at which the piston was swept. Thus,

$$\frac{3}{2} nR(T - T_0) = \left( p_a + \frac{Mg}{S} \right)(V_0 - V).$$

Note that if the piston moves upward, the gas itself does work against atmospheric pressure and the weight of the piston, while its internal energy decreases. Equality (2) remains valid in this case as well.

Solving equations (1) and (2) together, we find

$$T = \frac{3}{5} T_0 + \frac{2}{5} \left( p_a + \frac{Mg}{S} \right) V_0,$$

$$V = \frac{2}{5} V_0 + \frac{3}{5} \frac{nRT_0}{p_a + \frac{Mg}{S}}.$$
