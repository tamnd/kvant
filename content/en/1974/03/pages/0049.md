⟦folio 47⟧

Confusion with the “Inverse Theorem”
(For the ending, beginning, see p. 25)

If all your life you think that days are happiness. . . If you finally steal them, you finish them off. . . “Children, if the direct theorem is true,” if there is happiness in days. . . The direct one may be correct. The opposite one is an embarrassment. The stolen money did not give Nazarov happiness. . . “Children, if the direct theorem is true, this does not mean that its inverse is also true.” How to solve this theorem?>

Let’s leave aside the country’s figures of speech like “we call such reasoning a theorem” or “how to solve this theorem.” Let’s just figure out what kind of theorem the author has in mind, especially since he apparently liked the above passage so much that it even served as the reason for the title of the story.

As far as one can understand, the author calls the statement “if there is money, then there is happiness” a “direct theorem.” And with his story he quite convincingly shows that this “theorem”, the truth of which was Nazarov’s life credo, is not true - getting money by dishonest means does not bring happiness. The author sent a pshet: “The direct (theorem) may be true.” It is not true: mathematically speaking, Nazarov’s story is a counterexample that refutes its validity.

What does the converse theorem have to do with it? Alas, it has absolutely nothing to do with it (although, by the way, it is not true either). Apparently, the author, carried away by the external “beauty” of his reasoning, did not bother to understand in essence what the direct theorem is.
