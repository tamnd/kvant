a substance passes through three states - solid, liquid and gaseous. The question naturally arises: how will the properties of the substance change if heating continues further and the temperature goes beyond several thousand degrees? Of course, at a very high temperature, the picture we depict of heating a substance in a refractory vessel can only be imagined theoretically, since the limit of the thermal side of even the most refractory materials is relatively small - no more than 3000-4000 °K. However, this practical effect does not eliminate questions about how a substance will behave as its temperature continuously increases. Therefore, we will not abandon the adopted simple scheme for now. Let us assume that the walls of the vessel have the magical ability to withstand any high temperature without collapsing or experiencing any changes. So the heating continues. In this case, already at 3000-5000 °K we will be able to notice the first signs of the emergence of new processes that will be associated with changes in the properties of the atoms of the substance themselves.

As is known, each atom consists of a positively charged nucleus, in which almost all the mass of the atoms is concentrated, and electrons rotating around the nucleus and collectively forming the so-called electron shell of the atoms. This shell, and especially its outer layer, which contains electrons relatively weakly bound to the atomic nucleus, has a rather fragile structure. When atoms collide with any rapidly moving particle, one of the outer electrons can be torn off from the atoms, which turns into a positively charged ion. It is this process that is described and will be most characteristic of the considered stage of heating the substance. At a sufficiently high temperature, the gas transfers to be

neutral: positive ions and free electrons separated from atoms appear in it.

With increasing temperature, the relative fraction of ions and electrons in this mixture increases very quickly. Under conditions when the heated substance is in thermal equilibrium with the environment (in our case, with the walls of an imaginary ideal vessel) at a temperature of several tens of thousands of degrees, the overwhelming majority of atoms in any one are gaseous and neutral atoms are practically absent.
