Zhashikh in the horizontal plane $Q$, on the vertical plane $P$ (Fig. 6). Note that in this case the techniques will turn into direct ones. Indeed, let us draw a plane through a line on the plane $Q$ and a point $O$. The line of its intersection with the plane $P$ will be the image of the selected straight line. It is clear that if two lines intersect (not on line $l_2$, see below Fig. 8), then their images also intersect. Even images of parallel lines (Fig. 7) can intersect, and the point $N_1$ of intersection of these lines lies on the line $l$, passing through the point $O$ and parallel to the plane $Q$ (this is how the railway rails “intersect” on the horizon).

And what line on the plane $Q$ corresponds to the “horizontal” line $l_1$ on the plane $P$ - the “horizontal line”? There is no such direct line. At the same time, for any other straight line in the plane $P$ there is a line in the plane $Q$ that corresponds to it (check). On the other hand, for the line $l_2$ the intersection of the plane $Q$ with the plane $R$ (R is parallel to the plane $P$ and passes through the point $O$, see Fig. 8) and only for this line there is no image on the plane $P$ for any of its points, and even images of lines parallel $l_2$ and those lying in $Q$ do not intersect on $P$. The desire to eliminate such “discrimination” led to the creation of the concept of the projective plane.
