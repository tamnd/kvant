The linear line will correspond to a horizontal plane - “straight line”, and to points at infinity - straight lines lying in the horizontal plane (for example, $ON_1$ on figure 7) - “points”. In this model, the equality of all points and lines of the projective plane is most clearly visible.

The resulting model is very good for studying the properties of the design plane, but I would like to have a model in which the points would be points and the straight lines would be lines. It turns out that such a model is not difficult to obtain from the previous one. Let's add another hemisphere with a center at point $O$, tangent to the plane $Q$ (the base of the hemisphere is parallel to the plane $Q$). Then each line connecting the point $O$ with a point on the plane $Q$ will correspond to a point on the hemisphere - the same one at which this line intersects the hemisphere. In this case, the prime of the normal plane will correspond to a semicircle on the hemisphere, passing through the diametrically projected points of the border of the hemisphere. The family of parallel lines on the plane $Q$ will correspond to two diametrically projected points on the boundary of the hemisphere (Fig. 11), and the line at infinity will correspond to the boundary of the hemisphere.

Unfortunately, each “point at infinity” (point of the “line at infinity”) corresponds to two diametrically projected points on the boundary of the hemisphere. To avoid this drawback of the model, you just need to glue together two semicircles of the hemisphere boundary, but in such a way that the diametrically projected points are glued together. It turns out that this is not at all easy to do. Not only does it allow the hemisphere to bend, it also allows it to intersect itself. The result can be seen at figure 12 - the surface intersects itself along the line $AB$.

In this model, naturally, it is already difficult to depict straight lines well. Therefore, it is most common to use another model: a circle, the diametrically projected points of which are mentally considered to be glued together. Such a model is obtained from the model on the hemisphere (Fig. 11), if this hemisphere is projected onto the plane $Q$.

Six countries can be drawn on the design plane, each two of which border each other (Fig. 13). Note that on an ordinary plane there can be no more than four such countries. It has been proven that six colors are sufficient for the correct coloring of any map on the design plane (a coloring is called correct if any two countries that have a common border are colored in different colors), and for an ordinary plane, no map has yet been found for which four colors would not be enough for the correct coloring, but it has not been proven that any map can be correctly colored with four colors.
