The electron shell of a hydrogen atom contains only one electron, and therefore electron loss ends. In atoms of other elements, the electron shell has a more complex structure. It consists of electrons that have varying degrees of connection with the atom as a whole. Electrons belonging to the outer layers of the shell come off relatively easily. As mentioned above, at a temperature of the order of 30,000 °K there should be almost no admixture of neutral atoms left. This means that we can talk about complete ionization of the gas. However, it does not follow from this that processionization has ended, since positive ions in the mentioned temperature range retain a significant part of their “electronic clothing”.

The greater the atomic number of an element in Mendeleev’s periodic system, the greater the number of electrons in the atom and the more connected the electrons in the inner layers of the shell are with the atomic nucleus. Therefore, the final ionization of atoms of such heavy elements occurs only at very high temperatures (millions or even ten million degrees). Note that in a heavy gas, during final ionization, for each positive electron there will be only the same number of free electrons as there are initially in a bound state in the atom. In this case, the gas as a whole remains neutral, since the ionization processes themselves do not create an excess of charges of one sign or another.

The vonization of gas at high temperatures involves various interaction processes between individual atoms, on the one hand, and electrons, ions and light radiation, on the other.

A gas in which a significant part of the atoms is molecularized is called plasma. This name was proposed in 1923 by American physicists Langmuir and Tonk. Plasma is the normal form of existence of matter at temperatures of the order of 10,000 °K and above. At the same time, it is also the most common state of matter in natural conditions. The sun and all the stars are nothing more than giant clumps of high-temperature plasma. The upper layer of the Earth's atmospheric shell is also formed from plasma - this is the so-called onisphere.

In order to approach an understandable plasma, we took advantage of the simple idea of ​​heating a substance in some ideal state. However, in practical terms, this is not at all a practical and, in any case, not the most easily feasible method of producing plasma. Both in laboratory experiments and in technical conditions for producing plasma, various types of electronic discharges in gases are considered. During an electronic discharge, a current passes through the gas. The carriers of this current are electrons and ions, which form as a result of gas ionization. The process itself is inextricably linked with the passage of current. It is only thanks to the presence of current in the gas that new ions and electrons appear all the time, and therefore the degree of onization is maintained at a certain level. Whether it is lightning, an electron arc, an elegantly colored discharge in an advertising tube, or a discharge in a fluorescent fluorescent lamp - in all cases we are dealing with phenomena taking place in a highly ionized plasma.
