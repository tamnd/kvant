Plasma is a very common state of matter in nature. Its study began relatively recently. Plasma physics holds great promise for humans, primarily in the field of energy. It is plasma physics that must solve the problems of controlling thermonuclear reactions and creating thermonuclear power plants. It also includes work on new methods of generating electrical energy from conventional fuel without the help of steam boilers and turbines, as well as electric generators.

The article below belongs to the recently deceased member of the editorial collection of our magazine, Academician Lev Andreevich Artsimovich. He was the leader of Soviet research in the field of control of thermonuclear reactions. It was he and his collaborators who managed to carry out such reactions for the first time under laboratory conditions.

The text of the article is based on an excerpt from the popular science book by L. A. Artsimovich “Elementary Plasma Physics”, published by the Atomizdat publishing house in the third edition in 1969. The publication was prepared by V. A. Leshkovtsev.

Let there be a small amount of a substance in a closed vessel made of a very refractory material. Let's start heating the vessel, gradually increasing its temperature. If initially the substance contained in the vessel was in a solid state, then as the temperature increases, at some point it begins to melt, and at an even higher temperature it will evaporate, and the resulting gas will evenly fill the entire volume. When the temperature reaches a high enough level, all the molecules of the gas (if it is a molecular gas, such as hydrogen, nitrogen or oxygen) dissociate, that is, break up into individual atoms. As a result, inside the vessel will contain a gaseous mixture of the elements that make up the substance. The atoms of these elements will move quickly and completely randomly, experiencing random collisions with each other from time to time.

The average speed of chaotic thermal motion of atoms increases in proportion to the square root of the absolute temperature of the gas. The lighter the gas, that is, the lower the atomic weight of the substance, the greater it is. The value of the average speed $\bar{v}$ can be found using the following formula:

$$\bar{v} = 1,3 \cdot 10^4 \sqrt{\frac{T}{A}}.$$  (1)

Here $T$ is the absolute temperature and $A$ is the atomic weight of the substance. From formula (1) it follows, for example, that at $T = 1000^\circ\text{K}$ the average speed of hydrogen atoms is $4 \cdot 10^3\text{ m/c}$, and the average speed of mercury atoms is only $3 \cdot 10^2\text{ m/c}$.
