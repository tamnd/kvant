On the other hand, it is necessary to take into account that with the primary leakage of charges of one sign (electrons) in an ionized gas, an excess charge of another sign is immediately formed, which helps to equalize the flow of electrons of ions and prevents an increase in the difference between the concentrations of particles of both signs. The conditions under which this effect will be sufficient to support quasi-neutrality can be explained by the following considerations.

Let us assume for simplicity that in an ionized gas, in addition to electrons, only singly charged ions predominate. Quasineutrality means that $n_3$ differs very little from $n_1$. How will a noticeable deviation of $n_3$ from $n_1$ affect the behavior of individual particles? Obviously, everything will depend on how strong the figurative influence of the electric field that arises with such a deviation will be on the movement of charged particles. Two extreme cases immediately stand out here. If the number of charged particles in the volume is small, then the electric fields created by them are too weak to affect their movement, even if all the fields add up. In this case, individual electrons and ions in their behavior are in no way connected with each other and each of the particles of motion as if the others were absent. Consequently, the conditions of quasi-neutrality are not required here. The opposite case corresponds to an ionized gas with a high concentration of charged particles occupying a fairly large volume. In this case, the excess charges arising from a strong violation of the equality between $n_3$ and $n_1$,

In this correspondence, $n_3$ is the concentration of charged particles (the number of electrons per unit volume) and $r$ is the linear size of the region occupied by the ionized gas, for example, the radius of the spherical flask in which this gas is located. The appearance of the quantity $r$ in this expression is not difficult to distinguish. For a given concentration of charged particles, the immi potential created, and therefore the potential energy of an individual particle, depends on the size of the region in which these particles are located. Therefore, the quantity characterizing this size must be included in the formula expressing the conditions of quasineutrality. The quantity $5 \sqrt{\frac{T_3}{n_3}} = r_D$ is called the Debye radius after the German physicist Debye.
