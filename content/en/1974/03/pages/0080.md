Since the plane is moving uniformly, the vector sum of forces is zero. Consequently, the sum of the projections of all forces onto the direction of motion is also equal to zero. From the last condition it is easy to find that $f = mg \sin \alpha = mg \frac{h_1 - h_2}{l}$. When the plane rises (according to the condition, along a line making the same angle $\alpha$ with the horizon), then, in addition to the forces listed above, it is also affected by the traction force $F$, created by the motor and directed in the direction of movement. The drag force $f$ has the same magnitude, since the speed of the aircraft has not changed. Just as during descent, the sum of the projections of forces on the direction of movement is equal to zero, and the projection $mg \sin \alpha$ of the gravitational force and the projection $f$ of the resistance force are directed against the movement, and the projection $F$ of the traction force is directed towards the movement.

Therefore, $F = mg \sin \alpha + f = 2mg \frac{h_1 - h_2}{l}$.

As is known, the power is $N = Fv$. Thus, when ascending, the aircraft’s motor develops power

$$N = 2mgv \frac{h_1 - h_2}{l} = 200 \text{ ктм} \approx 272 \text{ л. c.}$$

The pressure of water on oil, according to Pascal’s law, is $p = p_1 + p_0$, where $p_1 = \rho_0 g (h + H)$ is the hydrostatic pressure of the water table, $\rho_0 = 10^3 \text{ кц/м}^3$ is the density of water, $g = 10 \text{ m/c}^2$ is the acceleration of free pressure. On the other hand, the pressure on water from the oil side should also be equal to $p$, since the oil-water boundary is motionless. It consists of the pressure $p_2 = F/S$, which is exerted on the oil before the sauce, and the hydrostatic pressure of the oil $p_3 = \rho gh$. Thus, $p = p_1 + p_0 = p_2 + p_3$. Hence $F = S [p_0 + \rho_0 g (h + H) - \rho gh] \approx 1,22 \cdot 10^4 \text{ н}$.

4. Let $A$ be a point and $B$ its image when lens $J$ is in its original position (Fig. 1). Consideration of $a$ from point to lens can be found from the lens formula $\frac{1}{a} - \frac{1}{b} = -\frac{1}{f}$. There are minus signs in the formula because the image and focus in the case of a diverging lens are minimal.

Hence $a = \frac{fb}{f - b}$. When the lens is shifted and occupies position $J'$, the image is on the secondary axis of the lens in a plane lying at the same distance $b$ from the lens (point $B'$). The required distance is $l = BB'$. From the similarity of triangles $ABB'$ and $AOO'$, where $O$ and $O'$ are the centers of the lens in the initial and final positions, it follows: $\frac{l}{L} = \frac{a - b}{a}$.
