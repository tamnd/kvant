Look out the window. You will see the landscape. Now imagine that this is not a window, but a painting. It is obviously impossible to blame the “author” of this picture for having a bad perspective. Let's try to repeat this picture, for example, with chalk directly on the window glass. Let's install it and take it to the window. And then the “picture” begins to change - new objects will appear on it, and those that were there will begin to mix and change their shape. However, let's go up to the window and try to depict what we see. Failure occurs: a slight movement of the head, and the picture changes, the images move to other places. What to do?

Take a look at the engraving by A. Durer (Fig. 4). Using an ingenious device, the artist draws the base as it is visible from the point where the screw is attached to the wall. Use this tool and you will be able to correctly depict the perspective of the landscape in your window. However, don't think that you have already become an artist. A camera will reproduce this picture even more accurately, but in order for a picture to become a real work of art, talent is needed.

But let’s leave painting for a while and move on to geometry. Let's consider the scheme of Durer's method (Fig. 5). Let us define a plane P and a point O in space. In order to construct an image of a point N on a plane P, we draw a line through points N and O. The point N₁ at which this line intersects the plane P will be the image of a point N on a plane P. This method of constructing an image is called a central projection onto the plane P from point O.
