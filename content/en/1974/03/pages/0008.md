Since the current supplying the discharge occurs in the plasma during a gas discharge, a large temperature difference between electrons and ions is usually maintained all the time. So, for example, in the gas-discharge devices mentioned above (advertising tubes, fluorescent lamps, rotary rectifiers, etc.), the values ​​of $T_3$ usually lie within several tens of thousands of degrees, while the values ​​of $T_1$ and $T_2$, as a rule, do not exceed one to two thousand degrees. In an arc discharge, which is used for electric welding, the electron and ion temperatures are close to each other due to the fact that in this case the discharge occurs in a gas with a higher density and the collisions between electrons and ions quickly equalize the temperature difference. However, even in the arc discharge, $T_3$ is still greater than $T_1$ ($T_3$ is on the order of several tens of thousands of degrees, and $T_1$ and $T_2$ are about 6000 °K).

Under some special conditions in a highly charged plasma, the ion temperature can significantly exceed the electron temperature. Such conditions arise, for example, during short-term electrical discharges of very high power under experimental conditions designed to study methods for generating so-called thermonuclear reactions.

Now let us somewhat refine the general understanding of plasma and its main characteristics. Plasma, that is, infused gas, can have a rather complex composition. Even if plasma is formed as a result of the induction of chemical processes, such as nitrogen, oxygen or mercury vapor, its ion component will contain ions of different types - with one, two, third or more elementary charges. It should be noted that in addition to atomic ions, molecular ions, as well as neutral atoms and molecules, may be present. Each of these components will characterize the thermal values ​​$n_1$ and temperatures $T$. In the simplest case, when they are all singly charged atomic ions, and the neutral component is completely dissociated and consists only of atoms, electrons, ions and neutral atoms will be present in the plasma. Such conditions are created only during intense discharges in hydrogen, action or tritium. In the example given, the concentration of ions $n_1$ is approximately equal to the concentration of electrons $n_3$. In a more general case, when the plasma contains singly charged ions with a concentration of $n_1$, doubly charged ions with a concentration of $n_2$, triply charged ions with a concentration of $n_3$, etc., we can write the following approximate equality:

$$n_3 = n_1 + 2n_2 + 3n_3 + \dots$$

This correspondence between the concentration of negative and positive charges in the plasma indicates that the plasma as a whole is quasineutral, that is, there is no noticeable excess of charges of one sign over charges of the other sign.

It is necessary to dwell on this property of plasma in a little more detail, since it is of significant importance and, in the final case, it contains the very definition of the concept “plasma”.

A natural question arises: to what degree of accuracy should the conditions of quasineutrality be observed in an infused gas?
