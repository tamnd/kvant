The law of conservation of energy in mechanics can be obtained using Newton's laws and knowing expressions for forces depending on the distances between bodies or their parts (gravitational forces and elastic forces).

The basic concepts used are work, kinemic energy, potential energy and total energy. These concepts, with the exception of potential energy, are not particularly complex. Nevertheless, the greatest number of misconceptions arise in the study of mechanics precisely in connection with the law of conservation of energy. The reason, apparently, is that, on the one hand, the study of mechanics is not always clearly visible in the sense of basic concepts, and on the other hand (and this is the main thing), often, to facilitate understanding of the essence of the matter, simple partial cases are considered, but then, on their basis, more general inclinations are thoughtlessly made, which sometimes turn out to be untenable. The purpose of this article is to dwell in detail on the misunderstandings associated with the concepts of work and energy in mechanics.

Kinetic energy

The simplest concept is kinetic energy. Using the definition of work and Newton’s second law, it is easy to show that in all cases the work of any dream $F$ is equal to the change in the kinetic energy of the body $K = \frac{mv^2}{2}$:

$$A = (F \Delta s) = \Delta K.$$ (2)

The nature of the dream is completely unimportant here. These may be dreams of gravity, dreams of elasticity, or the force of tremium.

The kinetic energy of an individual body is determined by its mass and speed and does not depend on whether this body interacts with other bodies or not. The amount of kinetic energy of the body generator, like the work of sleep, depends on the reporting system.

The kinetic energy of a body system is equal to the sum of the kinetic energies of the body included in this system.

Potential energy - the energy of interaction of a body

It is not always emphasized that potential energy in mechanics is the energy of interaction of at least two bodies. The concept of potential energy refers to a system of bodies, and not to one body. If a system has several bodies, then the total potential energy of the system is equal to the sum of the potential energies of all pairs of interacting bodies (any body interacts with each of the others).

Obviously, when deriving a formula connecting the change in potential energy with the work of forces, one of the bodies of the system is taken as stationary. So, when the distribution of load on the Earth under the influence of gravity is considered, then the mixing of the Earth can be claimed. Therefore, the work of the interaction forces between the Earth and the load is reduced to the work of only one force acting on the load. Or another example. A compressed or extended arm acting on a body is usually fixed at one end, and this end of the arm does not move (in fact, it is attached to the globe). In this case, the work is performed only by the elastic force of the deformed arm applied to the body.

Because of this, the potential energy of a system of two bodies is used to be considered as the energy of one body. This can lead to confusion.

In fact, the following statement is true in all cases: the change in the potential energy of two bodies interacting with forces depends only on the distance between the bodies and is equal to the work of these forces taken with a minus sign:

Change in potential energy of a deformation spring

Since work determines the change in potential energy, and not the energy itself, the change in energy in mechanics has a physical meaning. Therefore, one can arbitrarily choose the state of the system in which its potential energy is considered equal to zero. This state corresponds to a zero level of potential energy. The choice of the zero level is made in different ways and is dictated solely by one’s own convenience, that is, the simplicity of writing the equation expressing the law of conservation of energy.

Law of conservation of energy in a closed system

In a closed system in which conservative forces act, the work done by these forces is $A = -\Delta \Pi$. On the other hand, in all cases $A = \Delta K$. This means that when work is done, an increase in kinetic energy is accompanied by a decrease in potential energy and vice versa:

$$\Delta K = -\Delta \Pi.$$

This implies the law of conservation of total mechanical energy for a closed system of bodies:

$$E = K + \Pi = \text{const.} \quad (10)$$

This is all quite simple and misconceptions are rare. The situation is different if external forces act on the system.

Of course, external forces change the location of the bodies of the system (since their kinetic energy changes), and due to this the working of internal forces changes, which means the potential energy of the system changes. The role of friction forces

Speed-dependent forces in mechanics are friction forces. Like all forces, friction forces change the kinetic energy of a system. Moreover, although friction forces can perform positive work, the total work of friction forces of the internal system is always negative, and they reduce the kinetic energy of the system. You can understand why this happens with this example.

Let us find the change in kinetic energy in a system consisting of a cart of mass $M$ moving without friction at a speed of $v_0$ on a smooth horizontal surface, and a kipch of mass $m$ placed on the cart at the initial moment of time (Fig. 10). Let the kippchisna slide along the cart and travel a distance $l$ relative to it. After this, kippchisna along with the cart. The friction coefficient between the kipch and the cart is $k$.

So, the work of the friction force leads to the loss of kinetic energy of the system. But at the same time, under the influence of the friction force, the potential energy of the system does not increase, as happens under the influence of conservative forces. This is a consequence of the fact that the friction force depends on the distances between the bodies, and on their relative velocities. As a result, the work of these forces depends on the shape of the body's trajectory, and not just on its initial and final position in space. The friction forces acting inside the system change the kinetic energy of the system in the same way as external forces. Therefore, in the presence of friction forces in a closed system, its mechanical energy kills:

$$\Delta E = A_{\text{tp}} < 0.$$
