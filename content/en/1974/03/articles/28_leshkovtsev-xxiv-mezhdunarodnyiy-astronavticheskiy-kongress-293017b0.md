XXIV International Astronautical Congress

From October 7 to October 13, 1973, the XXIV International Astronautical Congress took place in the capital of Soviet Azerbaijan, Baku. Such congresses are organized daily by the International Astronautical Federal and International Astronautical Academies. The first of these organizations arose in 1950, several years before the launch of the first artificial Earth satellite. It represents the creation of national scientific societies engaged in the study of space problems. Currently, the Federation includes 56 member organizations from 36 countries. The Soviet member of the Federation is the Commission for the Research and Use of Outer Space of the USSR Academy of Sciences. The second organization brings together individual scientists working in various branches of space research. By the time of the congress, its ranks included 491 members from 29 countries.

There are more than 1,500 people from 28 countries at the precinct congress. Among them are Soviet cosmonauts G. T. Beregovoy, B. V. Egorov, V. I. Sevastyanov, V. A. Shatalov, as well as American astronaut T. Stafford.

The congress was held under the motto “Space research: impact on science and technology.” To further highlight this influence and pay tribute to other international organizations, two sessions of the congress were dedicated to the centenary of the World Meteorological Organization and two more to the 25th anniversary of the World Health Organization.

More than 350 reports on various areas of space research were read at the congress. Practically all the main problems that humanity faces on the way to penetrate into space have been widely condemned. Space methods for studying the environment and natural resources, technical processes in space, processes in jet engines, gas dynamics of space flights, scientific spacecraft, space technology research.

A separate meeting was devoted to safety issues in youth experiments on batch technology. Specialists from the Soviet Union, USA, Germany, Italy and Spain spoke there about the work of young space research enthusiasts on models of package and package engines.

During the congress, the VI International Symposium on Safety and Rescue in Space, the VII International Symposium on the History of Astronautics, the XVI Space Law Team and the III International Student Conference of the Astronautical Federation were also held.

A characteristic feature of most of the reports was that they examined problems that were still far from a final solution. The participants of the congress sought to open the gap of time that hides the future of space problems from our view. Among them are the creation of an international scientific laboratory on the planet Mars, an international rafted orbital laboratory and problems of space transport. Space transport is associated with the creation of reusable spacecraft, which, like airplanes, could launch into space more than once and return to Earth. Such ships could serve long-term orbital stations - change crews and supply them with everything they need. Transport ships can also be used to inspect artificial satellites - replacing their equipment and repairing their fittings in orbit.

Currently, man is being provided with the technical capabilities and knowledge necessary to create an international manned station in orbit located near Mars, that is, to create a common artificial satellite of Mars.

Space research is gradually becoming international. Soviet scientists actively collaborate with scientists from other socialist countries, as well as with France, India and other countries. On the first day of the congress, the director of the Institute of Spectroscopy of the USSR Academy of Sciences, Professor S. L. Mandelstam, spoke about the scientific results obtained as a result of the launches of the Intercosmos satellites.

By mutual agreement between the governments of the USSR and the USA, the first joint Soviet-American space experiment to dock the Soyuz and Apollo spacecraft should take place in 1975. There is still a lot of time left until this moment, but congress participants have already seen it on screen in a popular science film presented by the American delegates. And after this, our branch was shown about joint Soviet-American research “Bering”. They were undertaken to develop methods for studying terrestrial objects (vegetation cover, characteristic soils, snow and ice cover) from space. A report on these methods on behalf of a larger group of scientists and cosmonauts was made by V.I. Sevastyanov.

In connection with the upcoming long-term space experiments to the planets of the solar system, congress officials discussed in detail numerous scientific problems of man's additional flight in hostile outer space. These problems fall into two groups. The first group affects the human condition in such a flight. How will long-term exposure to irreversibility affect his body? How will a long separation from an attractive world and being in unusual conditions affect him - will his psyche fail? How will the body overcome the inevitable limitation of movements, lack of physical activity - the so-called physical inactivity?

Until now, man's stay in space in various experiments has lasted no more than three months. The data obtained would seem to convince us that the human body is capable of adapting even to such unusual conditions. Astronauts usually return vigorous and healthy. But the final answer to the question of how safe their stay in space was can only be given by long-term physiological and biochemical examinations. It is not for nothing that the Soviet doctor-comonaut Boris Egorov said at one of the press conferences in Baku that all scientific information received about the influence of lack of consciousness and other unusual factors characterizes the consumption of either the whole organism as a whole or its individual organs. But it still remains unclear how these factors affect the vitality of individual animal cells that make up our body. It will take a long time before we can answer such questions.

The second group of communication problems relates to providing astronauts with everything necessary for long flights - food, oxygen, water. If the flight is relatively short, all this can be contained in sufficient quantities. But in longer flights, a special system of life support for the astronauts is created. Such systems must restore the necessary components from the products of life insecurity, and carry out a kind of circulation of substances in the miniature world of the spaceship. At the congress it was noted that American scientists are trying to use various physical and chemical methods - absorbers, reagents, etc. - to restore necessary products. Soviet scientists are persistently searching for combinations of biological systems capable of converting waste from life's insecurity into new, valuable products.
