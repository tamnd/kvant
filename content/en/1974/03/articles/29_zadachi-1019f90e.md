Möbius strip and intertwining wheels

The surface of the ring that is pressed onto the finger has two sides (Fig. 1).
One side of the ring is in contact with the finger, the other side is the outer one. These sides have two borders (two edges), each shaped like a circle. If some insect wants to intertwine the ring from the outside to the inside, then it must inevitably cross one or another border.

It is easy to prepare a simple model of a surface of a completely different “style” - not double-sided, like the surface of a ring, but a one-sided surface (Fig. 2). August Möbius was the first to describe such a surface (in 1863). Twist one end of the straight paper strip half a turn and glue it to the other end of the same strip. The result is a model of a surface that does not have two sides - “internal” and “external”. This model is called the “Möbius strip”.

To make sure that the surface of the Möbius strip has only one side, return the colored pencil and begin to color the sheet sequentially, without lifting the pencil from its surface or crossing the edges of the sheet. Return to the place where you started, you will see that the entire surface of the sheet will be painted, although you have never crossed its edge.

Now take several sheets of thicker paper, glue, scissors and do some practical exercises with the Möbius strip and other models made from straight strips of paper.

To the article “On the law of conservation of energy in mechanics”

1. During the collision, a force acts on the ball from the side of the wall, always in one direction. Therefore the impulse changes:
   $\Delta (mv) = F\Delta t$.
The movement of the ball during the collision will first occur against the force F, and then in the direction of F. Therefore, the work A = -FΔs + FΔs = ΔK=0.
The work, and therefore the change in kinetic energy during the collision, is zero.

1. Let us denote the points where the initial charges $q_1$ and $q_2$ are located by the letters $P$ and $Q$. Let the distance between these points be $L$. Let us temporarily remove the charge $q_1$ to infinity. In this case, according to the definition of potential, it is necessary to do work $A_1 = -q_1 (\varphi_0P + \varphi_2P)$, where $\varphi_0P = q_0/4\pi\epsilon_0 l_1$ and $\varphi_2P = q_2/4\pi\epsilon_0 L$ are the potentials at point $P$ created by charges $q_0$ and $q_2$. Let us now move the charge $q_2$ to point $P$. To do this, you will need to do work $A_2 = q_2 (\varphi_0P - \varphi_0Q)$, where $\varphi_0Q = q_0/4\pi\epsilon_0 l_2$ is the potential at point $Q$ created by charge $q_2$ located at point $P$. The total work done when moving charges $q_1$ and $q_2$ is equal to $A = A_1 + A_2 + A_3 = \frac{q_0 (q_2 - q_1) (l_2 - l_1)}{4\pi\epsilon_0 l_2 l_1} = 3 \cdot 10^{-4} \text{ дж}$.

2. The plane descends in a straight line making an angle $\alpha$ with the horizon, and $\sin \alpha = \frac{h_1 - h_2}{l}$. What actually works:

the gravitational force, directed vertically downward and equal to $mg$, the lifting force, directed initially along the line of motion of the aircraft, and the force $f$ of air resistance, directed in the direction opposite to the movement.

⟦folio 78⟧

Since the plane is moving uniformly, the vector sum of forces is zero. Consequently, the sum of the projections of all forces onto the direction of motion is also equal to zero. From the last condition it is easy to find that $f = mg \sin \alpha = mg \frac{h_1 - h_2}{l}$. When the plane rises (according to the condition, along a line making the same angle $\alpha$ with the horizon), then, in addition to the forces listed above, it is also affected by the traction force $F$, created by the motor and directed in the direction of movement. The drag force $f$ has the same magnitude, since the speed of the aircraft has not changed. Just as during descent, the sum of the projections of forces on the direction of movement is equal to zero, and the projection $mg \sin \alpha$ of the gravitational force and the projection $f$ of the resistance force are directed against the movement, and the projection $F$ of the traction force is directed towards the movement.

Therefore, $F = mg \sin \alpha + f = 2mg \frac{h_1 - h_2}{l}$.

As is known, the power is $N = Fv$. Thus, when ascending, the aircraft’s motor develops power

$$N = 2mgv \frac{h_1 - h_2}{l} = 200 \text{ ктм} \approx 272 \text{ л. c.}$$
