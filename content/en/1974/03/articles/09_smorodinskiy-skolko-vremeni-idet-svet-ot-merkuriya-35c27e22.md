FROM MERCURY?

For a long time now, no one has been surprised that light takes time to travel from a celestial body to the Earth. The light from the Sun travels its way for about 8 minutes. This figure is easy to verify. The distance from the Earth to the Sun is 150 million km, that is, 1.5·10¹¹ m. The speed of light is 3·10⁸ m/s. Dividing one number by another, we get 500 s≈8 min.

The general theory of relativity introduces, however, small but very important amendments to such reasoning. The effects of general relativity are best seen around Mercury. This is what we will talk about.

The Mercury-Earth distance takes its maximum and minimum values ​​at the moment of “conjunction” of Mercury with the Sun, that is, at the moments when the Earth, the Sun and the planet lie almost on the same straight line. At superior conjunction, when the distance between Mercury and Earth is maximum,

it is equal to $r_{\text{max}} = 1,38$ astronomical units (1 AU is the Earth-Sun distance); in the lower connection it is minimal and equal to $r_{\text{min}} = 0,62$ a.u. (Fig. 1) *). If we multiply these numbers by 8 minutes, we get the rough time it takes for light to travel from Mercury to Earth in both positions. Such a calculation, of course, gives the correct result if we are not interested in small details. But it’s the “little things” that we’ll be talking about.

How can we understand this number 72 km? It is clear that it is difficult to calculate. However, you can get an idea of ​​the order of magnitude if you only know what the gravitational radius is and know how to work with dimensions.

The quantity characteristic of the gravity field of a massive body is the potential field, determined according to Newton’s law of universal gravitation:

$$\varphi = -\frac{\gamma M}{r}.$$

This formula includes two quantities: the product $\gamma M$, which characterizes the source of the field - in our case, the Sun, and $r$ - the distance. Instead of $\gamma M$, often in the theory of field relativity, it is hardened by another quantity different from $\gamma M$ only possible by telelem, but having the dimension of length

$$R_{\text{rp}} = \frac{2\gamma M}{c^2}.$$

This quantity is called the gravitational radius. The gravitational radius of the Sun is 3 km, the gravitational radius of the Earth is 9 mm *).

The law of universal gravitation can now be written as follows:

$$\frac{\varphi}{c^2} = -\frac{1}{2} \frac{R_{\text{rp}}}{r}.$$

On the left is the gravity field potential in dimensionless units; there is also a dimensionless quantity on the right.**)

*) Please note that it is customary to define $R_{\text{rp}}$ with a coefficient of 2. Sometimes there are formulas without this coefficient.
**) A dimensionless quantity is a quantity that does not change with changes in units of measurement.

The value $\frac{\varphi}{c^2}$ usually characterizes the magnitude of the gravity field of a massive body.

It can be expected that when light passes near the surface of the Sun (at $r \approx R_{\odot}$), its speed will decrease by an amount proportional to $\frac{\varphi}{c^2} \sim \frac{R_{\text{rp}}}{R_{\odot}}$ (since this is the only quantity that characterizes the field of the Sun):

$$\Delta v = \frac{c \cdot R_{\text{rp}}}{R_{\odot}}.$$

We can say that the space around the Sun has the optical properties of a medium with a refractive index slightly greater than unity.

If light were also affected only in the vicinity of the Sun, for example, on a section of path with an extent of 3-4 times the diameter of the Sun (let’s take 10 radii to determine), then we could say that the time of passage of light increases by values ​​$\Delta t$, determined from the equation

$$t + \Delta t = \frac{10R_{\odot}}{v_{\text{cb}}} = \frac{10R_{\odot}}{c - \Delta v} \approx \frac{10R_{\odot}}{c} \left(1 + \frac{\Delta v}{c}\right),$$

or

$$\Delta t \approx \frac{10R_{\odot}}{c} \cdot \frac{\Delta v}{c} = \frac{10R_{\odot}}{c} \cdot \frac{R_{\text{rp}}}{R_{\odot}} = \frac{10R_{\text{rp}}}{c}.$$

This estimate gives for $\Delta t$ the time required for light to travel a distance of 30 km. Of course, our estimate is rough; The choice of coefficient 10 is especially inconsistent. Moreover, as it turned out, the correct formula also contains the distance from the Sun to the planets and the radius of the Sun, as well as along the entire path (and not just in close radii). Deflection of a ray of light in the field of the Sun

It was said above that the space around the Sun acts on light as a medium with a refractive index slightly greater than one. This means that the light of distant stars, passing near the Sun, must bend its path in the same way as it does in the sun. In principle, this effect has been known for a long time. Even when Newton put forward theories according to which light is a stream of tiny particles - corpuscles, it was clear to him that light should be attracted to the Sun. Since in the field of gravity the acceleration of all bodies is the same, the path of light does not depend on the mass of the corpuscles and is a parabola. Recall that Kepler's laws do not include the mass of planets. Zandemann in 1801 derived from these messages a formula according to which a ray of light passing near the edge of the solar disk is deflected by an angle $\theta = R_{\text{rp}} / R_{\odot} \approx 0,85''$. This result turned out to be erroneous. In 1915, Einstein, based on the general theory of the relative surface of a new form he created, which gave twice the value for the effect

$$\theta = \frac{2R_{\text{rp}}}{R_{\odot}} \approx 1,7''.$$
