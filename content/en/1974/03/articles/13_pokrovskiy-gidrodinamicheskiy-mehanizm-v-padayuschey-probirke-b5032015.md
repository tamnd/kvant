Hydrodynamic mechanism in a falling test tube

G. I. Pokrovsky

Pour water into an ordinary test tube and, holding it with your hand as shown in figure, let it fall (from a small height), maintaining a vertical position, on the table surface. This surface must reach the top of the water for it to penetrate and produce a hard blow. At the moment of such an impact, the flow of water in the test tube, which (due to the action of capillary forces) has a concave shape, will quickly level out, and a thin stream of water will rapidly turn upward from its central part along the axis of the penetration. This jet breaks up into drops, and the first of them flies up to a height significantly exceeding the height of the fall of the test tube. This means that the energy of the water in the test tube at the moment of impact is redistributed in such a way that a small part of the water near the central part of the menascus receives greater speed and is rapidly thrown up.

A device that redistributes energy can be called a mechanism. Typically, this word refers to a combination of parts made of solid matter (levers, gears, etc.). However, there may be mechanisms made of liquid and even gases. An example of a mechanism of this kind is water in a test tube.

Hydrodynamic mechanisms are of particular importance when the action is very strong, which cannot be withstood by ordinary parts made of solid matter. For example, when a high-power explosive charge explodes, part of the energy of this explosion can be concentrated by making a recess in the charge and inserting a metal wave lining into it. The force of the explosion destroys the metal lining and creates a thin metal stream. The speed of this jet can (with the appropriate form of facing) reach the second escape velocity.

Thus, the observation and analysis of the assessment of a simple and modest phenomenon occurring in a test tube reveals one of the most interesting problems in ultra-high-speed hydrodynamics.
