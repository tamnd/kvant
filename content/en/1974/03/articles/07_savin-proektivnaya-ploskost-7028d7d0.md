Figure and plans

Recently, while rewriting a science textbook for the 3rd grade, I noticed two pictures: a classroom drawn by an artist (Fig. 1) and a plan of the same classroom (Fig. 2). Take a look at them. The class in figure 1 is depicted as you would see it standing at the board. What about the plan? You probably haven’t seen your class like this, even if you climbed onto a chair placed in pairs.

However, making a good figure classroom, room, courtyard is a much more difficult task than drawing the corresponding plan. Indeed, to draw a plan, it is enough to be patient and change the sizes of objects and the distances between them, and then transfer to a sheet of paper in accordance with the scale. A task that is feasible for any third grader.

And figure? “It takes talent,” you say. Well, undoubtedly, Galya Misevich from Kyiv is a talented girl, figure, made by him at the age of seven (Fig. 3), graced the cover of the magazine “Murzilka”. However, something is not right about him. It... lacks a character!

For the first time, artists of the Renaissance, especially Albrecht Duer (1471-1528) and Leonardo da Vinci (1452-1519), became interested in the correct depiction of personages. Then mathematicians came to solve this problem and created a beautiful science - design geometry, its foundations were laid by Gerard Desartes (1593-1661) and Blaise Pascal (1623-1662).

Take a look at the engraving by A. Durer (Fig. 4). Using an ingenious device, the artist draws the base as it is visible from the point where the screw is attached to the wall. Use this tool and you will be able to correctly depict the perspective of the landscape in your window. However, don't think that you have already become an artist. But let’s leave painting for a while and move on to geometry. Let's consider the scheme of Durer's method (Fig. 5). Let us define a plane P and a point O in space. In order to construct an image of a point N on a plane P, we draw a line through points N and O. The point N₁ at which this line intersects the plane P will be the image of a point N on a plane P. This method of constructing an image is called a central projection onto the plane P from point O.

Project plane

Let us mentally supplement the general plane with points of another “straight line,” which we will call “infinitely distant” (we will also call its points “infinitely distant”). Let's connect the elements of the plane with the new introduced elements.

First, we will assume that any straight line of the plane intersects with a line at infinity and, moreover, at a single point (this point, naturally, will be at infinity, since it belongs to the line at infinity).

Secondly, we will assume that any line parallel to the given one intersects the line at infinity at the same point as the given one. then this point is the intersection point of all lines parallel to this one (in the sense of parallelism on a common plane).

Now, with central projection, the straight line $l_1$ can be considered the image of the “infinitely distant straight line” of the plane $Q$, and the image of the straight line $l_2$ - the “infinitely distant straight line” of the $P$ plane, and only for the point of intersection of the “infinitely distant straight line” of the plane $Q$ with the straight line $l_2$ there is no place on the straight line $l_1$, that is, this point will remain “at infinity” (and similarly for the “infinitely distant line” plane $P$ and line $l_1$).

What are the properties of the resulting projective plane? And this depends on from what point of view you look at them. It is most natural to consider those properties of the projective plane that do not change during central projection. Indeed, in this case, the difference between the lines on the original plane and the line at infinity is eliminated, since the properties of the straight line at infinity $Q$ will in this case completely correspond to the properties of the line $l_1$ of the plane $P$.

We list the main of these properties:

1) For any two points, there is one and only one line to which they simultaneously belong.
2) Any two lines intersect at one and only one point.

In this case, the angles between the straight lines are also given; their values ​​are not preserved during the central design, and the distances between the points are not preserved. Each of these circumstances impoverishes the geometry of the projective plane. Nevertheless, a more meaningful theory emerges, which is called projective geometry and is distinguished by the extreme elegance of its theorems. Here is one of them.

Design plane models

We obtained the projective plane by introducing "points at infinity" and "line at infinity". Is it possible to see what came out of this? We saw the “line at infinity” (without one point) when we projected the projective plane Q onto the plane P. But at the same time, other points “went to infinity” - the line l₂. I would like to get a model of the design plane in which all the points were “visible”.

One such model has already been almost built. Namely, let us fix a point O in space and call the straight lines passing through the point O “points,” and the planes passing through the point O “straight lines.” Look again at figure 6-8. Each point of the projective plane actually corresponds to a single “point” of the model - a straight line passing through the point and point O, and to each straight line - a plane passing through the point and point O. The resulting model is very good for studying the properties of the design plane, but I would like to have a model in which the points would be points and the straight lines would be lines. It turns out that such a model is not difficult to obtain from the previous one. Let's add another hemisphere with a center at point $O$, tangent to the plane $Q$ (the base of the hemisphere is parallel to the plane $Q$). Then each line connecting the point $O$ with a point on the plane $Q$ will correspond to a point on the hemisphere - the same one at which this line intersects the hemisphere. In this case, the prime of the normal plane will correspond to a semicircle on the hemisphere, passing through the diametrically projected points of the border of the hemisphere. The family of parallel lines on the plane $Q$ will correspond to two diametrically projected points on the boundary of the hemisphere (Fig. 11), and the line at infinity will correspond to the boundary of the hemisphere.

Unfortunately, each “point at infinity” (point of the “line at infinity”) corresponds to two diametrically projected points on the boundary of the hemisphere. To avoid this drawback of the model, you just need to glue together two semicircles of the hemisphere boundary, but in such a way that the diametrically projected points are glued together. It turns out that this is not at all easy to do. Not only does it allow the hemisphere to bend, it also allows it to intersect itself. The result can be seen at figure 12 - the surface intersects itself along the line $AB$.

In this model, naturally, it is already difficult to depict straight lines well. Therefore, it is most common to use another model: a circle, the diametrically projected points of which are mentally considered to be glued together. Such a model is obtained from the model on the hemisphere (Fig. 11), if this hemisphere is projected onto the plane $Q$.

Six countries can be drawn on the design plane, each two of which border each other (Fig. 13). Note that on an ordinary plane there can be no more than four such countries. It has been proven that six colors are sufficient for the correct coloring of any map on the design plane (a coloring is called correct if any two countries that have a common border are colored in different colors), and for an ordinary plane, no map has yet been found for which four colors would not be enough for the correct coloring, but it has not been proven that any map can be correctly colored with four colors.
