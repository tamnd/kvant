Plasma is a very common state of matter in nature. Its study began relatively recently. Plasma physics holds great promise for humans, primarily in the field of energy. It is plasma physics that must solve the problems of controlling thermonuclear reactions and creating thermonuclear power plants. It also includes work on new methods of generating electrical energy from conventional fuel without the help of steam boilers and turbines, as well as electric generators.

The article below belongs to the recently deceased member of the editorial collection of our magazine, Academician Lev Andreevich Artsimovich. He was the leader of Soviet research in the field of control of thermonuclear reactions. It was he and his collaborators who managed to carry out such reactions for the first time under laboratory conditions.

The text of the article is based on an excerpt from the popular science book by L. A. Artsimovich “Elementary Plasma Physics”, published by the Atomizdat publishing house in the third edition in 1969. The publication was prepared by V. A. Leshkovtsev.

Let there be a small amount of a substance in a closed vessel made of a very refractory material. Let's start heating the vessel, gradually increasing its temperature. If initially the substance contained in the vessel was in a solid state, then as the temperature increases, at some point it begins to melt, and at an even higher temperature it will evaporate, and the resulting gas will evenly fill the entire volume. When the temperature reaches a high enough level, all the molecules of the gas (if it is a molecular gas, such as hydrogen, nitrogen or oxygen) dissociate, that is, break up into individual atoms. As a result, inside the vessel will contain a gaseous mixture of the elements that make up the substance. The atoms of these elements will move quickly and completely randomly, experiencing random collisions with each other from time to time.

The average speed of chaotic thermal motion of atoms increases in proportion to the square root of the absolute temperature of the gas. The lighter the gas, that is, the lower the atomic weight of the substance, the greater it is. The value of the average speed $\bar{v}$ can be found using the following formula:

$$\bar{v} = 1,3 \cdot 10^4 \sqrt{\frac{T}{A}}.$$  (1)

Here $T$ is the absolute temperature and $A$ is the atomic weight of the substance. From formula (1) it follows, for example, that at $T = 1000^\circ\text{K}$ the average speed of hydrogen atoms is $4 \cdot 10^3\text{ m/c}$, and the average speed of mercury atoms is only $3 \cdot 10^2\text{ m/c}$.

The question naturally arises: how will the properties of the substance change if heating continues further and the temperature goes beyond several thousand degrees? Of course, at a very high temperature, the picture we depict of heating a substance in a refractory vessel can only be imagined theoretically, since the limit of the thermal side of even the most refractory materials is relatively small - no more than 3000-4000 °K. However, this practical effect does not eliminate questions about how a substance will behave as its temperature continuously increases. Therefore, we will not abandon the adopted simple scheme for now. Let us assume that the walls of the vessel have the magical ability to withstand any high temperature without collapsing or experiencing any changes. So the heating continues. In this case, already at 3000-5000 °K we will be able to notice the first signs of the emergence of new processes that will be associated with changes in the properties of the atoms of the substance themselves.

As is known, each atom consists of a positively charged nucleus, in which almost all the mass of the atoms is concentrated, and electrons rotating around the nucleus and collectively forming the so-called electron shell of the atoms. This shell, and especially its outer layer, which contains electrons relatively weakly bound to the atomic nucleus, has a rather fragile structure. When atoms collide with any rapidly moving particle, one of the outer electrons can be torn off from the atoms, which turns into a positively charged ion. It is this process that is described and will be most characteristic of the considered stage of heating the substance. At a sufficiently high temperature, the gas transfers to be

neutral: positive ions and free electrons separated from atoms appear in it.

With increasing temperature, the relative fraction of ions and electrons in this mixture increases very quickly. Under conditions when the heated substance is in thermal equilibrium with the environment (in our case, with the walls of an imaginary ideal vessel) at a temperature of several tens of thousands of degrees, the overwhelming majority of atoms in any one are gaseous and neutral atoms are practically absent.

The electron shell of a hydrogen atom contains only one electron, and therefore electron loss ends. In atoms of other elements, the electron shell has a more complex structure. It consists of electrons that have varying degrees of connection with the atom as a whole. Electrons belonging to the outer layers of the shell come off relatively easily. As mentioned above, at a temperature of the order of 30,000 °K there should be almost no admixture of neutral atoms left. This means that we can talk about complete ionization of the gas. However, it does not follow from this that processionization has ended, since positive ions in the mentioned temperature range retain a significant part of their “electronic clothing”.

The greater the atomic number of an element in Mendeleev’s periodic system, the greater the number of electrons in the atom and the more connected the electrons in the inner layers of the shell are with the atomic nucleus. Therefore, the final ionization of atoms of such heavy elements occurs only at very high temperatures (millions or even ten million degrees). Note that in a heavy gas, during final ionization, for each positive electron there will be only the same number of free electrons as there are initially in a bound state in the atom. In this case, the gas as a whole remains neutral, since the ionization processes themselves do not create an excess of charges of one sign or another.

The vonization of gas at high temperatures involves various interaction processes between individual atoms, on the one hand, and electrons, ions and light radiation, on the other.

A gas in which a significant part of the atoms is molecularized is called plasma. This name was proposed in 1923 by American physicists Langmuir and Tonk. Plasma is the normal form of existence of matter at temperatures of the order of 10,000 °K and above. At the same time, it is also the most common state of matter in natural conditions. The sun and all the stars are nothing more than giant clumps of high-temperature plasma. The upper layer of the Earth's atmospheric shell is also formed from plasma - this is the so-called onisphere.

In order to approach an understandable plasma, we took advantage of the simple idea of ​​heating a substance in some ideal state. However, in practical terms, this is not at all a practical and, in any case, not the most easily feasible method of producing plasma. Both in laboratory experiments and in technical conditions for producing plasma, various types of electronic discharges in gases are considered. During an electronic discharge, a current passes through the gas. The carriers of this current are electrons and ions, which form as a result of gas ionization. The process itself is inextricably linked with the passage of current. It is only thanks to the presence of current in the gas that new ions and electrons appear all the time, and therefore the degree of onization is maintained at a certain level. Whether it is lightning, an electron arc, an elegantly colored discharge in an advertising tube, or a discharge in a fluorescent fluorescent lamp - in all cases we are dealing with phenomena taking place in a highly ionized plasma.

At the same time, there is one difference between the plasma, which, when the substance is heated, becomes equal to the vessel in which it is located, and the plasma of a gas discharge. The plasma of a gas discharge is not in thermal (tell) relative equilibrium. She heated

It is cooled from the inside due to the energy released during the passage of current, and is cooled from the surface due to contact with the cold walls of the gas-discharge device or with the surrounding layers of ordinary gas. The plasma produced by intense gas discharges can be many times hotter than the metal, glass or neutral gas that surrounds it. In addition, such a plasma is thermally nonequilibrium in one more respect. It consists of a mixture of several components, unequally heated. However, of these components are electrons, another are positive ions and a third are neutral atoms. They are also evenly mixed together, like oxygen and nitrogen in the atmosphere.

In contrast to the general gas mixture, all parts of which, unequally depending on their belonging to one or another component, have the same average kinetic energy of random thermal motion, the average kinetic energy of electrons, electrons and neutral atoms of gas discharge plasma is different. Electrons, as a rule, have higher energies than ions, and the kinetic energy of ions can exceed the energy of neutral atoms and molecules. Pozny can say that plasma is a mixture of components with different temperatures. As is known, the average value of the kinetic energy $\bar{E}$ of a part of the gas participating in the random thermal movement is related to the temperature $T$ by the following simple formula:

$$\bar{E} = \frac{3}{2} k T,$$

where $k$ is Boltzmann’s constant.

Due to the difference in the average kinetic energy of electrons, electrons and neutral parts in plasma, together with one general temperature, three different temperatures should be distinguished - electronic $T_3$, intern $T_и$ and atomic $T_a$. Usually $T_3 \gg T_и > T_a$. The very large difference between $T_3$ and $T_и$, characteristic of most forms of gas discharge, is due to the enormous difference in the mass of the electrons of the ions. External sources of electrical energy, with the help of which a gas discharge is created and maintained, transfer energy directly to plasma electrons, since it is light electrons that carry current. They gain their energy by colliding with fast-moving electrons. However, in each individual collision, due to the large difference in mass, the light electron transfers only a small part of its kinetic energy to it, bouncing off it like a ping-pong ball hitting a massive steel ball.

A simple analysis based on the application of the law of conservation of energy and the law of conservation of momentum shows that if a body of small mass $m_1$ collides elastically with a body of many times greater mass $m_2$, then the relative proportion of kinetic energy that a light body is able to transfer to a heavy one cannot exceed $\frac{4m_1}{m_2}$ (try to prove this yourself). The ratio of the mass of an electron to its mass is equal to $1:1840 A$, where $A$ is the atomic weight of the substance that belongs to it. Consequently, the most relative value of the energy transferred in one collision is only about $2 \cdot 10^{-3} \cdot \frac{1}{A}$.

An electron must experience a lot of collisions with electrons in order to completely give up the excess energy it has.

Since parallel to the processes in which energy is exchanged between electrons and ions, there is a process of acquiring energy by electrons from electrical sources.

Since the current supplying the discharge occurs in the plasma during a gas discharge, a large temperature difference between electrons and ions is usually maintained all the time. So, for example, in the gas-discharge devices mentioned above (advertising tubes, fluorescent lamps, rotary rectifiers, etc.), the values ​​of $T_3$ usually lie within several tens of thousands of degrees, while the values ​​of $T_1$ and $T_2$, as a rule, do not exceed one to two thousand degrees. In an arc discharge, which is used for electric welding, the electron and ion temperatures are close to each other due to the fact that in this case the discharge occurs in a gas with a higher density and the collisions between electrons and ions quickly equalize the temperature difference. However, even in the arc discharge, $T_3$ is still greater than $T_1$ ($T_3$ is on the order of several tens of thousands of degrees, and $T_1$ and $T_2$ are about 6000 °K).

Under some special conditions in a highly charged plasma, the ion temperature can significantly exceed the electron temperature. Such conditions arise, for example, during short-term electrical discharges of very high power under experimental conditions designed to study methods for generating so-called thermonuclear reactions.

Now let us somewhat refine the general understanding of plasma and its main characteristics. Plasma, that is, infused gas, can have a rather complex composition. Even if plasma is formed as a result of the induction of chemical processes, such as nitrogen, oxygen or mercury vapor, its ion component will contain ions of different types - with one, two, third or more elementary charges. It should be noted that in addition to atomic ions, molecular ions, as well as neutral atoms and molecules, may be present. Each of these components will characterize the thermal values ​​$n_1$ and temperatures $T$. In the simplest case, when they are all singly charged atomic ions, and the neutral component is completely dissociated and consists only of atoms, electrons, ions and neutral atoms will be present in the plasma. Such conditions are created only during intense discharges in hydrogen, action or tritium. In the example given, the concentration of ions $n_1$ is approximately equal to the concentration of electrons $n_3$. In a more general case, when the plasma contains singly charged ions with a concentration of $n_1$, doubly charged ions with a concentration of $n_2$, triply charged ions with a concentration of $n_3$, etc., we can write the following approximate equality:

$$n_3 = n_1 + 2n_2 + 3n_3 + \dots$$

This correspondence between the concentration of negative and positive charges in the plasma indicates that the plasma as a whole is quasineutral, that is, there is no noticeable excess of charges of one sign over charges of the other sign.

It is necessary to dwell on this property of plasma in a little more detail, since it is of significant importance and, in the final case, it contains the very definition of the concept “plasma”.

A natural question arises: to what degree of accuracy should the conditions of quasineutrality be observed in an infused gas? No matter how the inization is created, the advance advice is that there should be equal amounts of positive and negative charges. Due to the difference in the speed of movement of electrons and ions, electrons can more easily leave the volume in which they originated. Therefore, if, due to the processes of inization of atoms, the same number of charges of opposite signs are initially formed, then due to the rapid disappearance of electrons dying on the walls

The conditions under which this effect will be sufficient to support quasi-neutrality can be explained by the following considerations.

Let us assume for simplicity that in an ionized gas, in addition to electrons, only singly charged ions predominate. Quasineutrality means that $n_3$ differs very little from $n_1$. How will a noticeable deviation of $n_3$ from $n_1$ affect the behavior of individual particles? Obviously, everything will depend on how strong the figurative influence of the electric field that arises with such a deviation will be on the movement of charged particles. Two extreme cases immediately stand out here. If the number of charged particles in the volume is small, then the electric fields created by them are too weak to affect their movement, even if all the fields add up. In this case, individual electrons and ions in their behavior are in no way connected with each other and each of the particles of motion as if the others were absent. Consequently, the conditions of quasi-neutrality are not required here. The opposite case corresponds to an ionized gas with a high concentration of charged particles occupying a fairly large volume.
