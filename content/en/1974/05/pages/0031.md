From Berlin, Euler, in particular, wrote correspondence with Lomonosov, in whose work he highly valued the happy combination of theory and experiment. In 1747, he gave a brilliant review of Lomonosov’s statues on physics and chemistry sent to him for conclusion, which greatly disappointed the influential academic official Schumacher, who was extremely hostile towards Lomonosov.

Euler lived in Berlin for 25 years and worked in the same direction as in St. Petersburg. However, the aggravated relationship with the King of Prussia, Frederick II (who, although he bowed to Voltaire and French culture, was essentially a typical soldier) led to the fact that Euler in 1766 decided to return to the St. Petersburg Academy of Sciences, where he was greeted with the greatest honor and arranged as well as possible.

For the last 16 years of his life, Euler again lived in St. Petersburg. There he died in 1783.

Euler was distinguished by his incomparable ability to work and wrote about 900 scientific papers during his life, and this despite the fact that he lost one eye at the age of 31 and was almost blind in the second at the age of 66.

There is no scientist whose name is mentioned in educational mathematical literature as often as the name of Euler. It is enough to open volume 48 of the second edition of the Great Soviet Encyclopedia on pages 338-340 to find information about twenty formulas, equations, integrals, etc., bearing the name of Euler. There are even more of them in textbooks for higher education; and many of the theories and methods he introduced into the general system have long ceased to be associated with anyone’s name. Further schools still study logarithms and trigonometry largely “according to Euler.”

In addition to mathematics, Euler also dealt with many other, including very applied, issues - the main ship, cartography, mechanics, astronomy, physics, dioptrics. But still, Euler's main achievements relate to mathematics.

The complete collected works of Euler are designed for volume 72 (62 large volumes have already been published). 30 of them are devoted to mathematics, 31 contain his works on mechanics and astronomy, 11 will contain works on physics and other subjects. In percentage terms, work in mathematics is distributed (by volume, and this gives a better characteristic than by the number of works, since the works differ extremely in size) as follows: analysis - 60%, geometry - 17%, number theory - 13%, algebra - 7%, probability theory - 3%.

Within the analysis, a particularly large place is occupied by works on integral calculus - 33%, on differential equations - 25%, on series - 22% and on calculus of variations - 11%. The remaining 9% includes the volume “Differential Calculus” and the first volume “Introduction to the Analysis of Infinite Malch”. Overall, these statistics are pretty accurate.
