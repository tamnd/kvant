HE LIVED A HAPPY LIFE

I. K. Kikoin

In the history of physics, the names of outstanding scientists are known, whose properties and works more or less significantly determined their century. For example, Lomonosov almost a century before the “deadline” formulated the idea of ​​a molecular-kinetic theory. The same can be said about Tsiolkovsky, who was about half a century ahead of the era of rocket technology. The well-deserved recognition and words of these scientists came too late for them. During their lifetime they could not participate in the development of the idea. The science and technology contemporary to these scientists were not prepared to accept their ideas. And this circumstance, in a certain sense, was a disaster for such undoubtedly great scientists: they were not recognized

Academician Igor Vasilyevich Kurchatov was a happy scientist. He invariably intuitively felt the development of contemporary physics. He always dealt with the most pressing issues in physics. Thus, in the mid-20s, the electrical properties of dielectrics were one of the pressing problems in physics. It was to this time that Kurchatov’s work in the field of electrical conductivity of dielectric crystals dates back, which then led him to remarkable studies of ferroelectricity. Here Kurchatov, one might say, was doubly lucky.
