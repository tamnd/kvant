The analytical theory of numbers created by Euler continues to develop today. One of the most profound methods in it was created in 1934 by Academician I.M. Vinogradov.

Euler was also interested in the arithmetical nature of numbers. From him comes Goldbach’s formulation of the question of the transcendence of the numbers $\alpha^\beta$, where $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are algebraic numbers*). This problem was solved already in our time (1930) by A. O. Gelfoid, a member of the USSR Academy of Sciences.

What makes Euler's work on number theory not only is the depth of his ideas and the subtlety of his methods, but also the fact that he never gave up on the computational difficulties of the problem.

Much of Euler's work on number theory, sent to the development of academic publications, was then collected in the publication of his student Fuss entitled "Commentationes Arithmeticae Collectae", that is, "Collection of Arithmetic Studies".

Euler's work on geometry

All of Euler's works on geometry number 75, and they occupy three of the volumes of his complete collected works. Some of them, although interesting, are not very important. Some simply made up an era. Firstly, Euler should be considered one of the readers of research on geometry in space in general. He was the first to give a coherent presentation of analytical geometry in space (in the “Introduction to Analysis”) and, in particular, introduced the so-called Euler angles, which make it possible to study the rotations of a body around a point.

In his 1752 work, “Proof of Certain Remarkable Properties to Which Bodies with Flat Faces are Subject,” Euler gave a proof that in a convex polyhedron the numbers B of its vertices, P of its edges, and G of its faces are always related by the relations B—P+G = 2. This is, in some senses, the first major theorem of topology*) (the deepest part of geometry) in the history of mathematics, which (in a somewhat more general form) has not lost its significance to this day.

Euler's proof is based on the sequential destruction of the vertices of a polyhedron. If I were to present it now, I would have to say the following. Every convex polyhedron is defined by the specification of its vertices - it is the “convex hull” of its vertices, that is, the smallest convex body to which these vertices belong**). Let the numbers of its vertices, edges and faces be B, P and G. Let us remove one of its vertices O and consider the convex polyhedron of which it is the vertices.
