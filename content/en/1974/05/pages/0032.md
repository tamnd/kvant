Euler began to consistently build an elementary theory of numbers. He began with the theory of remainders (residues), which are obtained by dividing powers of a fixed natural number $a$ by a prime number $p$ (modulus) that is not a divisor $a$. It turns out that the residue $a^{p-1}$ is always equal to one. (This is the so-called “Fermat’s little theorem”*). Euler also gave its proof.) But $a^m$ when divided by $p$ can have a remainder equal to one, and for $m$, less than $p-1$. Then $m$ is a divisor of $p-1$ and subtractions of powers $a$ to $p-1$ are repeated periodically. Particularly important are those values ​​of $a$ for which, when divided by $p$, the remainder is 1 only when the exponent is equal to $p-1$, and not when less. Euler called them the first with different roots $p$. For such values, $a$ powers $a, a^2, \dots, a^{p-1}$ give all $p-1$ distinct non-zero possible remainders modulo $p$. If two numbers $b$ and $c$ have the same residues as $a^\beta$ and $a^\gamma$, then $\beta$ and $\gamma$ are also called desks of the numbers $b$ and $c$. When $b$ is multiplied by $c$, the index is added (that is, an analogy with the theory of logarithms is obtained).

Next Euler took up square residues. We call $a$ the square residue of a prime number $p$ if the residue $a$ is the same as that of some square. All residues are divided into two sets: square residues and square non-residues $p$ (with $p>2$ their number is equal, zero residue is not considered).

If $a$ is the product of several factors, then whether $a$ is squarely residue or not modulo $p$ depends on how many of its factors are nonresidues. If this number is odd, then $a$ is a non-residue, if it is even, it is a residue. The most difficult question related to square residues is to find, given a fixed $a$, the form of those primes $p$ for which $a$ is a square residue. Euler noticed that the $p$, which are in some specific arithmetic progressions, depends on the $a$. This is the so-called square law in writing*).

Euler also spent many years solving indeterminate equations of the 2nd degree in two unknowns. Fermat in 1651 invited all mathematicians of the world to show that for a positive integer $A$, which is not a square, the indefinite equation $x^2-Ay^2=1$ (now called Pell’s equation) has infinitely many solutions $(x,y)$ in integers. Euler studied the general indefinite equation $ax^2+bxy+cy^2+dx+ey+f=0$, where $a, b, c, d, e$ and $f$ are integers and $x$ and $y$ also look for integers.

The same 1st degree equations $ax+by+c=0$ were solved in ancient times: if $c$ is divisible by the most common divisor $a$ and $b$, then such an equation always has infinitely many solutions.

Euler realized that for a 2nd degree equation the problem posed by Fermat coincided with the proof that the square root of the natural number $A$ (if $A$ is not a square) always resolves into a periodic continued fraction**)
