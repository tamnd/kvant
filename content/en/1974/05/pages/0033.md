⟦folio 31⟧

$$\sqrt{A} = a_1 + \frac{1}{a_2} + \frac{1}{a_3} + \dots$$

The function $\phi(n)$, introduced by Euler, equal to the number of numbers less than $n$ and coprime to $n$, also played a very important role in the ordering of elementary number theory.

In all these three functional questions (which, more than two centuries after Euler, constituted the bulk of elementary number theory) - power residues, the law of reciprocity, the solution of an indefinite equation of the 2nd degree - Euler went very far, but in all three he failed: the existence of a primitive root for every $p$ was proved by Gauss, and as he proved! The law of reciprocity was also proved by Gauss, and he gave 6 different, but, admittedly, very difficult proofs. The periodicity of the expansion $\sqrt{A}$ was proved by Lagrange in 1768, and thereby, as Euler had previously shown, he solved the general equation of the 2nd degree and, in particular, the Pell equation. This work of Lagrange is undoubtedly the crown jewel in Lagrange's crown of glory as a mathematician. It subsequently had the goal of a number of replacement generalizations, which are still being worked on today.

In Euler's correspondence with his friend Goldbach, an academician of the St. Petersburg Academy of Sciences, there are two significant “Goldbach tasks”: to prove that every odd natural number is the sum of three prime numbers, and every integer is the sum of two. The first of these statements was proven using a very substitution method already in our time (1937) by Academician I.M. Vinogradov, but the second has not been proven to this day.

Euler took the initiative to create the second part of the theory of numbers - the analytical theory of numbers, in which deeply

$$\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^s} = \prod \frac{1}{1 - \frac{1}{p^s}},$$

where $\Pi$ denotes the product, which is taken over all prime numbers $p$. From this formula he obtained a new, similar proof of the infinity of the number of prime numbers, and from it he deduced (though without a very strict justification) the approximate equality

$$\sum_{p \leqslant x} \frac{1}{p} \approx \ln \sum_{n \leqslant x} \frac{1}{n}$$

(here $p$, as above, are prime numbers, and $n$ are integers).

From this equality arose the current method of studying the law of distribution of prime numbers $p$ in the natural series of numbers and in progressions. With its help, P. L. Chebyshev subsequently established how the function $\pi(x)$ behaves - the number of prime numbers $p$ not exceeding the number $x$ - with $x$ tending to infinity**), and Zh. A. Damr, using Riemann’s most profound report on the behavior of $\zeta(s)$ in the complexity plane, proved the limit law:

$$\pi(x) : \frac{x}{\ln x} \rightarrow 1.$$

*) $\Sigma$ — summation sign; for example, the entry $\sum_{n=k} f(n)$ means the following amount: $f(k) + f(k+1) + \dots + f(l)$.
**) See «Kvant», 1971, No. 5.
