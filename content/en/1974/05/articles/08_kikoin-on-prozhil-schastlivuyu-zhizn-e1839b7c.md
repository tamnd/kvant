HE LIVED A HAPPY LIFE

I. K. Kikoin

In the history of physics, the names of outstanding scientists are known, whose properties and works more or less significantly determined their century. For example, Lomonosov almost a century before the “deadline” formulated the idea of ​​a molecular-kinetic theory. The same can be said about Tsiolkovsky, who was about half a century ahead of the era of rocket technology. The well-deserved recognition and words of these scientists came too late for them. During their lifetime they could not participate in the development of the idea. The science and technology contemporary to these scientists were not prepared to accept their ideas. And this circumstance, in a certain sense, was a disaster for such undoubtedly great scientists: they were not recognized

Academician Igor Vasilyevich Kurchatov was a happy scientist. He invariably intuitively felt the development of contemporary physics. He always dealt with the most pressing issues in physics. Thus, in the mid-20s, the electrical properties of dielectrics were one of the pressing problems in physics. It was to this time that Kurchatov’s work in the field of electrical conductivity of dielectric crystals dates back, which then led him to remarkable studies of ferroelectricity. Here Kurchatov, one might say, was doubly lucky. His research into ferroelectricity coincided in time with the advent of the quantum theory of ferromagnetism, the electrical analogue of which is ferroelectric

Since the 30s, as is known, the rapid development of nuclear physics began, which was accompanied by a cascade of major discoveries. This is the discovery of the neutron, positron, artificial radioactivity, etc. Kurchatov will decisively switch his laboratory to work in this promising area of ​​physics.

At this time, at the Leningrad Physico-Technical Institute (LPTI), where Kurchatov worked, there was practically no “culture” of atomic nucleus physics, except for the small laboratory of D. V. Skobeltsyn, which mainly dealt with cosmic rays.

Kurchatov and his group had to start everything practically from scratch. At this time, Igor Vasilyevich spent whole days in the library and studied literature. The “literary” period lasted relatively short. Very soon, experimental work on nuclear physics began in Kurchatov’s laboratory. Quite quickly, the main direction of his interest was determined: artificial radioactivity during irradiation with neutrons. Then one could see a typical picture: Igor Vasilyevich rushes from one end of the corridor to the other with an irradiated image in his hand to study the next short-lived nucleus.

Soon after the start of work, a monograph by I.V. Kurchatov was published entitled “Disintegration of the Atomic Nucleus” (1935). In this regard, I cannot help but recall the tragicomic event at the institute, one of the heroes of which was the author of these lines. In the early 30s, I needed to use α-particles in aluminum powder, which served as a source of positrons (this was shortly after the Jollu-Curie spouses discovered artificial radioactivity). For this purpose, I used a radio station, which was covered with a thin mica window. The irradiated sample was placed on the CLUDE. To avoid the danger of radion leakage, the role was stored in another room, which was invariably locked. However, by some chance the room was not closed, someone entered it and, apparently by accident, the mica window of the role fell through. The counters in all the institute's laboratories immediately started singing. Got up

panic. Measures were immediately taken: all windows were open, all ventilation was turned on. A few hours later the “accident” was cleared and the meters returned to normal. One could have calmed down on this if it had not been for the introduction of one of the employees, who decided to report this case to the director of the institute, Abram Fedorovich Ioffe. A.F. Ioffe had a highly developed wealth of radioactive radiation. The incident greatly upset him, and he appointed a commission to investigate, find out the causes and determine the consequences. Kurchatov was appointed chairman of the commission. Calculations showed that even if the long-lived decay product of radon - for the sake of E with a decay period of 29 years - from its entire amplitude had settled on the surface of the institute building, the radioactivity would have been an order of magnitude less than the natural background. The commission drew up a corresponding act, which reassured A.F. Ioffe. However, over the course of several months, this event was remembered more than once, and it was reflected in one of the regular skits.
