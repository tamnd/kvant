PHYSICS
AT THE USSR ACADEMY OF SCIENCES
(1917-1974)
V. A. Leshkovtsev

The Academy of Sciences, created by Peter I, was almost the only center of physical science in Russia throughout the 18th century. But already at the very beginning of the 19th century, new scientific centers began to emerge and successfully develop in the country, where physical research was carried out. These were universities, the Medical-Surgical Academy, and the Moscow Society for Test Products. Over time, non-academic physics began to play a very important role in the development of physics in Russia. Alexander Grigorievich Stoletov, Nikolai Alekseevich Umov, Pyotr Nikolaevich Lebedev, Alexander Stepanovich Popov and a number of other famous Russian physicists were not members of the Academy of Sciences. We can say that the role of the Academy in the development of domestic physics at the end of the 19th and beginning of the 20th centuries was modest.

This situation changed radically soon after the Great October Revolution. In the early thirties, the Academy of Sciences became the highest scientific institution of the Soviet Union, uniting the largest scientists in various fields of knowledge, including physicists. Already in these years, we see in its composition the outstanding Soviet physicists Abram Fedorovich Noffe, Dmitry Sergeevich Rozhdestvensky, Leonid Isaakovich Mandelstam, Sergei Ivanovich Vavilov,

Dmitry Vladimirovich Skobeltsyn, Nikolai Dmitrievich Papaleksi, Vladimir Aleksadrovich Fok, Igor Evgenievich Tamm, Pyotr Leonidovich Kapitsa, Lev Davidovich Landu and others. The network of scientific institutes involved in the development of physical problems is rapidly expanding.

Physics has a decisive influence on the development of the technology sector - energy, communications, industrial production. In our country, it is one of the main driving forces of the scientific and technological revolution that is transforming modern industry. In this regard, there is a need for the successful development of physical research.

Currently, the USSR Academy of Sciences includes more than twenty scientific physical institutes. Secondary physical institutes named after A.F. Ioffe, Institute of Physical Problems named after S.I. Vavilov, Institute of Critical Science named after A.V. Shubnikov, Institute of Theoretical Physics named after L.D. Landu, Institute of Radio Engineering and Electronics, Institute of Creative Body Physics, Institute of High Fission Physics and others. Numerous scientific physical institutes have been created in the Republican Academies. In addition, members of the Academy lead work in many physical institutes.

The USSR Academy of Sciences determines the development of modern physics and ensures the training of scientific workers of the highest qualifications.

Despite the large number of directions into which modern physics has been divided, its main sections will remain the physics of the creative body, optics and radio electronics, the physics of the atomic nucleus and elementary particles. In each of these areas, modern physicists have carried out research that has become classic.

We will teach our story from the physics of the created body.

A huge contribution to this extremely important area of ​​physics was made by the work of Academician Abram Fedorovich Yuffe and his students.

Most created bodies have a critical structure. When studying their properties, physicists always

strive to use samples that are as close as possible to ideal critical structures and in terms of the degree of chemical purity. This is fully justified, because only with perfect images can unambiguous research results be obtained.

For a long time, physics was dominated by the idea that real critical bodies differ little from ideal ones. The generally accepted theory of critical bodies, developed by Max Born, was based on the idea of ​​an ideal crystal, where every atom is in its place, and any structural disturbances (impurities, internal defects) are completely absent. This theory described many properties of critical bodies (electrical conductivity, thermal conductivity, etc.). But as soon as it comes to determining tensile strength, there is a huge discrepancy between theoretical ideas and examinations.

A.F. Ioffe's method became one of the main methods for studying the mechanism of deformation of crystalline bodies.

Continuing these studies, A.F. Ioffe established that plastic deformation occurs in a crystal not intermittently, as all physicists had thought until then, but in an abrupt manner. Under non-permanent load, the deformation occurs in jumps, repeating at regular intervals and even accompanied by faint clicks, reminiscent of a clock ticking.

With these, as well as some other works, A. F. Ioffe laid the foundation for modern ideas about the mechanism of strength and plasticity of real solids. He gave physicists a profound idea about the need to study various defects in the crystal lattice, which extremely strongly influence many of the properties of solids.

Another area of ​​solid-state physics, in which Academician A.F. Ioffe also made a huge contribution together with his students, is semiconductor physics. Today it is difficult to imagine the development of technology without semiconductors. But more than forty years ago, when A.F. Ioffe began a systematic study of the properties of semiconductors, many physicists were critical of this endeavor. At that time, it seemed that only metals and dielectrics were materials worthy of serious physical research. Conductors and insulators are important and necessary technical things, but semiconductors, although they include a large number of natural compounds, are a useless and unpromising material. But A.F. Ioffe brilliantly presented the enormous revolutionary role that semiconductors are already playing in technology today. “It can be suggested,” he said a little later, “that the progress of technology in much smaller quantities makes it possible to become one of the main methods for studying the mechanism of deformation of crystalline bodies.

At first, it was necessary to develop methods for obtaining sufficiently pure semiconductors and methods for determining their basic physical characteristics: the concentration of current carriers, the type of conductivity (electronic or hole), mobility of electrons and holes, etc. Many of these methods, first created by A.F. Ioffe and his students, subsequently became classified.

Ioffe's school carried out a whole series of Püner studies on the electrical, galvanomagnetic, thermoelectric and photoelectric properties of various types of semiconductors.

One of the most important results obtained by A.F. Ioffe and his colleagues was the discovery of the enormous influence of impurities on the electrical properties of semiconductors. A.F. Ioffe showed that the application only changes the conductivity of semiconductors over a wide range, but can even change the current storage device, transforming an electronic semiconductor into a hole one and vice versa. Moreover, the role of application can be played not only by foreign atoms, but also by the semiconductor’s own atoms in case of their excess or deficiency. For example, an excess of atoms in a PbS semiconductor makes this semiconductor an electronic one, and an excess of sulfur atoms makes it a hole semiconductor.

A.F. Ioffe was the first to formulate and experimentally substantiate modern ideas about the mechanism of the rectifying action of semiconductors. He showed that as a result of contact of two semiconductors with different current carriers - electron and hole - a blocking layer is formed (in modern terminology, a “p-n junction”). In this case, the current can freely pass through the contacting

A.F. Ioffe paid especially great attention to the study of thermoelectric and photoelectric properties of semiconductors. Using these properties, it is possible to create new methods for directly converting the energy of heat and light into electrical energy. The work of A.F. A.F. Ioffe created the theory of thermoelectric and thermoelectric refrigerators, opening a new general technology for modern technology - semiconductor energy. Under his leadership, ten new types of semiconductor and energy devices were designed, which received a variety of practical applications.

The analogy between electric and magnetic fields has been known for a long time. There is also a well-known formula analogy between magnetic and dielectric constants. But for a long time, among dielectrics, a substance with an anomalously high dielectric constant, similar to the anomalous magnetic permeability of ferromagnetic materials, was not discovered. Only in the twenties were several such substances found, including Rochelle salt. Their research was carried out by academician Igor Vasilyevich Kurchatov and his colleagues. Within a short time, their work laid the foundation for a new field of dielectric physics called ferroelectric. It turned out that the dependence of the electric moment of the Rochelle cols on the external electric field is the same as the dependence of the magnetized ferromagnet on the magnetic field: the same histeresis loop is observed. The Curie point (there were two of them) and the phenomena accompanying the transition through this point were studied. In a word, the electrical version of the long history of the study of ferromagnets was reproduced.

In the history of Soviet physics, the name of Academician I.V. Kurchatov is inextricably linked with the solution to the great problem of creating domestic atomic and thermonuclear weapons and the development of nuclear energy.

You will find a story about this research in the article by Academician I.K. Kikon, “He Wasted a Happy Life,” published in the same issue of our magazine.

In modern physics of the creative body, theoretical physics plays an important role with its complex mathematical apparatus. The crystalline body, which was previously viewed as a system consisting of vibrating atoms and molecules, is now viewed as a volume filled with background gas. Phonons are called quanta of the field of a bulb lattice - by analogy with phonomes, quanta of an electromagnetic field. The phonon, like the photon, behaves in many respects as if it were a particle; therefore they are often called quasiparticles. The honor of introducing the concept of “phonon” into science belongs to academician Igor Evgenievich Tamm, who in 1930 used the concept of “elasticity quantum” when considering the issue of light scattering in crystals.

In the same year, the “phonon” was introduced into the physical literature by Yakov Ilyich Frenkel, corresponding member of the USSR Academy of Sciences. The behavior of a phonon gas is described using the same mathematical apparatus as the study of an absolute black body. Without the concept of a phonon, it would be difficult to imagine how any excitation, such as the vibration of an individual atom, moves in a solid.

In 1931, Ya. I. Frenkel theoretically predicted a very interesting physical phenomenon. Solving the problem of excitation of atoms by light in an ideal crystal, he showed that the excited state that arises in any atom of such a crystal after the absorption of a light quantum cannot be localized where this atom is located, and must not move throughout the crystal in the form of a free-valued quasiparticle, which Frenkel calls an “exciton.”

The fact is that, as calculations show, the energy of the crystal will not change if not the initial excited atom, but any other atom of the crystal is in the same excited state. The states in which any of the atoms of the crystal is excited are physically indistinguishable. Therefore, the excitation energy will move from.atoma to.atomy until one of the atoms that received it goes into a normal unexcited state, emitting the quantum it received. The existence of excitons was later confirmed in experiments by Corresponding Member of the USSR Academy of Sciences Evgeniy Fedorovich Gross. There is now a broad scientific field that can be called “exciton physics.”

For a long time, physics could not give the correct answer to such a seemingly simple question: “Why is the sky blue?” Even Newton, who devoted many years of hard work to this problem, was never able to solve it. Another English physicist, Rayleigh, was the first to create a satisfactory theory of light scattering in the atmosphere. Assuming that the light scattered by the molecules was not affected, there was a good agreement with the observational results.

It would seem that everything is clear, the problem is completely solved. And only one physicist, Academician L.I. Mandelstam, did not agree with this interpretation. He proved that the true cause of the blue color of the sky is density fluctuations (random changes in air density relative to the average density) occurring under the influence of thermal motion. L.I. Mandelstam showed that Rayleigh's formula is correct, and the physical essence of the light scattering picture has been improved. This work was published back in 1907. It is one of the first works on the theory of fluctuations, that is, microsoptic deviations of a particular physical quantity from the average value.

From the first years of scientific activity, Academician S.I. Vavilov became interested in the phenomenon of photoliminescence, the study of which he did not stop until the end of his life. Under the influence of light, certain substances themselves emit a characteristic glow, which is called liminescence. This glow continues after the irradiation stops. During liminescence, the absorption of exciting light occurs and the emission (with a certain time delay) of liminescence light, the wavelength of which is different from the wavelength of the absorbed light.

Over the past decades, people of non-existence have been widely used in various fields of science and technology. Based on it, new methods for chemical and varietal analysis of various substances have been developed - the so-called logistic analysis. In the development of these practical applications, the logistics industry belongs to S. I. Vavilov. But the work on the creation of logistical light sources, which opened a new stage in the history of lighting technology, is especially important.

The main source of light these days, overlay light bulbs, is very important. The efficiency of these lamps does not exceed 8%. A large 90% of the energy is lost or creating invisible infrared radiation. The spectral composition of visible light from these lamps differs significantly from the composition of sunlight, to which the human eye is most adapted. The temperature of the tungsten filament in the lamp was 2200-2300°C. To obtain light close to sunlight and increase light output, it would have to be raised to 6000°C. However, long before this, the light bulb filament will be scattered.

The disadvantages of electronic overlay lamps allow scientists to look for new, more economical and convenient light sources. Logistics lamps turned out to be such sources. Logistic substances are light transformers. They can transform one type of light into another, for example, invisible ultraviolet rays, that is, useless in lighting technology, into visible ones, or homogeneous light into wide spectral bands of the most varied states.

S.I. Vavilov was the first to propose powerful ultraviolet radiation from mercury lamps to produce visible light using logistic substances. Following this path, he created logistics lamps and fluorescent lamps.

The main part of Vavilov's logistic lamps is a gas-discharge tube filled with ruthium vapor at low pressure. Electronic passages through the tube during discharge excite ultraviolet radiation from mercury. If a discharge is observed through the walls of the tube, you will notice that its interior glows with a faint blue light. The main share of mercury radiation is concentrated in the ultraviolet region. To convert it into visible light, a layer of “light transformer”—crystalline logistics powder—is applied to the inner wall of the tube. By using various powders, one can obtain frontal light. Of greatest practical interest are powders whose candlelight is close to the daytime scattering of sunlight (for example, daylight under a cloudy sky). The efficiency and average lamp life are significantly longer than usual.

Let us now move on to work in the field of atomic nucleus physics.

Atomic nuclei include charged particles - protons, brought together to a distance of the order of 10⁻¹³ cm. It would seem that they should immediately collapse under the influence of the enormous power-plant repulsion of protons from each other. But we know that this does not happen. Only very heavy nuclei of elements at the end of the periodic table are unstable, and this leads to radioactive decay. What gives such incredible strength to atomic nuclei? Atomic nuclei are significant only because powerful nuclear forces act between all the particles contained in them.

The main idea on which modern ideas about the nature of nuclear forces are based was put forward in 1934 by academician I.E. Tamm. He was the first to understand that these forces can only be exchanged. By this time, the basic ideas about the quantum nature of the electromagnetic field had already been developed, according to which the interaction of two charged particles is carried out through quanta emitted and absorbed by these particles. Thus, interaction is the result of the exchange of intermediate particles created by the electromagnetic field.

I. E. Tamm proposed that not only electromagnetic, but also nuclear interaction is quantum in nature and is carried out through the exchange of some kind of intermediate particles. Assuming that nuclear particles exchange electrons, I. E. Tamm built the first mathematical theory of nuclear forces. However, the magnitudes of these forces turned out to be several orders of magnitude less than their actual value.

Soon after this, the Japanese physicist Yukawa showed that if the mass of the exchange particle is approximately 300 times greater than the mass of the electron, then I. E. Tamm’s theory well describes the peculiarity of nuclear forces. As a result, physicists have discovered particles responsible for the transfer of nuclear forces. They turned out to be the so-called pi mesons. The mass of pi-mesons and their properties correspond to the Tamm-Yukawa theory.

I. E. Tamm was the first to come to the seemingly paradoxical conclusion that the neutron should have its own magnetic moment, although this part has no electric charge. In 1934, he not only theoretically presented the existence of a magnetic moment in the neutron, but also correctly determined the sign of this moment.

The success of nuclear physics in the second half of the twentieth century is inextricably linked to charged particle accelerators. Accelerators make it possible to create new particle elements that are born at the moment of interaction of accelerated projectile particles with particles that are part of the projectiles. Moreover, the higher the energy of accelerated particles, the more diverse new elements, particles and antiparticles (mesons, hyperons, resonances) can be obtained with their help. Accelerators are also used to study the structure of particle elements (protons and neutrons). They also create super-heavy chemical elements that come after uranium in Mendleev’s periodic table (they are called transuranium) and atomic nuclei with an unusual ratio of the number of protons and neutrons.

Currently, the most common are cyclic accelerators, in which charged particles move along paths close to circles, accelerating many times in special accelerating devices. The first such accelerator was the cyclotron. However, the limit of energy that can be imparted to protons in a cyclotron is relatively small and amounts to several tens of millions of electron volts.

The creation of modern accelerators with tens and hundreds of billions of electron volts became possible thanks to the work of academician Vladimir Iosifovich Vekslar. In 1944, he proposed the important principle of “autophasing” of accelerated particles, which opened new horizons for nuclear physics and the physics of particle elements. This principle makes it possible to create new types of accelerators - phasotrons, synchrophasotrons.
