After the end of class at the evening math school, when the last students had already gone home, we saw a notebook forgotten by someone. It was a diary without the author's names. We found the entries interesting, and we decided to print an excerpt from the diary.

“...Goats are sneaky and eat everything they can reach. My friend cleverly took advantage of this circumstance. He lived in a village and knew mushroom places well. But when friends came and asked where to go for mushrooms tomorrow, he answered: “The goat will show.” In fact, by evening his goat was eating grass in an area shaped like an arrow, directed towards the mushroom spot itself (Fig. 1). I wondered how he did it. Yesterday I told my neighbor, a mathematician, about the smart goat, and this is what he answered me.

Mathematician. - It's not at all as difficult as you think. Let's figure it out from the very beginning. If you tie it to a peg with a ten-meter rope in a field, it will eat the grass, of course, in a circle with a radius of 10 meters. Let's try to tie it differently. At points A and B we will knock out the pegs and stretch the rope between them. We will fasten one end of the second rope to the “collar” of the goat, and at the other end we will make a loop that will slide along the first rope. Now the goat will be able to eat grass in an area consisting of a rectangle and two semicircles with centers at points A and B (Fig. 2). The distance from the point of the border of the “eaten out” section to the first rope is always the length of the second rope.

Me. - I’ll think about your tasks. I wonder if you completely came up with the “Hungry Goat Theory” yourself or did you use some mathematical concepts?

Mathematician. — I have already talked about such things as the distance from a point to a segment, the intersection of figures. But, in my opinion, the most necessary mathematical concept that was used is the concept of “neighborhood of a figure of radius R.”

Task 5. What is the area of ​​the neighborhood of radius $R$ of a convex polygon with perimeter $P$ and area $S$?

Ya. - We stopped talking about koeda.

Mathematician. - No, why? If we have wire, we can put a loop of rope of length $R$ on it. Then the koeda will eat up a neighborhood of the radius $R$ of the wire contour. We assume, of course, that the contour is welded to a single peg, so that it is motionless, and the loop slides freely along it.

Ya. - So, with the help of a wire, you can make the koeda eat the neighborhood of the radius $R$ of the circle and the border of the rectangle?

Mathematician. - Yes. In the vicinity of the cross the situation is more complicated.

Task 6. Think about what kind of wire circuit is needed to obtain the neighborhood of the cross.

Ya. - Indeed, the cross itself will not work as a contour - after all, the loop will not pass through the point $O$ (Fig. 6, a). We need to think about it... I wonder if in “Head Goat Theory” it is possible to use the neighborhood of the rectangle itself, and not its borders?

Mathematician. — Let’s build a low fence along the border of the rectangle so that the koeda can easily break through it. We will tie one end of a rope of length $R$ to the collar, and the other to the flyer - “yakoyu”, which can only move inside the rectangle. The koeda cannot drag him over the fence. Then the koeda will feed in the vicinity of the radius $R$ of the rectangle.

You may know that the difference $\Phi_1/\Phi_2$ of two figures $\Phi_1$ and $\Phi_2$ is the figure consisting of all those points $\Phi_1$ that do not lie in $\Phi_2$. For example, the difference between a rectangle with sides of 30 m and 40 m and the neighborhood of its border of radius 5 m will be a rectangle with sides of 20 m and 30 m (Fig. 7).

ME – In “The Head Goat Theory” it can be used like this. We will attach the dog to the rectangular wire contour $30\text{м} \times 40\text{м}$ with a five-meter chain. She will run around the 5 m radius neighborhood of the border of this rectangle and will not let the koeda go beyond the rectangle $20\text{м} \times 30\text{м}$.

Mathematician. - This is a tough task.

Task 7. How can one dog keep an untied Koeda in a semicircle?

The “head goat theory” can be developed in different directions. For example, you can use only pegs and ropes and require that all ropes be kept taut at all times. Then you can force the koeda to confine itself to the rectangle $ABCD$ like this: drive in pegs at the vertices of the rectangle, pull the ropes between $A$ and $B$ and between $C$ and $D$, throw a ring of rope over the ropes $AB$ and $CD$, pull it all the way and tie it Koedu to the ring. Indeed, for the points of a rectangle, the sum of the distances to two opposite sides is constant. Now you can make the koeda “eat” the triangle even under new restrictions...”
