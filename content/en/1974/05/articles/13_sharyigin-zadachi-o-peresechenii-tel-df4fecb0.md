Exercise
about crossing the body
I. F. Sharytsin

In this article, we will develop a solution to several rather difficult and, in our opinion, useful problems for calculating the volume of intersections of a body. The main difficulty of such problems is that solving them requires a well-developed imagination. In particular, when solving any problem involving the intersection of a body, it is very important to make a good drawing. Often the solution to a problem depends on the drawing. Before moving on to analyzing the problems, we give you the following conditions: after reading the conditions of a particular problem, do not immediately look at its solution, think for some time, try to make a drawing and solve the problem yourself. Then you will receive both satisfaction and the opportunity to compare your solution with the one proposed in the stat (it is quite possible that you manage to come up with a solution different from the one given in the stat). If Vashlia is not notified by the spell, then all the same, the time spent on thinking will add up and make the article easier to read.

Task 1. A cube with edge a contains three regular quadrangular rectangular edges of two rectangular faces of the cube. Find the volume of the portion of the cube belonging to all these gram prizes.

Since it is quite difficult to imagine, much less immediately select, a skilled body, we will proceed as follows. Let's first place one prism (Fig. 1) into the cube, then see what the second one “cuts off” from it, and, finally, what remains when we add the third prism.

Let the vertices of the second prism be the midpoints of the edges $AA_1, A_1D_1, D_1D, AD, BB_1, B_1C_1, C_1C$ and CB (the bases of this prism lie in the “front” and “back” faces of the cube). The edge of the second prism, connecting the midpoints of the edges $DD_1$ and $CC_1$ of the cube, intersects the segment $NN_1$ at its midpoint. This means that the face of the second prism, passing through the points $K_1M_1$ and the midpoints of the edges $DD_1$ and $CC_1$, intersect with the face $KK_1N_1N$ along the straight line $K_1P$, and this entire face of the second prism will cut off the prism $K_1M_1PN_1$ from the first.

Problem 2. Given two equal regular triangular paramidas of volume $V$, located symmetrically relative to the point $O$. Find the volume of the common part of these two paramidas if the point $O$ lies at the height of one of them and divides this height in a relative ratio of 2:1, counting from the paramid.

In this task, it is extremely important, although quite difficult, to make a correct, good, well-founded drawing. Let $SABC$ be the first paramid. The base $A_1B_1C_1$ of the second paramid intersects the height $SH_1$ at the point $H$, dividing it in a relative ratio of 1:2 (counting from the vertex $S$), and at the intersection with the paramid $SABC$ gives a triangle $MNP$, similar to the triangle $ABC$, $MN = \frac{1}{3} AB$.

From here it is easy to deduce (check!) that the triangle $MNP$ is entirely inside the triangle $A_1B_1C_1$. Similarly, inside the base $ABC$ there will be a triangle $M_1N_1P_1$, which is a section of the second paramid by the plane of the triangle $ABC$.

Problem 3. Given a cube with edge α. The second cube is obtained from this one by repeating around its diagonal angle α ($\alpha \leq 60^\circ$). Find the object volume of these cubes.

In this task, it is difficult to imagine and depict the intersection of a body as a whole; it is easier to understand how individual elements of one body intersect with elements of another body.

Let us denote the vertices of this cube by the letters A, B, C, D, A₁, B₁, C₁, D₁ (see Fig. 5), and the empty space is rotated around the diagonal B₁D counterclockwise. Let's see which pieces of the original cube can be separated when it is rotated. The vertices of the cube are located farthest from the center of the cube O - the middle of the diagon B₁D, which remains in place. Therefore, when turning inside the cube, the vertices cannot leave; on the contrary, they end up outside the cube and pieces of the cube will be surrounded near the vertices. Further, only faces can put pieces of a cube together. Therefore, it is enough to find which piece of the cube surrounds each face.

Problem 4. Given a cube $ABCDA_1B_1C_1D_1$ with edge $a$. The midpoints of its two opposite edges $AB$ and $C_1D_1$ serve as the midpoint of two crossing edges of a regular metrahedron, and on one of these edges lies the corresponding edge of the cube. Find the volume of the cube and metrahedron.

Let the point $K$—the midpoint $AB$—be the midpoint of the edge $PQ$ of a regular metrahedron, and $M$—the midpoint of $D_1C_1$ and at the same time the midpoint of the edge $RS$ of the metrahedron ($D_1C_1$ lies on $RS$ (Fig. 7)).

If the edge of a metrahedron is equal to $b$, then the distance between the midpoints of its crossing edges is easily calculated, it is equal to $b\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$ (check it!). On the other hand, $MK = a\sqrt{2}$. So, $b = 2a$.
