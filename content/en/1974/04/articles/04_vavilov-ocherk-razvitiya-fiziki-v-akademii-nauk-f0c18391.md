ESSAY
DEVELOPMENTS OF PHYSICS
AT THE ACADEMY OF SCIENCES

S. I. Vavilov

The outstanding Soviet physicist Academician Sergei Ivanovich Vavilov, who was Chairman of the USSR Academy of Sciences from 1945 to 1951, paid a lot of attention to issues of the history of physics. Among his works there is an article entitled “Essay on the development of physics in the Academy of Sciences over 220 years.” It was published in 1945, for the anniversary of the Academy, in “Essays on the History of the Academy of Sciences,” published by the Publishing House of the USSR Academy of Sciences. In this article, S.I. Vavilov characterized the contribution of Academy members to the development of Russian physics. Minor editorial additions to the article are given in square brackets.

“... when gathering the Academy in Russia, where there were still no universities or even secondary schools, Peter undoubtedly understood that it could not be copies of the Paris Academy of Sciences and the Royal Society of London. The Russian Academy could not be the pinnacle of the scientific paradise, as in France and England, due to the absence of this paradise itself. The task of the new Academy was, first of all, to create it. For this reason, an Academy of a very special kind arose on the banks of the Neva. Along with the scientific meetings at which... Bernoulli, Euler and others made reports on the most pressing issues of European science, all academicians were engaged in university and hypnastic teaching and, above all, were professors. In addition, academicians were entrusted with more difficult work on the foreign study of Russia, its nature, history, peoples, language; Academicians were constantly involved in technical expertise and organization of production. This must be kept in mind when considering the first period of physics at the Academy until the very end of the 18th century. The number of people involved in the development of physics in the Academy of Sciences throughout the 18th century is very small; These are academicians who officially occupy the department of physics or actually worked in this field, a small technical staff of mechanics, optics and individual inventors, like the famous Kulibin.

For a long time, until recently, the scientific life of the Academy was entirely determined by the personnel of its members. Therefore, naturally, our essay is based on the commented list of members of the Academy...

Daniel Bernoulli (1700-1782) ... in his famous treatise on hydrodynamics (which he wrote in St. Petersburg) and a long series of memoirs on mechanics and acoustics, showed himself to be one of the most notable representatives of mathematical physics. He spent 8 years in St. Petersburg (1725-1733), but until the end of his life he maintained a technical connection with the Academy...

Johann Leitmann (1667-1736) ... took the department of mechanics and optics at the Academy, and he can rightfully be called the father of optical engineering and precision mechanics in Russia. He was known to Peter I as the author of books on watches and glass grinding, and Peter sought to invite him to Russia even before the founding of the Academy. Arriving in St. Petersburg in 1726, he soon set about organizing work on precision mechanics and optics... There are sufficient reasons to think that special attention and success in the practical fields of optics in St. Petersburg in the 18th century in the works of Lomonosov, Kulibin, Epinus, Euler and others were largely determined by the work of Academician Leitman.

The great Leonhard Euler (1707-1783) ... was at the St. Petersburg Academy first a professor of physiology, then physics and, finally, mathematics ... Together with Peter I and Lomonosy, Euler became the good genius of our Academy, determining its glory, its strength, its productivity. Euler's physics, of course, fades in the rays of his mathematical glory, but in itself it is very impressive and deserves much more attention than it has received so far. Despite the fatal errors of Euler's wave optics, it was she who prepared Fresnel optics. Euler's dioptric follants*), written in St. Petersburg, marked a turning point in the development of geometric optics. His “Letters on Certain Physical and Physiphysical Matters,” written to a certain German princesce, is an excellent popularization and complete originality of the idea of ​​an encyclopedia of physics of the 18th century. Based on the excellent Russian translation of the “Letters” made by Euler’s student Academician Rumovsky, many generations of Russian people studied physics.

Georg Richmann (1711-1753) ... imprinted his name in the history of physics as an experimenter who almost died at his post in the name of science ... He dealt with issues of steam formation, the influence of pumps and machines for changing water, as well as other various natural and technical phenomena and problems. Richmann moved on to electrical experiments long before Franklin’s famous publication... The news of Franklin’s experiments in 1752 made the same impression throughout the world as Galileo’s first observations with a telephone did in their time, and at the end of the 19th century, Roentgen’s experiments. Everywhere, including St. Petersburg, they eagerly began to repeat them. Richman began observing thunderstorms and atmospheric electric power with great enthusiasm at his home, on the corner of the 5th line and Bolshoy Prospekt of Vasilievsky Island. Well known

Mihanl Vasilyevich Lomonosov (1711-1765)… In all areas of science and “art” that were dealt with by the St. Petersburg Academy in the 18th century (with the exception of mathematics), Lomonosov was undoubtedly the most substituted and most original representative. For the Russian state, Lomonosov became a questionable proof of the talents, inclinations and skills of the Russian people in the field of science and culture... It is known that Lomonosov considered chemistry to be his main “profession”, but in chemistry Lomonosov was primarily a great physicist-chemist. At the same time, in the field of pure physics, Lomonosov showed himself with amazing breadth and originality. Deserving Lomonosov in physics begins with creating the necessary conditions for its development in Russia...

In the second edition [translated by M.V. Lomonosov] of “Wolfan Experimental Physics”, in 1760, Lomonosov included “Additions to Experimental Physics”, containing brief references to some results in the field of physics obtained by himself. This is, first of all, his kinetic theory of heat, or, as he says, “my system of thermal motion,” one of the substituted representatives of the kinetic theory; further, his original and, despite the fallacy, witty and profound “new theory of colors”, in which for the first time a concrete attempt is made to connect the doctrine of light and matter; finally, his theory of atmospheric electricity...

The publication of Lomonosov's scientific manuscripts, as well as the study of his forgotten printed scientific materials, revealed Lomonosov to us as a substitute optician, inventor and designer of new telescopes, microsops, photometers, periscopes, who built them in a laboratory-workshop together with assistant craftsmen...

Many of Lomonosov's notes and aphorisms concerning the laws of conservation of motion and matter, the theory of atoms and ether, the question of the correspondence of heavy and inert mass...

Franz Epinius (1724-1802) appeared at the Academy as a representative of a new generation and a significant new direction in physics. For this generation, the Vyotonic method of solving problems of accepting and repelling forces becomes indisputable, although until recently for Bülfingar, Euler, Lomonosov it was completive. Stating from the very beginning in his famous treatise “An Experience in the Theory of Electricity and Magnetism” (Petersburg, 1755): “I consider it an undoubted axiom that a body cannot act where it does not exist,” Epinius actually uses the Vyotonian scheme in his theory of electrostatic interactions and induction. The name of Epinius is also associated with other important physical results. He was responsible for the discovery of the pyroelectric properties of tourmaline, the implementation of the first achromatic microsop *), built in St. Petersburg and for a long time stored in the physics room of the Academy of Sciences...

A quick look at the activities of [non-born] academic physicists gives a fairly clear picture of the state and development of physics at the St. Petersburg Academy in the 18th century. In terms of quality, this physics ranked one...

Vasily Vladimirovich Petrov (1761-1834)...
In 1795, V.V. Petrov became an extraordinary professor at the newly formed Medical-Surgical Academy. Here he assembled a model physics office for his own, where his main experimental work was carried out. They are collected in three books:
1) “Collected physical and chemical new experiments and observations”, 1801;
2) “News about Galvania - Voltaic experiments”, 1803; 3) “New Electrical Experiments”, 1804. In these volumes we have a very large amount of experimental material... on elucidating the nature of various conditions of luminescence*), on significant experiments with the voltage arc, discoveries by Petrov, and on electrostatics. Unlike Lomonosov, Petrov was not at all inclined to broad generalizations; he was an empiricist who, however, constructed his experiments in a very rational and thoughtful manner. Among Russian scientists and foreigners working in Russia at that time, V.V. Petrov was undoubtedly a major phenomenon...

E. H. Lenz

Emilius Khristanovich Lenz (1804-1865) ... firmly inscribed his name in the history of electromagnetism along with Ohm and Joule and became the founder of a branch of Russian physics that focused on electromagnetism and the development of precision changes in this area.

[Among Lenz’s numerous scientific works, two are especially famous: “On determining the direction of galvanic currents excited by electrodynamic induction” and “On the laws of heat release by galvanic current.” In the first of them, he established a rule determining the direction of induced currents (the famous Lenz rule), in the second he discovered the law of the thermal action of current (Joule-Lenz law).]

Jacobi was one of the most notable representatives of that new phase in the history of physics, when its results immediately, in the form of the most important factor, passed into technology, electromagnetism turned into electrical engineering. Physicists Jacobi and Lenz in 1839 sailed along the Neva on a motor boat built by Immi with two galleys, which developed one horsepower from 64 Grove elements. In the physics laboratory, various new telegraph apparatus were built and studied, and galvanoplasty was developed in various forms, the replaced samples of which serve as decoration for the Physics Institute of the Academy of Sciences. Physicist Jacobi, together with Lenz, designs a lightning rod for a powder magazine and is the inventor of electric mine fuses.

The name of Academician Jacobi, an outstanding physicist, a brilliant electrical engineer and inventor, should rightfully be placed along with other leading names of academicians in physics - Euler, Lomonosov, Petrov...

Genrikh Ivanovich Wild (1833-1902) - organizer of the Tsarist and Russian meteorological network, author of futometric and polarization instruments, an outstanding researcher of terrestrial magnetism. Russia G.I. Wild completed the 27 best creative years of his life. The Russian meteorological network before Wild's work consisted of only 31 stations; when he left, it had 650 stations equipped with new instruments...

Boris Borisovich Goltsyn (1862-1916)… Goltsyn’s scientific work first proceeded in various directions (critical states of matter, X-rays, spectroscopy, physiological optics). The optical works of V.V. Goltsyn are very interesting and important... Among them, the experimental proof of the spectroscopic Dopper phenomenon *), which continues and highly refines the experiments of A. A. Belopolsky, was especially important. At the beginning of the new century, Goltsyn's interests concentrated on a new geophysical discipline - seismology, of which Goltsyn can rightfully be considered one of the founders. Goltsyn gave the first experiments, developed equipment, organized a fairly extensive network of seismological stations...
