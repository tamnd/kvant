ROTATING EARTH

Sometimes you can come across a statement that, due to the fact that the Earth rotates around its axis, the softness force acting on a body differs in different places on the Earth’s surface. They also say that body weight varies in different places on Earth. Both this and other connections are due to the fact that in different places on the rotating Earth the acceleration of the free fall of the body is different. Is all this true? In this article we will try to answer this question.

1. Mechanical motion, as we know, is relative. This means that if we use different reporting systems, then relative to these systems the movement of the same body turns out to be different. If, for example, we associate a coordinate system with an airplane flying at a constant speed in the horizontal direction, then in this coordinate system the cargo dropped (or rather, fallen) from the airplane moves down along a vertical straight line (Fig. 1). But in a coordinate system associated with some point on the Earth’s surface, for example, with point O (see Fig. 2), the same load moves along a curve called a parabola. Consequently, the trajectories of movement are different. The speeds of movement are also different. 5. Now let us discuss the question of the force of gravity acting on the rotating Earth, and the weight of bodies on it.

The force of gravity applied to any body of mass $m$ on the surface of the Earth is the force of universal gravity between this body and the Earth. The absolute value of this force is equal to

$$F = \gamma \frac{mM}{R^2},$$

and it is directed towards the center of the Earth. It is clear that the rotation of the Earth cannot influence this force in any way.

According to Newton's second law, this force is equal to the product of the mass of the body and the acceleration imparted by this force:

$$\gamma \frac{mM}{R^2} = mg.$$

Hence,

$$g = \gamma \frac{M}{R^2}.$$

Acceleration $g$, included in the expression for the forces of gravity, is the acceleration in the “stationary”, or, as they say, in the inertial reporting system. As is known, Newton’s laws of motion, which we used when writing formula (5), are true only for such systems. The situation is different with the quantity called body weight.

The weight of a body is the force exerted by the body on the support on which it is placed or on the suspension from which it is suspended. This force can be any, depending on the acceleration with respect to the fixed coordinate system of motion of the support or suspension together with the body.

If a support or suspension is at rest relative to a fixed coordinate system or moves relative to it without acceleration, then the weight of the body is simply equal to the forces of gravity acting on it.

But the scales, with the help of which the weight of a body is measured anywhere on the Earth, except for stripes, move with acceleration (due to the rotation of the Earth). Therefore, apart from the stripes, my weight is not equal to the strength of my softness. The weight of the body $P$, changed by the scales moving with acceleration $a$ relative to the stationary Earth, is equal to

$$P = m(g - a).$$

In our case, the role of acceleration $a$ is played by acceleration $\omega^2 R \cos \varphi$. That's why

$$P = m(g - \omega^2 R \cos \varphi).$$

Since the acceleration $\omega^2 R \cos \varphi$ is different at different spines, the body weight changed at different spines is also different. The lowest body weight will be at the equator. However, the magnitude of the body's weight at the equator and the total difference is as small as the magnitude of the acceleration of gravity changed at these points.

We see, therefore, that when we use a “fixed”, that is, inertial coordinate system, then for the weight of the body we can write the expression

$$P = mg.$$

But when we use a non-perinary coordinate system, that is, a coordinate system moving with acceleration, then Newton’s second law cannot be used. Therefore, it is impossible to use the formula $P = mg'$, which is obtained from Newton’s second law. However, we wrote formulas (6) and (6a), which just mean that $P = mg'$, where $g'$ is the acceleration relative to the non-perinary coordinate system. But this is “illegal”, isn’t it? Yes, it's illegal. But why “illegal” formulas are also written will be discussed in another article.
