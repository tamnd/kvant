3. We considered the case of a free fall of a body at the pole and at the equator of the Earth. Let us now consider the free fall of a body in an arbitrary place (somewhere between the equator and the stripes of the Earth).

From figure 5 it is clear that now the centripetal acceleration associated with the rotation of the Earth is directed at the equator, and towards that point on the axis of rotation of the Earth, which is the center of the corresponding parallel circle. (The radius of this circle at figure 5 is denoted by the letter r.) Repeating the above reasoning, we come to the conclusion that the acceleration of gravity $g'$, changed at any place, will be less than the acceleration $g$, changed at the pole. It is necessary to take into account the centripetal acceleration of the corresponding point on the earth's surface. But now the acceleration directions $g$ and $\omega^2 r$ included in formula (4) form an angle $\varphi$, which is the latitude of the point at which the acceleration $g'$ changes. Using the rule of vector addition, we will find that the vector $g'$ is no longer directed towards the center of the Earth, but is slightly deviated towards the equator (see Fig. 5).

It is clear that the acceleration $\omega^2 r$ at latitude $\varphi$ is less than the acceleration $\omega^2 R$ at the equator. It is easy to see that at any latitude $\varphi$ the ratio $r/R$

4. Let's now see how numerically the value $g'$ for a given latitude $\varphi$ differs from $g$. To do this, obviously, you only need to calculate the values ​​of $\omega^2 R$.

The angular velocity $\omega$ of the Earth’s rotation is small, since the period of the Earth’s rotation is equal to one day, that is, 86164 s. Hence,

$$\omega = \frac{2\pi}{86164} = 7.3 \cdot 10^{-5} \text{ рад/с.}$$

The radius of the Earth at the equator is 6378 km, or $\approx 6,38 \cdot 10^6$ m. Thus, at the equator ($\varphi = 0$)

$$\omega^2 R = (7.3 \cdot 10^{-5})^2 \cdot 6.38 \cdot 10^6 = 3.4 \cdot 10^{-2} \text{ м}^2.$$

The acceleration of free fall at the equator differs by this column from the acceleration that would be observed if the Earth did not perform its daily movement. In any other place this difference is even smaller. The difference is so small that it would not be worth writing an article about it if it were not for the fundamental importance of the very question of the movement of bodies relative to the acceleration of the movement of the coordinate system.
