A gymnasium and a university were initially organized at the Academy, since at that time there were no other sources from which the Academy could receive scientifically trained young people. The student population was determined to be 30 people, and a curriculum for their training was developed. At the same time, specially supported “... to ensure that one student is not suddenly burdened with many lectures.” In order to have a real opportunity to maintain the number of university students, a gymnasium was established, which ordered 20 people “to be kept at the end of the academic year and those who are fit to be promoted to students, and those who are unfit to be sent to the Academy of Arts.”

The Academy was founded, but there were no scientists in Russia. That is why the first academicians were invited from outside the country. A total of 23 academicians were invited. Their choice was extremely successful; the vast majority of them energetically began organizing work and scientific research. And very soon the Academy had an excellent chemical laboratorium for its time, an astronomical observatory, a physics office, mechanical and optical workshops, a printing house, a library and other scientific institutions.

From the first days of the Academy's existence, mathematics played a significant role in it. Among the 23 invited academicians, seven were mathematicians. The names of many of them are firmly entrenched in the history of mathematics: Nikolai and Daniil Bernoulli, Leonard Euler, Christan Goldbach.

Peter I did not come to the celebration of the opening of the Academy: its first state was only in August 1725, six months after his death. But the beginning of the work was quite successful: meetings were held regularly, an extensive study of the country and its natural resources began, a systematic study of the most important natural phenomena was carried out, and first-class mathematical research was carried out. Beginning in 1728, the Academy began to publish the scientific journal “Comments of the St. Petersburg Academy”, and six years after that, Daniel Bernoulli, by that time had returned to Switzerland, wrote L. Euler: “I cannot oblige you enough with what greed the St. Petersburg thematics are copied in court... It is desirable that their printing be accelerated.” We were talking about mathematical works that were published in this first Russian scientific journal. Thus, already at the dawn of its existence, the Academy turned into one of the largest scientific centers in the world where mathematical research was carried out.

D. Bernoulli (1700-1782) worked in Russia for only a few years, but even during this period he left a noticeable mark on the history of Russian science. It is to him that the Academy owes the invitation to L. Euler to its membership. In the publications of the Academy, D. Bernoulli published a number of works that laid the foundation for such important areas of mathematical research as mathematical hydrodynamics, mathematical theory of oscillations, mechanics of gases, as well as the theory of trigonometric series. The theory of trigonometric series a hundred years later was developed in detail by the French mathematician Fourier in connection with the development of the mathematical theory of tell propagation. However, trigonometric series were first used to solve problems in mechanics and mathematical physics by D. Bernoulli.
