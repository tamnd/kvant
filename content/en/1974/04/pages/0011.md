The famous ancient Greek geometer proved the fact that there are infinitely many prime numbers, and Chebyshev discovered a remarkable asymptotic pattern of their distribution among all positive integers. We present this result in the formulation that was published by Chebyshev in his second article “On Prime Numbers”: Let us denote by π (x) the number of prime numbers smaller in magnitude than x. Then for all sufficiently large x the following inequalities hold:

$$0,92149 \leq \pi(x) : \frac{x}{\ln x} \leq 1,10555,$$

food ln x means logarithm to base e = 2.7128. . . numbers x.

Chebyshev further proved that if as x → ∞ the ratio π(x) : x/ln x tends to a certain limit, then this limit can only be the number 1.

The proof of the existence of this limit required more cumbersome and complex reasoning and appeared only fifty years after Chebyshev’s discovery. This proof was obtained simultaneously by two mathematicians: the French scientist J. Hadamard and the Belgian scientist C. J. de la Vallée Poussin. Chebyshev's method is quite elementary and, one must think, its possibilities are far from being exhausted.

The second direction of mathematical research, in which Chebyshev left a bright mark, is associated with theoretical probability. Here he pointed to a new important object of research and developed simple and at the same time very powerful research methods. First of all, we must point out the law of large numbers in the form of Chebyshev, which is still presented unchanged in all textbooks of probability theory, and at the same time its proof is given in the form proposed by him. This result of Chebyshev gives weight to the general conditions under which the total effect of a large number of random ingredients turns out to be almost constant. The significance of Chebyshev's discovery of the law is not only of general theoretical interest, but also has great practical significance.

The third direction of research, in which Chebyshev originated the very idea of ​​its origin, is associated with the so-called best approximation of a function. This area of ​​mathematical thought, which is entirely the invention of Chebyshev in its foundations, has today turned into a part of mathematics rich in results and connections with other branches of knowledge. The starting point for constructing this theory is a purely practical problem that arose for Chebyshev in connection with an order from the faculty of St. Petersburg University to prepare and teach a course in practical mechanics.

Chebyshev was faced with the question of reducing possible deviations of the movement of the rod from rectilinear in the so-called Watt parallelogram, and this purely practical question led him to the formulation of a number of new mathematical problems. The simplest of them is widely known: the environments of all polynomials of degree n

$$P_n(x) = x^n + a_1x^{n-1} + \dots + a_n$$

find a volume for which max |P_n(x)| at |x| ≤ 1 has the minimum value.

The answer was found by P. L. Chebyshev back in 1859; It turned out that the required polynomial (it is called the Chebyshev polynomial) has the following form:

$$P_n(x) = \frac{1}{2^{n-1}} \cos(n \arccos x).$$
