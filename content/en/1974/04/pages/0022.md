Newton and the law of universal gravitation, the absolute value of this acceleration is equal to

$$g = \gamma \frac{M}{R^2},$$

where $\gamma$ is the gravitational constant, $M$ is the mass of the Earth and $R$ is its radius. This acceleration is directed along the polar radius towards the center of the Earth. The value $g = \gamma \frac{M}{R^2}$ is the value of the acceleration of free radium relative to a stationary (inertial) reference frame.

And what value of the acceleration of the falling body will be obtained when changing at the equator? For these changes, here too you need to install a ruler with a scale (Fig. 4). Here too we will direct the coordinate axis to the center of the Earth along the equatorial radius. Of course, at the equator the absolute value of the acceleration caused by the Earth's gravity will be the same as at the pole. The rotation of the Earth does not affect the force with which it attracts bodies *). But the change in acceleration at the equator is made relative to the reference system, which itself moves with acceleration relative to the chosen one

$$g = g' + \omega^2 R$$

(the acceleration of a free falling body relative to a fixed coordinate system ($g$) is equal to the sum of the acceleration of the body relative to the moving system ($g'$) and the acceleration of the moving system itself relative to the fixed one ($\omega^2 R$)).

In other words, the acceleration $g'$ of free fall, changed at the equator, that is, the relative acceleration of the moving reference frame, is not equal to the acceleration $g$, the change at the pole. It is less than $g$ by amounts $\omega^2 R$:

$$g' = g - \omega^2 R.$$
