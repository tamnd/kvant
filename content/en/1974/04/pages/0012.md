⟦folio 10⟧

A. M. Lyapunov

Exercise. Write explicitly the Chebyshev polynomials for $n = 1, 2, 3$ and 4.

Let us dwell on the results of two outstanding students of P. L. Chebyshev - A. M. Lyapunov (1857-1918) and A. A. Markov (1856-1922).

A. M. Lyapunov, while still at St. Petersburg University, received from P. L. Chebyshev the topic of independent work, which he successfully completed. This topic was related to one of the most difficult questions in mechanics: the stability of shape of liquid rotating masses. It was further developed in his master's thesis.

After successfully defending his dissertation, A. M. Lyapunov was invited to teach at Kharkov University. Naturally, the first years of teaching required Lyapunov to spend a lot of time on preparation, especially since he had to teach a number of mathematical disciplines and theoretical mechanics. But this preparation and the subsequent need to comprehend the material for presentation led to the formulation of new scientific questions that required careful research. At Kharkov University, Lyapunov took up such a new direction in mathematics, which later received the name of the theory of motion stability.

There are numerous problems of theory and practice to study the behavior of solving systems of differential equations with an unlimited increase in the argument of the property. Lyapunov developed effective methods for their research. At that time, Lyapunov’s work was of interest primarily for problems of celestial mechanics (issues of stability of the solar system, etc.). However, starting from the thirties of our century, the names and methods of Lyapunov received non-presidential practical significance for the task of advice, radio engineering, mechanical engineering, etc. Lyapunov laid the foundations of the theory of motion stability, developed research methods and examined the most important parts of the cases. These ideas were developed by him in a number of works and finally in 1892 in his doctoral dissertation “The General Problem of Stability of Movement,” which brought him world fame.

In 1900, A. M. Lyapunov was elected a corresponding member of the Academy of Sciences, and a year later - its full member. After his election, Lyapunov moved to St. Petersburg, and, having given up teaching, devoted himself entirely to scientific research.

At the turn of the century, Lyapunov turned to questions of probability theory and studied one of the tasks of his teacher - finding the most general conditions under which the normalized sums of independent random variables with a large number of terms have an almost normal distribution. The theorem he proved was called the central limit theory of probability.
