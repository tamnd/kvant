Memories
Otto Dunkela

In 1957, the editors of The American Mathematical Monthly published a special (additional) issue: “The Otto Dunkel Memorial Problem Book.”
This issue was dedicated to the memory of Professor Otto Dankela (1869-1951), who for years headed the “Problems Department” of this journal.

The issue dedicated to Dunkel contained "400 "Best" Problems" drawn from the 2,704 submitted by the magazine's readers between 1918 and 1950. Among the correspondents were students, teachers of secondary and higher schools, as well as well-known scientists in America and abroad (G. Duedeni, G. S. M. Coxeter, O. Ore, G. Polya, C. Trigg; many tasks of O. Dunkel himself).

The selection of the “Best” problems was entrusted to twenty-four REM mathematicians, who for many years actively collaborated in the “Problems Department” of the Monthly. Each of them presented their own list of “Best” problems. Based on these lists, the final selection was made, in which the main role was played by the “number of votes” received by a particular task.

This year, the publishing house “Mir” published a book in the series “Problems and Olympiads”*)

This part provides the necessary information from the theory and comments on individual solutions.

Compared to books already published in the same series, the collection “Selected Problems” is more difficult. The majority of the problems are intended not for schoolchildren, but for first-year university students. These problems can be used for student Olympiads, which have come to an end in recent years and have begun to gain popularity, but are still very poorly supplied with literature.

Almost all the problems in the collection can be said to be simple. But fun is not the only quality of the selected tasks. The vast majority of problems involve issues of modern and classical mathematics, the solution of which requires, along with wit and ingenuity, the ability to freely use a variety of tools in the mathematical arsenal. The collection, of course, can serve as a guide for mastering any branch of mathematics, although it contains a lot of problems in elementary geometry, number theory (delimity, diffants of equations), a large selection of problems in algebra and analysis (determinants, calculation of integrals, differential equations, series), and the projective geometry that has almost gone out of fashion is richly presented above. All these tasks can be used both to increase schoolchildren’s and students’ interest in the material being studied, and to train future “Olympians” and for the activities of organizing committees.

The more elementary problems in the collection are the last hundred problems (problems 301-400). Among them, a lover of “entertaining” mathematics will find a lot of interesting things for himself (problem 277 about flying around the world, problem 303 about the “black” spot, problem 312 about bank employees). In general, this unique collection in many respects will undoubtedly contribute to the development of creative abilities - after all, the solution to a non-standard problem is to undertake to improve in the issue under consideration and, in the end, not only to prove a “foreign” theorem, but also to discover one’s own.

3. Another property of the harmonic series (No. 304). Prove that the partial sum $\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{4} + \dots + \frac{1}{N}$ of a harmonic series is not an integer for any N.

6. Cutting task (No. 326). How can a regular hexagon be cut into the smallest number of pieces that can be used to form an equilateral triangle of the same area? Each cut is made along a straight line.

7. Hamilton cycle on a polyhedron (No. 358). Let's assume that we want to be at the vertices of a polyhedron, but we can reach them only by moving along its edges. Hamilton solved the problem of finding all the vertices of a polyhedron along a route that does not pass through the same vertex twice. (In graph theory, such a route is usually called a Harmilton cycle.) It is not difficult to prove that Hamilton's proof problem has a solution. Prove that Hamilton's problem has no solution for the rhombodedecadron.

8. Determination of a fake coin using weighing (No. 359). One man had 12 coins, indistinguishable in appearance. The coin was fake and differed in weight from the real one. The man also had very sensitive balancing scales at hand, but without weights. Can he, with no more than three weighings, detect a counterfeit coin and determine whether it is heavier or lighter than a real one?

9. “Equilateral Birdmen” (No. 360). Let us call a set consisting of $n$ points on the plane an equilateral $n$-pointer if any three of them are located at the vertices of an equilateral triangle. Six points on a plane can be positioned so that they form an equilateral 6-point plane.

Prove that it is impossible to choose seven points on the plane that form an equilateral 7-point plane. What is the smallest number $k$ such that from any $k$ points in space we can choose three that are not located at the vertices of an equilateral triangle?
