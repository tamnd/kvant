⟦folio 54⟧

Ask - we answer

Dear editors
«Kvant»
In «Kvant» No. 6, 1977, in the article by I. Bashmakova “Isaac Newoton” there is an epigraph - a couplet:
“This world was shrouded in deep darkness.
Let there be light! And then Newton appeared.”
I remember that in some old book I read that on Newton’s grave, on the tombstone, there is a similar inscription, but in that book it was given in the original - in English. Is Marshak's translation a translation of this inscription? Are A. Pope's epigram and the inscription on Newton's grave identical?
If you can, please tell me the epifuro at Newton's grave.

I. I. Kornis, ushitel, Dzhambul

Dear comrade. I.I. Kornis!
You are interested in the question of the inscription on the grave of I. Newton and its connection with the couplet of A. Pope.
Newton was buried in Westminster Abbey in London and the epiphy on the grave monument reads:
“Here ends Sir Isaac Newton, the nobleman who, with an almost divine mind, was the first to prove with the torch of mathematics the motion of the planets, the paths of comets and the tides of the oceans.
He investigated the differences in light rays and the various properties of colors that appeared, which he had never suspected before. An applied, wise and faithful source of nature, antiquity and St. Scripture, he affirmed his philosophy of the majesty of the almighty God, and by law expressed the simplicity of the Gospel. Let mortals rejoice that such an adornment of the human race existed. Born December 25, 1642, died March 20, 1727.” (See S.I. Vavilov, Isaac Newoton, Publishing House of the USSR Academy of Sciences, M.-L., 1945, p. 216).
The statue of Newton, erected at Trinity College in 1755, bears the inscription from Lucretius Cara: “Qui genus humanum ingenio superavit” (i.e. “He was superior in intelligence to the human race”).
As you can see, neither one nor the other inscription has any connection with Pope’s couplet, which in the original sounds like this:
Nature and nature's laws lay hid in night.
God said: «Let Newton be!» And all was light.
These lines quickly became widely known. In his book “Isaac Newoton” S. I. Vavilov cites their rephasing made by M. V. Lomonosov:
Russia was covered in darkness for many years.
God of the rivers: “Let there be Peter!” And there will be light in Russia.

With respect, Professor I. Bashmakova
