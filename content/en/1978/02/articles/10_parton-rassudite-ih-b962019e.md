The “mathematical” name for a surface is a hyperbolic paraboloid.

Using (1), it is not difficult to show that the same hyperbolic paraboloid corresponds to more than one pair of skew lines!

For any lines $\begin{cases} y = kx \\ z = c \end{cases}$ and $\begin{cases} y = -kx \\ z = -c \end{cases}$ for which $k$ and $c$ are dependent
$$p = \frac{c(1 + k^2)}{2k},$$

where $p$ is a given constant, we have the same hyperbolic parabolide $xy = 2pz$. Therefore, two pairs of such lines lie in parallel planes $z = c, z = -c$. If we take into account that $k = y/x$ and $c = z$, then substituting them into (2), we obtain that all lines $m$ and $n$ form surfaces with the equation $z(x^2 + y^2) = -2pxy$.

The planes $xOz$ and $yOz$ are called the direction planes of the surface $F$: "half". The molar generatrices are parallel to the $xOz$ plane, and the other “half” is parallel to the $yOz$ plane. In our case, these planes are mutually perpendicular and the hyperbolic parabola is called equilateral. In the general case, these planes form an arbitrary angle. True, in the general case, a hyperbolic parabold cannot be obtained by combining the axes of rotation, however, the construction given in paragraph 4 is applied to it. Only on lines $O_1A$ and $O_2B$ it is necessary to connect points $A_1$ and $B_1$ such that $|O_1A| = \lambda |O_2B|$, where $\lambda \neq 1$.

Application. Proof of the lemmas

Note that a rotation with axis $l$ can be obtained by the composition of two planes symmetric with respect to the plane passing through the straight line $l$. Indeed, let $R_l(A) = A_1$. Let's take an arbitrary plane $\sigma \ni l$. Then $S_\sigma(A) = A'$. Let us now draw the plane $\pi$ through the line $l$ and the midpoint of the segment $[A'A_1]$. It's easy to report that $(A'A_1) \perp \pi$. Therefore $S_\pi \circ S_\sigma(A) = A_1$. On the other hand, $S_\pi \circ S_\sigma$ is a rotation, since this transformation leaves only the points of the line $l$ in place. But the rotation is uniquely specified by the axis and the images of an arbitrary point that does not lie on the axis. Therefore, $R_l = S_\pi \circ S_\sigma$.

1. Let the axes $u$ and $v$ intersect at point $O$. Consider the plane $\pi$ containing these lines. Then $R_u$ and $R_v$ can be represented as follows: $R_u = S_\pi \circ S_\sigma, R_v = S_\sigma \circ S_\pi$. Hence $R_v \circ R_u = (S_\sigma \circ S_\pi \circ S_\pi \circ S_\sigma)$ = $S_\sigma \circ S_\sigma$, since $S_\pi \circ S_\pi$ is the same transformation. But $\sigma_2 \ni O$ and $\sigma_1 \ni O$, so the line $\sigma_1 \cap \sigma_2$ passes through the point $O$.

2. Let $m$ be a line and $R_l(m) = m$. If a point is $A \in m$ and $A \notin l$, then $m$ passes through $A$ and $R_l(A)$. This means that $m$ belongs to some plane perpendicular to $l$, and we have arrived at a rotation of the plane, for which the statement is obvious.

3. It is proved in the same way as Lemma 1 that a plane can be drawn through $u$ and $v$. Parallel transfers will be obtained if $\sigma_1 \parallel \sigma_2$.

Judge them

While performing laboratory work, students received on the screen the color spectrum of white light passing through a prism.

- What a beauty! - Ira exclaimed. - And if the retina of the eye is in place of the screen, then what will we see? she asked. - Like what? - answered Masha. - Same as on the screen. Reflected colored rays from the spectrum onto the screen, as well as light rays from various objects, passing through the lens of the eye, cause the image to turn upside down on the retina. And the human brain “learned” to flip the images that appeared on the retina. Therefore, we see everything in real form. So the eye will see the general arrangement of colored rays in the spectrum of white light. “And I think that we see a spectrum “reverse” to what is visible on the screen,” said Ira.

In your opinion, reader, which of them is right?
