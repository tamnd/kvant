Dispute
about the frog

The main characters in this story are the Italian scientists Luigi Galvani and Alexa Ro Volta. The time of action is the last decade of the 18th century. The theme of the story is one of the most important discoveries in physics in the entire history of its development. It primarily led to the fact that the next, 19th century became not only the century of steam, but also the century of electricity.

This story began with the fact that in 1791, a professor of anatomy and medicine at the University of Bologna, Luigi Galvani (1737-1798), published his “Treatise on the forces of electricity during muscular movement.” Along with many other readers, another professor, professor of physics at the University of Pavni Alexa Volta (1745-1827), became acquainted with the treatise. A dispute arose between the two professors, which lasted several years, the result of which was the mentioned important discovery. Further, not one, but at least three. An important role both in this dispute and in the discoveries made was played by ... frogs.

Electricity Science
at the end of the 18th century

At the time in question, researchers had rather vague ideas about the nature of electricity, and none of them were generally acceptable or convincingly substantiated. More consistent than others were the views of the American scientist and statesman Benjamin Franklin. He believed that “electricity” is a special liquid (fluid) contained in each body in a certain, “normal” amount for that body. The process of electrifying bodies consists

is that part of this fluid from one body is transferred to another. Franklin proposed to consider the body to which the electric fluid is transferred and which thus has an excess of this fluid as positively charged, and the other body that has consumed part of the electric fluid as negatively charged. Franklin attributes a number of properties to the electric fluid, which, in his opinion, explained the electrical phenomenon. In particular, he noted that individual parts of the electrical fluid repel each other, but in general the electrical materia is very strongly attached to ordinary matter.

By the time in question, devices were invented and widely used by researchers to store electricity - electrostatic machines and capacitors in the form of so-called Leyden jars. It was found that one body does not allow electrical fluid to pass through itself at all, while others pass it through with varying degrees of “compliance” (that is, resistance).

Finally, the characteristics of the phenomenon accompanying an electric discharge, that is, the transition of an electric fluid from one body to another or to the Earth, were known. This is, firstly, a glow in the form of an electric spark, which can be extracted from a charged body, and it has been convincingly proven that lightning is also an electric spark of hail sizes. Secondly, when a discharge passes through the bodies of animals or humans, a sharp contraction of the muscle occurs, causing a shocking shock, sometimes very strong and life-threatening. Both of these phenomena were considered reliable indicators of the electrical nature of the processes in which they are observed.

Let us add that by this time the characteristics of fish (rays, eels, catfish) capable of creating electrical discharges that caused exactly the same shocks in the victim as discharges from Leyden jars and electric machines had been studied and described.

A frog appears on the stage

This was the situation in which the science of electricity was when the above-mentioned treatise by Luigi Galvani was published in 1791, which described the experiments he conducted in 1780-1790.

Galvani's observations were unusually difficult to understand. The first of them was as follows.

Working with a prearrated limb of a frog, Galvani and his assistants (one of them, according to some sources, was his wife, and it was she who made the decisive observation) noticed that if they touched the frog's femoral nerve with a scalpel (thereby grounding it) at the moment when a spark was removed from the electrical machine in the room, the frog's muscles began to contract sharply. The phenomenon is very strange, considering that the discharge did not pass through the frog's body and that the frog was dead.

A special experiment was carried out by Galvani in the yard during a thunderstorm and gave the same results: the frog mice regularly responded to each thunderstorm with contractions if the nerve was grounded.

Galvani does not give any obligation to this phenomenon, and he could not give it *).

*) The fact is that in an electric spark and in lightning, electrical vibrations occur, which propagate in the form of radio waves in all directions. The frog's muscles apparently served as the “user” for such waves and responded to them with contractions. However, the existence of these oscillations became known only many decades later - in 1859. They learned that they propagate in the form of radio waves almost 30 years later - in 1888. And 7 years later, A.S. Popov used radio waves for communication purposes. By the way, A.S. Popov also “caught” radio waves from lightning discharges with his device (he was even called a lightning detector). Galvani's dissected frogs also apparently responded to radio waves from electrical sparks and lightning discharges.

Alexaンドro Volta gets down to business. Frog dispute

Galvani's treatise aroused great interest. He also attracted the attention of Pavin University physics professor Alexンドro Volta, already known for his work on the study of electrical phenomena and the properties of electricity. Having learned about Galvani's experiments and not at all believing in the existence of animal electricity, Volta nevertheless repeated these experiments. An experimental physicist, he was able, no worse than the anatomist Galvani, to dissect frogs, repeat experiments and try the same results, which made him at first even believe in animal electricity.

In a lecture read on May 5, 1792, he extols Galvani in every possible way, praises the discovery he made, talks about its unusual importance, etc. However, a little later, on May 14, in another lecture, Volta already argues with Galvani, proving that what Galvani discovered is purely physical and not physiological, that animal electricity does not exist and that the matter is not at all in the frog, but in two dissimilar metals, collides with it and closes the circuit. As proof, Volta describes his significant experience, the following state. He applied the two ends of two different metal plates to his own tongue, and when the other ends of the plates were connected, he felt a sour taste, and the effect was not immediate, but long-lasting. (This method is now used to check the suitability of dry batteries.) The tongue has successfully replaced the frog!

This began a long dispute between Galvani and Volta about the role of the frog in his observations. Volta tried to prove that the frog, like the experimenter’s tongue, is only a timely indicator of the flow of electricity, and the main role is played by a pair of dissimilar metals. For this purpose, he tried to exclude from the “Galvanic” experiments frogs and, in general, his favorite biological object, including his own language. Galvani, trying to prove that the basis of the phenomenon is animal electricity, and metals only conduct it most efficiently, sought to eliminate metals from his experiments and obtain the same results using biological means alone. In short, Volta wanted to obtain electric current without frogs, and Galvani wanted to achieve muscle contraction without metals.

Oddly enough, success has increased the number of both participants in the dispute. In 1894, Galvani described an experiment in which the nerve and muscle of a frog were connected not by metal, but by the frog muscle. Each time the closure of such a circuit caused the frog to flinch. Galvani considered his task completed and did not continue his research. Soon Northern Italy was conquered by Napoleon Bonaparte. Galvani opposed the French rule in his homeland, lost his professorship in 1797, and died in 1798.

In all these studies, which are of great importance for physics and technology, Volta himself did not take any part. Like Galvani, Volta, having achieved success, practically stopped his scientific activities. His discovery received universal recognition and made a huge impression. Twice, in 1800 and 1801, he reported his discovery to the Paris Academy of Sciences. The last report was attended by Napoleon Bonaparte, who fully appreciated the importance of Volta's discovery. Volta was showered with awards. He received an Academy medal, a large monetary award, the title of count and senator, the highest French orders. But in science his role was over. In 1819 he left the professorial chair, and in 1827 he died.

The results of the frog dispute

The dispute between Galvani and Volta not only led to the appearance of the first source of direct electric current in history. This is not a dispute that ends in the victory of one of its participants and the defeat of the other. History did not offend Galvani either. And the point is not only that his name was assigned to a current source that he did not discover, although he could have discovered it (after all, in the experiment with two metals and a frog muscle, Galvani dealt specifically with the Galvanic element). But in its meaning, in which the frog’s muscle trembled, closed by the muscle, he actually discovered “animal electricity.” Much later (in 1837 State Duma) this was proven by the Talyan physicist Mattecchi and (in 1848 State Duma) by the German physicist Dubois-Rein. True, now this is called not animal electrical, but biological potentials or biocurrents.

Volt's discovery was not only of great practical importance. She also set scientists the task of explaining such phenomena as Volt's discovery, such as contact potential difference and Galvanic current.

Volta himself believed that the basis of the action of the Galvanic element was precisely the contact potential difference.
