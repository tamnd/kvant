How the distances between atoms in crystals change

The words “structure” and “structure” are very meaningful. Different studies mean different things under these words. A biologist can talk about the structure of muscle tissue, meaning the arrangement of the fibers relative to each other; When a metallographer talks about the structure of an alloy, he usually means the shape and size of the grains. These structures are observed using a general micro-cleavage at magnifications of only a few hundred times. But the same words are also used when talking about the particles that make up the atomic nucleus, whose placement varies by trillionths of a centimeter. Here the word “structure” already has a conventional meaning, and physicists use it with caution, remembering that visual visual representations are not suitable for the solid bricks of the universe.

In solid state physics, the words “structure” and “structure” can also be used in different senses. So, for example, when discussing the electronic structure of a metal, they do not mean the location of electrons in space, but the nature of their energy distribution; speaking about the structure of ferromagnetic materials, they describe the size, shape and placement of domains. There are other situations where the word “structure” is quite appropriate.

This article will discuss the atomic structure of crystals, which refers to the pattern created by the centers of atoms. Atomic structure

We proceed to the derivation of the basic law of selective reflection of X-rays by a crystal. Let the incident beam, which is an electromagnetic wave defining the length $\lambda$, fall on the crystal at some angle. We have the right to believe that the reflection of the X-ray wave will occur according to the law: the angle of incidence is equal to the angle of reflection, that is, according to the same law as for an optical beam. But there is a significant difference with an optical beam. A light beam does not penetrate deep into the crystal, but an X-ray beam can pass through the crystal. This means that the reflection of the X-ray beam will originate not only from the outer surface of the crystal, but from all its atomic planes.

Let us consider one of the systems characterized by the plane distance $d$ (Fig. 5). Each of the planes will reflect the incident beam at the same angle $\theta$ (the so-called diffraction angle). These reflected rays are correct, and then must interfere with each other. Moreover, secondary (reflected) rays will succeed each other if, after reflection from all planes of the family, they propagate in the same phase. In other words, if the path difference between the beams is equal to the number $n$ wavelengths. The path difference $|PM| + |MN|$ (see Fig. 5) between adjacent reflected rays is equal to $2d\sin\theta$ (prove it!). Consequently, the diffraction condition—the condition for the success of reflected rays—will have the form

$$2d\sin\theta=n\lambda.$$ (2)

It is called the Bragg equation.

A crystal, as we have already said, can be divided into systems of planes in an infinite number of ways. But effective for reflection (you understand the meaning of these words, right?) is only a system of planes with such a distance mechanism and oriented so relative to the incident beam that the Bragg equation is satisfied.

If the incident beam is monochromatic (that is, the electromagnetic wave has one specific length), then at an arbitrary position of the crystal relative to the beam, reflection may not occur (or rather, the total effect of the possibility of reflection may turn out to be zero). However, by rotating the crystal, we can alternately bring different systems of planes into a reflective position. It is this way of working that turned out to be the most suitable in practice.

Let us show that the intensity of the reflected beam will depend on the structure of the cell - in this case, on the internomial distance in the molecule and on the angle formed by the axis of the molecule with the axis of the cell.

The radiation intensity is proportional to the square of the wave amplitude. Indeed, let at the observation point the field created by the lattice of atoms be written as $A \cos \omega t$. The intensity is $\frac{A^2 \cos^2 \omega t}{\alpha}$. The dash above means upreading in time (oscillations occur quickly, and experience records the average values). But $\cos^2 \omega t = \frac{1}{2}$ (this is easy to prove). Therefore, the intensity turns out to be proportional to $A^2$ - the square of the wave amplitude.

In the case of a lattice of two-atomic molecules, the resulting field of an electromagnetic wave can be considered as the sum of the fields of two simple lattices. These two fields will arrive at the observation point with a phase shift, which we will denote $2\alpha$. Keeping the expression $A \cos \omega t$ for the lattice of nodes, we now write the sum of the fields of two lattices in the form

$$A \cos (\omega t + \alpha) + A \cos (\omega t - \alpha).$$

Each node is “expanded” into two particles, creating one field with a certain phase, and the other with a lag. Adding, squaring and averaging over time, we find that the intensity of the reflected beam will be proportional to $\cos^2 \alpha$.

By definition, $\alpha = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda} \Delta$, where $\lambda$ is the wavelength and $\Delta$ is the path difference. Although the derivation of the expression for the path difference $\Delta$ is no different from the derivation of the Bragg formula, for this case we nevertheless carried out a careful construction on figure 9, from which the reader, after a little torment, will find the necessary expression:

$$\Delta = |OC| + |OD| = 2r_n \sin \theta,$$

where $r_n$ is the projection of the radius vector $r$ (connecting the atoms of the molecule) onto the direction of propagation of the reflected wave (to the normal direction $n$), $\theta$ is the Bragg scattering angle. We use the Bragg equation and the definition of the inverse vector:

$$\Delta = 2r_n \sin \theta = \lambda r_n \frac{n}{d},$$

where does the phase difference come from?

$$\alpha = \frac{2\pi \Delta}{\lambda} = 2\pi r_n \frac{n}{d}.$$

So, the intensity of the reflected wave, proportional to $\cos^2 \alpha$, is really determined by the structure of the unit cell of the crystal. Obviously, if there are not two atoms in a cell, but many, then all the reasoning will be similar.
