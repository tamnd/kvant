6. Let us show that for any natural number $k$ the following statements hold: if $n \geq k$ then $f(n) \geq k$. For $k = 1$ the statement is trivial. Let it be true for $k$ and let it be true for $n \geq k + 1$. Then $n - 1 \geq k$ and based on the inductive proposition $f(n - 1) \geq k$ and $f(f(n - 1)) \geq k$. And since $f(n) > f(f(n - 1))$, then $f(n) > k$ or $f(n) \geq k + 1$. It follows that $f(k) \geq k$ for all natural numbers $k$. Let us assume that for some $k$ there is $f(k) > k$. Let us denote by $A$ the set of all $f(n)$ for $n > k$. Let $l = f(n)$ be the smallest element of the set $A$. Since $n - 1 \geq k$, then $f(n - 1) > k$. Indeed, if $n - 1 > k$, then this follows from the inequality $f(n - 1) \geq n - 1$, and if $n - 1 = k$, then from the inequality $f(k) > k$. Let $m = f(f(n - 1))$. From the above it follows that $m$ belongs to $A$. But the inequality $f(n) > f(f(n - 1))$ is true, which means $l > m$, which contradicts the fact that $l$ is the smallest element of the set $A$.

This means that assumption $f(k) > k$ is false, and for all $n$ $f(n) = n$ holds.
