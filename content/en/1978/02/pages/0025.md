c) Let the axis $u$ belong to the first class, and the axis $v$ to the second. Let us show that the lines $u$ and $v$ intersect. Indeed, when moving $R^{-1}_v \circ R_u$, the straight line $m$ will turn into itself with a change in direction. Therefore, we obtain a transformation of the line $m$ - symmetrically to a certain point $C \in m$ (proof!). Hence $R^{-1}_v \circ R_u(C) = C$ and $R_v(C) = R_u(C) = D$. From $R_u(C) = D, R_v(C) = D$ it follows that all points of the lines $u$ and $v$ are equidistant from the points $C$ and $D$. But then $u$ and $v$ lie in the same plane (see Fig. 1). Therefore, if $u$ and $v$ do not intersect, then they are parallel. Since $u \parallel xOz$ and $v \parallel yOz$, it follows from $u \parallel v$ that $u \parallel z$. But this is impossible, since $z \perp m$. Therefore, $u$ and $v$ intersect.

d) Axes $x$ and $y$ are obviously of different classes. Therefore, each axis intersects either line $x$ or line $y$. Since three axes cannot pass through the point $F$ (since then two of them would belong to the same class), there are no “extraneous” points in the figure.

4. It remains to construct our set. To do this, we construct a parallel with axes parallel to straight lines $x, y, z$ and center at point $O$ (see Fig. 5), such that the diagonals of the upper and lower bases lie on straight lines $m$ and $n$, respectively, and the distance from the vertex $A$ to straight line $m$ is equal to its side edge. Axes parallel to plane $yOz$ must pass through points $O_1$ and $O_2$. It is clear that these are straight lines $O_1A$ and $O_2B$. Each axis parallel to the plane $xOz$ must intersect with $O_1A$ and $O_2B$, then $|O_1A| = |O_2B|$. The corresponding construction is given on page 20 of the magazine. (You could, of course, use straight lines $y$ and $OA$, but then the construction would not be so symmetrical.) As you can see, the result is a surface similar to a saddle.

5. Taking straight lines $x, y$ and $z$ as coordinate axes, we derive the control of this surface. The conditions are set by the direct control of two planes intersecting along it. Then the controls of the lines $m$ and $n$ will be written in the form

$$\begin{cases} y = kx \\ z = c, \end{cases}$$

The direction vectors of these lines have the coordinates:

$$\vec{m} = \{1; k; 0\}, \vec{n} = \{1; -k; 0\}.$$

Let the point $P(x, y, z)$ be equidistant from the lines $m$ and $n$. If $M$ and $N$ are orthogonal projections of the point $P$ onto the lines $m$ and $n$, respectively, then $|MP| = |MN|$. Let us express the coordinates of the point $P$.

Since $M \in m$, the coordinates of the point $M$ are $k, y$ and $z$. Considering that $\vec{MP} \cdot \vec{m} = 0$, $\vec{MP} = (x - \alpha, y - k\alpha, z - c)$, $m = \{1, k, 0\}$, we obtain $x - \alpha + yk - k^2\alpha = 0$, which implies that $\alpha = \frac{x + ky}{1 + k^2}$, that is, the points $M$ has coordinates

$$\frac{x + ky}{1 + k^2}, \frac{x + ky}{1 + k^2}, c.$$

Reasoning similarly, we find the coordinates of the point $N$:

$$\frac{x - ky}{1 + k^2}, -k \frac{x - ky}{1 + k^2}, -c.$$

Writing the equality $|MP| = |NP|$ after simplification, we obtain the expression

$$xy = 2pz,$$

where $p = -\frac{1 + k^2}{2k}$.

Consequently, the set of points $m$ and $n$ are surfaces whose control has the form (1). (Control (1) was first obtained by L. Euler in 1748.)
