But if such a pair of mating metals is closed, then there will be no flow of electricity in the circuit, since the currents through the span have opposite directions (figure 1). In “galvanic” irons, electricity flows in a closed circuit. And Volta decided that the reason for this was that both in Galvani’s experiments and in his own, a third conductor appeared - liquid (liquid in frog mice or liquid on the tongue). He replaces the "wet" sections of the "galvanic" circuit with simply moistened cardboard and shows that such a system forms a source of continuous movement of electricity. Volta reported these experiments in 1796. Thus, as it seemed to Volta, Galvani's animal electricity was put to an end. But Volta still talks about "galvanism", giving credit to the one who gave the impetus to his own research. And to this day, phenomena associated with the passage of direct electric current are called galvanic.

This is how the “galvanic” cell, invented by Volta, was born. After 1796, the author of this invention remained silent for four years. All these years he was looking for a way to obtain more powerful sources than his first element, consisting of a pair of dissimilar metal electrodes (as we now call them), colliding with a liquid conductor. In the end, Volta found such a way. He arranged a series of his elements one behind the other in the form of bowls of liquid, in which the middle and cyclic electrodes are immersed: middle - liquid - cyclic - medium - liquid - cyclic, etc. (figure 2).
