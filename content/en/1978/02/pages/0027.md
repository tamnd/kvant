Spherical ellipse

An ellipse (“Geometry 10”, § 59; see also “Algebra 8”, paragraph 2) can be defined as a set of points on a plane, the sum of the distances from which to two given points of the plane (called foci) is a constant. If in this definition the “sum” is replaced by the “modulus of the difference”, we get an ellerbola. A parabola is a set of points on a plane, the distances from which to a given point on the plane (focus) and a given straight line (directrix) are the same.

A spherical ellipse is a set of points on a plane, the distances from which to a given point on the plane (focus) and a given straight line (directrix) are the same.

A spherical ellipse is a set of points on a plane, the distances from which to a given point on the plane (focus) and a given straight line (directrix) are the same.

A flat ellipse turns into a circle only when its foci merge. As N.I. Fuss showed, a spherical ellipse turns into a circle (namely, the greater circle of the foquera) if the constant sum of the distance referred to in the definition is equal to half the length of the greater ok-circle. The congested spherical triangles highlighted on figure 1 near the foci $F_1$, $F_2$ will help you prove Fuss's statement.

Consider a sphere $\Delta$ and a plane $\alpha$ tangent at the focus to some spherical ellipse $l$ on it. Let us draw all possible straight lines through the center of the sphere $O$ and points of the spherical ellipse $l$. It turns out that the finite surface $\Gamma$ obtained in this way will intersect the plane $\alpha$ along some ellipse $\lambda$. The surface $\Gamma$ will intersect the initial sphere along one more spherical ellipse $l'$, symmetrically with $l$ relative to $O$ (Fig. 2).

Interestingly, with respect to the foci of the ellipse $l$, the ellipse $l'$ will be a spherical hyperbola.

An ellipse (flat) can also be defined as a set of points (on a plane), the ratio of the distance from which to a given point (focus) and a given straight line (directrix) is constant and less than 1. A spherical ellipse can be defined in a similar way. It can be proven that the central projection of the directrix of the ellipse $\lambda$ relative to $O$ onto the sphere $\Delta$ is the directrix of the spherical ellipse $l$: the ratio of the distances from any point of the ellipse $l$ to the focus and to the resulting great circle in the described projection is constant and less than 1.

The angles between the segments connecting an arbitrary point of a plane ellipse with the foci and the tangent to the ellipse at this point are equal. This state has the so-called optical property of an ellipse. A spherical ellipse has a similar property: if at any point $M$ we draw a tangent great circle and connect the point $M$ along the great circles with the foci of the ellipse, then the angles between this latter circle and the tangent circle will be equal.

Many properties of the spherical ellipse were first discovered by the Russian mathematician, Swiss by birth, N. I. Fuss (1755-1825). N. I. Fuss was a student and assistant of the great L. Euler and carried out most of his first research in the field of mathematics and mechanics under his guidance. Subsequently, N.I. Fuss became a full member of the St. Petersburg Academy of Sciences and its permanent secretary. Interesting results concerning the spherical ellipse were also obtained by the Russian mathematician L. I. Magnus (1790-1861).
