2. Probability of Black Friday (No. 303). Prove that the thirteenth of the month is more likely to fall on a Friday than on other days of the week.

3. Another property of the harmonic series (No. 304). Prove that the partial sum $\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{4} + \dots + \frac{1}{N}$ of a harmonic series is not an integer for any N.

4. Prime numbers of varithmetic progression (No. 305). Prove that, with one exception, in any arithmetic progression whose terms are positive integers and the difference is less than 2000, no more than 10 consecutive terms can be prime numbers.

5. Zeros and extremum points of a cubic polynomial (No. 318). Given a cubic polynomial $y = x^3 + px^2 + qx$. Determine all integer pairs of coefficients $p$ and $q$ for which the equation $y = 0$ has different integer roots and integer coordinates of two extremum points.

6. Cutting task (No. 326). How can a regular hexagon be cut into the smallest number of pieces that can be used to form an equilateral triangle of the same area? Each cut is made along a straight line.

7. Hamilton cycle on a polyhedron (No. 358). Let's assume that we want to be at the vertices of a polyhedron, but we can reach them only by moving along its edges. Hamilton solved the problem of finding all the vertices of a polyhedron along a route that does not pass through the same vertex twice. (In graph theory, such a route is usually called a Harmilton cycle.) It is not difficult to prove that Hamilton's proof problem has a solution. Prove that Hamilton's problem has no solution for the rhombodedecadron.

8. Determination of a fake coin using weighing (No. 359). One man had 12 coins, indistinguishable in appearance. The coin was fake and differed in weight from the real one. The man also had very sensitive balancing scales at hand, but without weights. Can he, with no more than three weighings, detect a counterfeit coin and determine whether it is heavier or lighter than a real one?

9. “Equilateral Birdmen” (No. 360). Let us call a set consisting of $n$ points on the plane an equilateral $n$-pointer if any three of them are located at the vertices of an equilateral triangle. Six points on a plane can be positioned so that they form an equilateral 6-point plane.

Prove that it is impossible to choose seven points on the plane that form an equilateral 7-point plane. What is the smallest number $k$ such that from any $k$ points in space we can choose three that are not located at the vertices of an equilateral triangle?

11. Court mathematician and shtut (No. 374). One day, the court mathematician immediately received all the middle thalers he had desired for the year and, having folded 9 unequal coins from the coins, arranged them in the form of a magic square. Corollo liked the court mathematician's approach, but he complained that not a single column contained prime thalers.

“If Great Majesty added another 9 thalers to my wish,” the court mathematician noted, “I could increase the number of coins in each column by 1, and then all 9 numbers in the column would be prime.”

We checked. Everything turned out exactly as the court mathematician said. The king was already about to increase the desired mathematicians by 9 thalers a year, like a buffoon in action. He took one coin from each column, and the number of thalers remaining in each column turned out to be prime. Moreover, the 9 new numbers still made up the magic square. Nine “extra” thalers remained.

How many thalers did the court mathematician receive per year?
