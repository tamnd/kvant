V. Kasatkin

Communicative Anya

The reason for this story was the report that Anya Shakhovskaya gave at the traditional “Small Academy of Sciences” gatherings of Crimean schoolchildren this past summer. Anya lives in the village of Nauchny near Bakhchisarai. She is now in eighth grade. Anya is a year younger than the Small Academy - she turned 14 this year.

One summer, when Anya was at a tourist camp, she was appointed responsible for issuing food to tourists.

“Make sure you don’t make a mistake, calculate everything correctly,” the camp commander admonished her, “we don’t have a computer.”

- Don't worry, I'll think of something.

And Anya came up with an idea. In a nearby store she managed to break a scale and several weights.

In the morning, everyone came running to see Anya releasing the kruta to tourists.

Unfortunately, Anya managed to find only 5 weights - weighing 1 g, 3 g, 9 g, 27 g and 81 g. Nevertheless, Anya, apparently, did not experience any difficulties. She deftly placed the griri either on the empty scale or on the bowl with bags of cool. At the same time, she made some incomprehensible, at first glance, notes. Having released 35 g of cool, she wrote down: 1101; after weighing 52 g, the entry appeared: 11011; in the next line she wrote 11101 and said:

— That’s it, I weighed the last 100 grams. The work is finished.

After that, she picked up an abacus of a non-teachable design and, looking at the notes she had made, began to quickly work on them. It was an amazing coming of age.

A few minutes later, Anya finished her calculations and remarked with satisfaction:

- Everything is correct, not a single mistake.

Numerous critics wanted to understand how Anya worked. At their request, she said:

We are accustomed to the fact that in any number system we use

Use only positive integers (and the number 0). It is possible, however, to imagine a system of numbers in which negative integers will also be used as numbers. Since the masses of the weights that were at my disposal were powers of three, I will remember the so-called balanced ternary system.

The basis of the system is the number 3, the symbols used as numbers are: 1, 0 and $\bar{1}$ (read: “one with a line”). The notes I made were numbers in this system. Here's how to understand them:

$$110\bar{1} = 1 \cdot 3^3 + 1 \cdot 3^2 + 0 \cdot 3^1 - 1 \cdot 3^0 = 35,$$
$$1\bar{1}0\bar{1} = 1 \cdot 3^4 - 1 \cdot 3^3 + 0 \cdot 3^2 - 1 \cdot 3^1 +$$
$$+ 1 \cdot 3^0 = 52,$$
$$11\bar{1}01 = 1 \cdot 3^4 + 1 \cdot 3^3 - 1 \cdot 3^2 + 0 \cdot 3^1 +$$
$$+ 1 \cdot 3^0 = 100.$$

If I placed a weight on a bowl with a large scale, then its mass was subtracted, and when recording, I wrote the number “1” in the category corresponding to the mass of the weight. If I need to weigh, for example, 24 g, then I will put a weight of 27 g on a bowl of scales without a load (without grains), and a weight of 3 g on the other bowl and write down: $10\bar{1}0 = 1 \cdot 3^3 + 0 \cdot 3^2 - 1 \cdot 3^1 +$
$$+ 0 \cdot 3^0 = 24.$$

Number entries in this system can also begin with the number “1” - this is how negative numbers are written. Example:

$$\bar{1}1\bar{1} = -1 \cdot 3^2 + 1 \cdot 3^1 - 1 \cdot 3^0 = -7.$$

With numbers written in a balanced ternary number system, it is not difficult to perform the operations of addition and multiplication.

Here's how simple the tables for adding and multiplying numbers look:

**Addition table**

| + | 1 | 0 | $\bar{1}$ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | $1\bar{1}$ | 1 | 0 |
| 0 | 1 | 0 | $\bar{1}$ |
| $\bar{1}$ | 0 | $\bar{1}$ | $1\bar{1}$ |

To simplify the work, I used an abacus, specially designed for adding numbers in a balanced ternary system. These abacus are depicted on figure 1. The main feature is a vertical partition, to the left of which there are “negative” bones, and to the right - “positive”. At figure 2 the number $11\bar{1}01$ is set aside on the abacus. The second feature is that the black bones are fastened to each other.

Let's learn how to add one to these accounts. Let us need to find the sum 1+1. How this is done can be seen from figure 3, $a = \beta$. At figure 3, $\delta$ the fastened black stones are connected to the already deposited stone; therefore, discarding all three bones from the wire to the right, we must put one negative bone on the same wire, and on the next wire - one “positive” bone (it, as it should be, replaces the discarded stones of the first wire) - see Fig. 3, $\delta$. The result can be checked by looking at the table of adding numbers. Using similar reasoning, you can learn to add negative ones.

Note that if the abacus has a bone on both sides of the vertical partition, then they should be reset: $1 + \bar{1} = 0$. A little training and you will learn how to use this unusual abacus.

Don't think that the number system I told you about is just mathematical fun. In the USSR and Czechoslovakia, computers were built that operate on this number system. The Soviet car is called “Setun” (named after the river near Moscow). It was created by engineers and mathematicians of the computer center of Moscow University. They were attracted by two features of this number system: the absence of “sign rules” when performing arithmetic operations on numbers and the ease of obtaining the opposite number - the opposite number of a given number is obtained if each digit “1” of a given number is replaced by a “1” and vice versa.

For his invented abacus and a good report on the balanced ternary system, Ano Shakhovsky was elected a full member of the Minor Academy of Sciences.
