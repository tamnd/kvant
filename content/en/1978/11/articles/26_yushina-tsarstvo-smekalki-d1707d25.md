Kingdom of ingenuity

This year, the publishing house “Nauka” republished one of the old and very good Russian popular books on mathematics, which our grandfathers studied and loved - the book of the gymnasium teacher E. I. Ignatev “In the Kingdom of Ingenuity”.

E. I. Ignatev can rightfully be called a classic of the mathematical genre he occupies. In his book, the first edition of which was published in 1908, he collected a large number of diverse communication problems in argumentation and geometry. The second edition of the book appeared in 1911. This book has not been reprinted since then. Especially the books were its literary style, the language of the author. Of course, for this publication some transformation of the problem and their conditions was necessary. However, I would still like to see the classics reissued more carefully - otherwise the loss of the flavor of time is inevitable.

Over the past 70 years, the popularity of mathematical literature published in Russian has greatly expanded, and now many problems from Ignatev’s book are widely known. Nevertheless, this book is undoubtedly of great interest even now - and not only historical.

Ignatev spoke very well about the goals of the book, about how mathematics should be studied and how mathematics should be taught, and the role of the task of ingenuity in his “Prefaces” to the first and second editions of the book. Below are excerpts from them.

“Will anyone in our time deny the present need for the widest dissemination and popularization of mathematical knowledge? Primary mathematical knowledge should be included in our education and upbringing from a very early age. It goes without saying that intellectual initiative, quick-wittedness and “savvy” cannot be “infused” or “put into” anyone’s head. The results are reliable only when the introduction to the field of mathematical knowledge is made in an easy and pleasant way, using objects and examples from ordinary and everyday situations, selected with appropriate acuity and interest.

* * *

Regarding mathematics, in our society there are still the strangest predictors. Some say that only exceptional minds, gifted with very special abilities, can study mathematics, others argue that this requires a special, so to speak, “mathematical memory” for filling out formulas, etc.

One cannot, of course, argue against the fact that there are minds with pronounced inclinations towards one or another side of mental activity. But in the same way, it is in no way possible to assert that there are any conditions for the necessary mathematical knowledge, at least, say, in the size of a high school course.

Let us be fair and admit, finally, that the expression “incapable of mathematics” is primarily an inability, and, perhaps, sometimes a frivolous reluctance to put the teaching of mathematics at the proper level in the family and school.

Even less can we talk about the need for mathematics to have some kind of special, special memory for filling in (notching?) some formulas or rules, to turn the science of conscious and consistent logical thought into some kind of mechanical, unconscious process. Meanwhile, how far things can go in this regard is evidenced by the famous Russian mathematician V.P. Ermakov. This is what, by the way, he reported in one of his reports to the Kyiv Physics and Mathematics Society.

“When I had to read interval calculus to students, in the very first year an episode occurred that was forever preserved in my memory.

After reading part of the theory, I give problems for understanding. I ask students to solve problems in terdras. As I solve, I write my results on the board. However, to clarify ways to understand biominal intervals, I wrote a problem on the approach board. And in life, some students take some terdras out of their pockets and look into them.

- What is this?
— General formulas.
- For what?
— The professor advised us to have a list of general formulas and use it to solve specific examples. After all, you won’t demand that we memorize all the common formulas.

— You shouldn’t memorize any formulas in mathematics. But I also find it inappropriate to use manuals and find intervals using general formulas by substituting into them the given values ​​of indicators and coefficients. After all, general formulas did not fall to us from the sky; to derive them you used a series of reasonings; apply the same reasoning to particular examples.

Thus it turned out that integrals can be performed without general formulas. It was necessary, however, to modify the original calculations so that they could be directly applied to particular examples.

Another benefit was that for each partial example, students repeated the same reasoning that was necessary to derive the general formula. Frequent repetition acquired skills, and as a result, speed in solving problems.

This episode made me delve deeper into the creatures of mathematics.

In his young years, he focused all his attention on the final results. When analyzing any proof, I cared only about making sure of its consistency. So adding to the final result, and that’s enough! Then I tried to remember the final conclusions, but the whole process of proof quickly evaporated. But then the formulas were forgotten, and often these formulas turned out to be necessary for further studies. What was left to do? Building a library of reference books? But there were not enough funds for this, and there was no room for libraries. Inevitably, it was necessary to use the very process by which this or that formula was derived. Thus, together with the formulas, little by little I came to the proof itself. It turned out that it is easier to use the process of mathematical thinking than just formulas. And there is no need to remember the entire process of thinking; it is enough to outline the milestones along which our capes should go. And for several years now, I have been repeating to my servants: in mathematics we should remember not the formulas, but the process of thinking. [...]

If the process of mathematical thinking is expressed, then obtaining the formulas themselves is already a purely mechanical matter.

In the mechanism of algebraic operations, students must acquire skills in high school.

I have come to the conclusion that the above-mentioned principle should also be applied in secondary school...”

Let's continue the thought of V.P. Ermakov and say: this principle should especially form the basis of national - both family and school - education in the field of mathematical knowledge. Do not train either children or young men on various “tables” of addition, subtraction, multiplication, or on mechanically filling out various “rules” and formulas, but above all, teach them to mouse willingly and consciously. Other applications. Do not teach any lineman the most boring and mechanical calculations and exercises.

When someone needs them in life, he made them himself - and now there are all sorts of calculating machines, tables and other devices for this.
