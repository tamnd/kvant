Center of gravity of the hemisphere

22 centuries ago, Archimedes invented a mechanical method with which for the first time it was possible to find the volume of a sphere and the center of gravity of the hemisphere. Even earlier, Democritus and Eudoxus established that the volume of a cone is three times less than the volume of the described cylinder, and the center of gravity of the cone divides its height in a ratio of 3:1, counting from the top.

Let us prove that the volume of the sphere is one and a half times less than the volume of the described cylinder and that the center of gravity of the hemisphere divides its height in the ratio of 3:5, counting from the center of the sphere.

Consider a sphere and a cylinder circumscribed around it. The plane figure 1 passes through the cylinder axis, directed horizontally. Let us also consider a cone with its vertex at the center of the ball O and its base common with the base of the hemisphere.

Let us draw a plane MN through an arbitrary point A on the axis of the hemisphere, perpendicular to this axis. In section with a ball it gives a circle of radius AP, with a cone - a circle of radius AB, with a cylinder - a circle of radius AM=R. The Pythagorean theorem states that AP²+AO²=OP²=AM², and since the angle at the vertex of a cone is straight and AO=AB, AP²+AB²=AM².

Taking this ratio n times, we see that the cross sections of the ball and the cone have an area equal to the cross section of the cylinder:

$$S_{\text{ш}} + S_{\text{к}} = S_{\text{ц}}.$$ (1)

Since (1) is true for any point A on the axis of the hemisphere, from Cavalieri’s principle (“Algebra and the beginnings of analysis 10”, paragraph 107, or «Kvant», 1977, No. 2, pp. 7, 10) it is found that the volume of the hemisphere V_{\text{w}} and the cone V_{\text{k}} are in total equal to the volume of the hemisphere V_{\text{c}}:

$$V_{\text{ш}} + V_{\text{к}} = V_{\text{ц}}.$$ (2)

Let us now consider the axis of the cylinder as a lever with a section at the center O. Let us transfer the section of the cylinder to the right side figure to point A′, symmetrical to point A relative to the center O. Using (2), we establish that the sections of the ball and cone, suspended together at point A, balance the section of the cylinder suspended at point A′.

If we now fill with circles all the sections of the ball and the cone on the left, and the sections of the hemisphere - symmetrically to the right of the point O, then from the equilibrium of each three such circles it follows that the hemisphere and the cone together balance the hemisphere relative to the center O (Fig. 2).

But the center of gravity of the hemisphere is located in the middle C of its axis, and the center of gravity K of the cone, as mentioned above, is at a distance OK=(3/4)R from the vertex. Let the center of gravity of the hemisphere be at point X. Then, on the right side of the lever, the equilibrium conditions of these three bodies have the form:

$$|OX| \cdot V_{\text{ш}} + |OK| \cdot V_{\text{к}} = |OC| \cdot V_{\text{ц}}.$$ (3)

But V_{\text{k}} = (1/3)V_{\text{c}}, and from the relationship between volumes (2) we find V_{\text{c}}=V_{\text{c}}-V_{\text{c}}=(2/3)V_{\text{c}}, that is, the volume of the hemisphere is one and a half times less than the volume of the described cylinder. Therefore, equilibrium (3) takes the form:

$$(2/3)V_{\text{ц}} + OX + (1/3)V_{\text{ц}} \times (3/4)R = V_{\text{ц}} \cdot (1/2)R.$$

From here we get

$$|OX| = (3/8)R,$$

that is, the center of gravity of the hemisphere divides the height of the hemisphere in a ratio of 3:5, counting from the center of the ball.

How to find the center of gravity of a segment of a sphere or a segment of an ellipsoid using this method can be easily communicated to the reader himself.

M. Mamikon
