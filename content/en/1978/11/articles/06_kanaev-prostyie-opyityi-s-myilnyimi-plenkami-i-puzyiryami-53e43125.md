Simple experiments with soap films and bubbles

The experiments do not require any complex equipment, so they can be performed even at home.

Necessary conditions for successful experiments are careful preparation using glassware and preparing a good soap solution. It is best to prepare a solution from shampoo diluted in water with the addition of a small amount of pure glycerin and strong ammonia.

As a narrow tube for blowing soap bubbles, you can use an empty plastic refill from a ballpoint pen with a removable metal tip. It is better to blow small bubbles and make sure that there are no droplets of solution at the bottom. If you need a bubble from the end of the tube, this can be easily done by quickly moving your hand vertically upward.

After you have prepared everything you need and are ready to blow soap bubbles, proceed to specific experiments.

1. Release the soap bubble, tear it off the end of the tube and immediately begin to quickly move backwards - first straight, then deviating to the right, then to the left. The bubble will follow you all the time!

This behavior of the bubble is due to the fact that when a person moves sharply, an area of ​​​​low air pressure is created between him and the bubble - this is where the soap bubble rushes.

2. Take a wide glass tube (at least 2 cm in diameter). Make a circle out of foil with a diameter slightly larger than the diameter of the tube. After moistening the foil with soapy water, attach it to the end of the tube; Lower the other end of the tube into a deep vessel with water. Soon you will see that the “valve” made of foil opens slightly - this is “at work” by the soap bubble formed at the end of the tube, which inflates under the influence of the rolling air in the tube. The deeper the tube is immersed in the water, the greater the angle the “valve” opens. For some deep dives, this angle becomes equal to 90° (Fig. 1).

3. Carefully lower the soap bubble onto some flat wet surface (for example, water, paper or glass) - due to wetting, the lower part of the bubble will spread over the surface and the bubble will take the shape of a hemisphere.

Now get a flat soap film on the wire ring and place a soap bubble on it, the diameter of which is approximately equal to the diameter of the ring. You will see that both the bubble and the flat film have changed their shape - a biconvex line is obtained, both halves of which are symmetrical relative to the plane of the conductor ring.

4. Take a frame of glasses without glasses and dip it in a soapy solution - the frame will tighten with two

flat films. If you wear such glasses, then the surrounding objects will be visible in their natural form: there will be no increase, decrease, or decrease in visibility.

Change the experience. Tighten the frame again with films and, placing them in a horizontal plane, lower a soap bubble onto each of them (as in the previous experiment). You will receive glasses with biconvex soap lenses. But here’s what’s surprising: through such extraordinary glasses, the surrounding objects of space are without any changes, as is the case with flat films. Why?

The fact is that soap lenses are essentially air lenses, since there is air between the thinnest spherical soap films. Light rays passing through such a lens are practically not refracted.

5. Is it possible to get a flat soap film in a test tube, and not at the free end, but inside the test tube? Try this. We offer you two ways.

a) Partially fill the test tube with water. Take a strip of paper and, after moistening it with a soap solution, pass it along the end of the test tube - the end will be covered with a film. To make the film fit inside the test tube, tilt the test tube and shoot out some of the water through the film. As much water flies out in height, the film will go down.

6) Pour some water into the test tube and heat it to a boil using a candle or alcohol lamp. Move the heater, cover the hole with soap film. After some time, you will see that the film will gradually fall inside the test tube.

This is explained by the fact that water vapor condenses when cooled, a vacuum is created under the film, where the film rushes. To speed up the lowering process, the test tube can be cooled with running water.

6. Blow a soap bubble at the end of a narrow tube. Using plasticine, attach the free end of the tube to the foot of the shativa or to some horizontal crossbar located at a height of approximately 20 cm above the table surface. Using a medical syringe (or other narrow tube), you can pump air into or out of the bladder. Will there be distortions in the shape of the bubbles?

Of course not - this follows directly from Pascal's law.

7. Take a glass jar 60 mm high and fill it 2/3 with soap solution. Close the jar with a stopper into which two electrodes made of copper wire with a diameter of 1-2 mm and a length of 80 mm are inserted (Fig. 2). The lower ends of the electrodes should reach the bottom, but not touch it. The upper ends are at an angle of 90° and using the lead wires, connect the electrodes to the flashlight battery. Small bubbles of gas will begin to release at the cathode, which, rising upward, form foam on the surface of the solution. What gas is contained in the bubbles? Will bubbles appear if you pour alcohol, glycerin, or kerosene into a jar together with a soap solution?

To answer these questions, it is enough to remember that when an electrical circuit is closed, electrolysis of water occurs, so that hydrogen is released at the cathode. But in alcohol, glycerin and kerosene, bubbles will not appear at all, since the electrolysis product is completely different.
