Other good definitions

Of course, definition 5 is not the only one. Definition 4 provided an equivalent option. Let's give a few more.

On the limit 6. Let $\Delta_n$ be a regular circumscribed $n$-gon, $Q_n$ its perimeter; the circumference is called $\lim_{n \to \infty} Q_n$.

The sequence $(Q_n)$ is, of course, decreasing.

About the limit 7. The length of a circle is the number $\lim_{n \to \infty} P_2^n$: a sequence of perimeters of regular inscribed $2^n$-gons is assumed ($\Pi_{2^2}$ is an inscribed square, $\Pi_{2^3}$ is a regular inscribed octagon, etc.).

In this case, the monotony of the sequence of parameters is very visible; however, the definition itself is quite artificial - it is not clear where $2^n$ came from, whether the same limit will be obtained for $\Pi_{3^n}$.

Stands apart

About the limit 8. The circumference of a circle is a number that is greater than the perimeter of any inscribed polygon and less than the perimeter of any circumscribed polygon.

This is a very good definition. Unfortunately, the proof of the existence of such a number relies on a theorem that is not in the school mathematics course. However, assuming that it exists, it is the only one that can be proven.

Definition is only the first step. Next we need to look at ways to calculate the number $n$, its properties - but this needs a separate discussion.

You can read a lot of interesting things about the number $n$ in the book by F. Campan “The History of the Number $n$” (M., “Nauka”, 1971).
