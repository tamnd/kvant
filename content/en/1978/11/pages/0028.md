The device can serve as a thermometer if the volumes of water $V_1$ and $V_2$, separated by a drop of mercury, depend on temperature.

Let us first consider whether $V_1$ depends on $T$ (and $V_2$ on $T$) when the device is positioned horizontally. If a drop of mercury is in equilibrium at a temperature of $T$, then the pressures to the left and right of it should be the same: $p_1 = p_2$. Let us denote by $m_1$ and $m_2$ the masses of water, respectively, to the left and right of the drop, and by $\mu$ the molar mass of water. Then

$$p_1 = \frac{m_1}{\mu V_1} RT, \quad p_2 = \frac{m_2}{\mu V_2} RT,$$

so for $p_1 = p_2$ we have:

$$\frac{m_1}{V_1} = \frac{m_2}{V_2},$$ or $$\frac{V_1}{V_2} = \frac{m_1}{m_2}.$$

We see that when the device is positioned horizontally, $V_1/V_2$ does not depend on $T$. The sum $V_1+V_2$ is also constant. Therefore, neither $V_1$ nor $V_2$ depends on temperature $^*$). The device cannot serve as a thermometer.

When the device is positioned vertically, the pressure under the drop must be higher than the pressure above it, so that the following condition is satisfied:

$$p_1s = p_2s + mg,$$

where $m$ is the mass of the drop, $s$ is the cross-sectional area of ​​the tube, $p_1$ and $p_2$ are the water pressure, respectively, below and above the drop. Substituting into (2) expressions (1) for $p_1$ and $p_2$, we obtain

$$\frac{m_1}{V_1} - \frac{m_2}{V_2} = \frac{mg\mu}{sRT}.$$

And since $V_1+V_2$ is const, in this case both $V_1$ and $V_2$ depend on temperature. The device can serve as a thermometer.
