V. Orlov

The big body paradox

When solving problems in which the interaction of a body with significantly different masses is considered, the change in the energy of a body with a larger mass is usually neglected. And this neglect often turns out to be correct. However, it is necessary to clearly understand when such an assumption is possible and when this cannot be done.

To clarify this issue, consider a number of problems formulated in the form of paradoxes.

Problem 1. A stone of mass $m$ falls to the Earth from a height $h$. 1) Let us write down the law of conservation of energy, considering the movement of the stone in the reporting system associated with the center of mass of the stone-Earth system. By the time the stone falls to the Earth, all its potential energy $mgh$ has transferred into kinetic energy $\frac{mv^2}{2}$:

$$mgh = \frac{mv^2}{2},$$

where $v$ is the speed of the stone before hitting the Earth.

2) Let us write down the law of conservation of energy, considering the movement in the reporting system associated with an elevator that moves down at a constant speed

In this system, the Earth has kinetic energy $\frac{Mv^2}{2}$ (M is the mass of the Earth), the stone at the initial moment had kinetic energy $\frac{mv^2}{2}$ and potential energy $mgh$. By the time the stone falls to the Earth, both its kinetic energy and its potential energy are equal to zero, so for all systems we can write:

$$\frac{Mv^2}{2} + \frac{mv^2}{2} + mgh = \frac{Mv^2}{2} \Rightarrow \frac{mv^2}{2} + mgh = 0.$$

It turns out that the law of conservation of energy is not fulfilled in the “elevator” system, but is fulfilled in the reporting system, relative to which the elevator moves at a constant speed. Paradox?!
