The approach of bubbles can be traced right up to their collision. After the bubbles collide, a repulsive force begins to act between them. It is due to the fact that in mutually pressed bubbles (Fig. 5) the gas pressure increases, which seems to expand the bubbles.

Apparently, soap bubbles are perfectly suitable for creating a crystal model if you place not one, not two, but many identical bubbles on the surface of a soap solution. If the radius of the bubble is $R=5 \cdot 10^{-2}$ cm, then $N \sim (R_T/R)^2 \sim 4 \cdot 10^4$ bubbles can be placed on the surface of the soap solution in an objective plate, the radius of which is $R_T=10$ cm! Such a raft, composed of bubbles between which attractive and repulsive forces act, is a two-dimensional model of a crystal. For example, the authors of this evaluation of a beautiful model showed that the interaction of bubbles with a radius of $R \sim 10^{-1}$ cm is very similar to the interaction of atoms in a copper crystal.

Using the BLN model, it is possible to verify some consequences of the theory constructed in relation to a crystal that is absolutely free from any defects, the so-called ideal crystal. It is practically impossible for an experimenter to obtain such a crystal in nature, but constructing it from bubbles turned out to be simple and accessible (Fig. 6).
