⟦folio 42⟧

You have probably come across arithmetic equalities more than once in which all or some numbers are replaced by letters or other symbols. In such problems, it is necessary to decipher the meaning of each symbol and restore the numerical record.

In India and China, this type of “mathematical entertainment” appeared more than 1000 years ago. In Europe, such problems began to be invented only at the beginning of the 20th century. In the difficult years of our century, such problems were called “cryptarthmetic” - cryptarithmie (French), criptarithmetic (English). In European and American magazines this name has become generally recognized. (In our literature, they are usually called “number puzzles” or “number puzzles.”) Some encryption rules have also been established: replace different numbers with different letters; avoid the letter O to replace zero; use one non-alphabetic character (“asterisk”) to replace any number. It is desirable that the problem has a unique solution.

As soon as they appear, cryptographic problems immediately become popular. Their compilers are showing increasing ingenuity and sophistication. For example, problems arise in which letters replacing numbers form words and even phrases. The famous English compiler of mathematical puzzles, G. E. Dueden, was the first to come up with such problems. The historical first were his two alphabet and arithmetic puzzles, published in 1924:

send + more = money; *)
(two)·(two) = three.

The first puzzle went around the world and gained a reputation as a classic in this genre.
