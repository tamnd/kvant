Where does some of the bullet's kinetic energy go? When a bullet suddenly decelerates, heat is generated; in other words, the energy of the bullet turns into heat: $|\Delta K| = Q$. And we can change the amount of heat released (a bullet, for example, hits the calorimeter exactly). However, from our reasoning it follows that if we make the same changes three times, we should get different results: $Q_1 \neq Q_2 \neq Q_3$. Paradox?!

One can think of many more similar problems in which “paradoxes” arise only because the systems of bodies under consideration are not closed, and in all reasoning this is not taken into account. In Problem 2 above, the system does not include such “large” bodies as the Earth. And in task 1, point 2), although it is included in the Earth system, the change in its energy is considered equal to zero. In paragraph 1) Earth energy does not appear at all, however, any reader will probably say that everything is written here correctly.

What's the matter? Why do we get completely correct results when choosing some reference systems, but paradoxes when choosing others? Which reference system should be chosen when solving the problem, so that only more mass can be neglected, thereby simplifying the solution of the problem? To answer these questions, let’s return to the “paradox” problems and carry out all the reasoning “absolutely strictly.”

Problem 1.1) In the reference system associated with the center of mass of the stone-Earth system of bodies, at the initial moment the Earth will end and the entire energy of the system is equal to the potential energy of the stone. By the time the stone falls to the Earth, the energy of the system is equal to $\frac{mv^2}{2} + \frac{Mu^2}{2}$, where $\vec{v}$ is the speed that the stone has traveled under the influence of the Earth’s gravitational force, and $\vec{u}$ is the speed that the Earth has traveled under the influence of the stone’s gravitational force. Let's find the speed $\vec{u}$, use the law of conservation of momentum:

$$m|\vec{v}| - M|\vec{u}| = 0,$$

where

$$|\vec{u}| = \frac{m}{M}|\vec{v}|.$$

Now let’s write down the law of conservation of energy of the system:

$$mgh = \frac{mv^2}{2} + \frac{M|\vec{v}|^2}{2} = \frac{mv^2}{2}\left(1 + \frac{m}{M}\right).$$

This entry is “absolutely strict.” However, it is clear that in all implementations the problem (about the fall of bodies on Earth) $m \ll M$ and the value $\frac{m}{M}$ is nothing small compared to 1, so it can be neglected. Then in the reference system associated with the center of mass of the stone-Earth body system, the law of conservation of energy will be written as follows:

$$mgh = \frac{mv^2}{2}.$$

2) In the “elevator” reference frame, the naal energy of the stone-Earth system is equal to $mgh + \frac{mv^2}{2} + \frac{Mu^2}{2}$, at the moment the stone falls, the energy of the system is equal to $\frac{Mu^2}{2}$, where $\vec{u}$ is the speed.
