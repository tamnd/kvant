11. Möbius also proposes to prove that if the areas (oriented!) of the four-vertex $ABCD$, $BCDE$, $CDEA$, $DEAB$, $EABC$ are equal, respectively, $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\gamma$, $\delta$, $\varepsilon$, then the area $x$ quintuple $ABCD$ is the root of a quadratic equation
$$x^2 - x(\alpha + \beta + \gamma + \delta + \varepsilon) + (\alpha \beta + \beta \gamma + \gamma \delta + \delta \varepsilon + \varepsilon \alpha) = 0$$
(“the same,” says Möbius, as equation 3).

The reader checks this proof independently. It will be more difficult to solve the “continuation” of the Möbius problem:

Prove that if the areas of oriented triangles
$$ACD, BDE, CEA, DAB, EBC$$ are equal (respectively) $p, q, r, s, t$, then the area $x$ of the pentad $ABCD$ is the root of the quadratic equation
$$x^2 = (p + q + r + s + t)^2 - 4(pr + rt + tq + qs + sp).$$

12. Let us finally pay attention to the fact that both the Möbius problem and its “continuation” are special cases of such a problem.

On the sides $AB, BC, \dots$ of the oriented five-vertex $ABCD$ the points $A', B', C', D', E'$ are taken so that $AA' = \lambda AB; BB' = \lambda BC; \dots$. It is required to construct an equation that will satisfy the area $x$ of the pentagon $ABCD$, if the areas of triangles $ABCDE$, $BCD', \dots$ are given, equal to $a', b', c', d', e'$, respectively. (In the case when $\lambda = 0$ is the Möbius problem; if $\lambda = 1$ is its “continuation”).

A completely unexpected answer is obtained for the case when $\lambda = 1/3$; here it is:
$$x = \frac{3}{5}(a' + b' + c' + d' + e').$$

It will seem even more unexpected that for an arbitrary given $\lambda$ (not, however, equal to $1/3$) the problem has a surprising... ugly answer:
$$x^2(1 - \lambda - \lambda^2) - x(a' + b' + c' + d' + e')(1 - \lambda) + (a' + b' + c' + d' + e')^2\left[\frac{1 - 2\lambda + 2\lambda^2}{(2 - \lambda)^2} - \frac{(1 - \lambda)^2(1 - 3\lambda)^2}{[(1 - \lambda)^5 + 1](2 - \lambda)} \right] + [(1 - \lambda)(a'b' + b'c' + c'd' + d'e') + e'a') - \lambda^2(a'c' + c'e' + e'b' + b'd') + d'a')] \frac{(2 - \lambda)(1 - 3\lambda)^2}{(1 - \lambda)^5 + 1} = 0.$$

It may be a consolation that for $\lambda = 0$ and for $\lambda = 1$ this formula still leads to equations (3) and (6). An additional incentive for deriving formula (7) will be (for the real reader!) the fact that it provides a solution to the following problem: at what value of the number $\lambda$ (except for $\lambda = 1/3$) the area of ​​the pentagon $A'B'C'D'E'$ is uniquely determined by specifying the areas $a', b', c', d', e'$?

And finally, is it possible to solve this last problem without first deriving the “ugly” formula?
