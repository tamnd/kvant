Irreversibility of thermal phenomena and statistics

This article is a chapter from the book “The Cost of Matter,” written by the famous Soviet physicist and remarkable popularizer of science M. S. Bronstein. This book was published in 1936. The text is printed with minor changes.

We call tell phenomena all those phenomena that occur with macroscopic bodies, in which we can no longer limit ourselves to the distance of movement of the macroscopic body as a whole, but must also distance the movements of the smallest parts of this body relative to each other, i.e., in the final case, take into account the atomic structure of the body; Thus, for example, the expansion of a gas, the deformation of a metal rod, etc., in this sense of the word, we call the tell phenomena, along with the heating of a gas enclosed in a vessel of constant volume. The law of conservation of energy, which represents one of the basic principles of mechanics, is also valid in the field of thermal phenomena; This represents serious evidence of the view that a macroscopic body can be interpreted as a mechanical system consisting of atoms, i.e., in other words, that “the body is motion.” That is why the law of conservation of energy is called the principle of the mechanical theory of the body (instead of the expression “mechanical theory of the body,” the shorter name “thermodynamics” is even more often used).

When we try to apply the principles of mechanics to a macroscopic body consisting of a huge number of atoms, we encounter two significant difficulties from the very beginning. The first of these difficulties is purely practical. It lies in the fact that in order to solve the problem of increasing a mechanical system consisting, for example, of 10²⁵ particles, it is necessary to write and then solve the same huge number of equations; If we got the hang of writing one equation per second, then it would take us billions of billions of years to write such a system of equations.

Examining thermal phenomena carefully, we see that the above-described point of view on the theory of the body as the mechanics of a system consisting of a huge number of particles also encounters another difficulty, no longer of a practical, but of a deeper and more fundamental nature. To try this difficulty, consider the following example. Suppose that we are given a closed vessel, divided into two equal parts by a valve (Fig. 1).

Experience shows that the gas initially placed in part I will expand into the adjacent void and be distributed throughout the vessel with uniform density. This phenomenon occurs as if “by itself”: this means that from the moment in time when the shutter is pulled out, the gas is a closed system, which we monitor from the outside, in no way together

No matter how much we monitor the gas in the future, it will always remain distributed with the same density throughout the entire container; no matter how much we waited, we were unable to observe that the gas, distributed throughout the vessel $I + II$, “by itself,” i.e., without any outside intervention, left part $II$ and concentrated entirely in part $I$, so that we would be able to screw in the damper again and thereby return to the original state. From this it is clear that the process of gas expansion into emptiness is an irreversible process: this means that although it itself can occur in a closed system without any outside intervention, the reverse process (transition of all the gas into part $I$ so that a void is formed in part $II$) “by itself” will not occur. Experience shows that warm phenomena almost always have this property of “irreversibility”: for example, if there are two bodies next to each other, one of which is hotter than the other, then their temperature gradually equalizes, that is, heat “by itself” flows from the warmer body to the colder one; however, the reverse process will never occur without any outside intervention. A piece of sugar placed in hot tea dissolves in it, but it never happens that from hot tea in which a piece of sugar has already been dissolved, the latter will separate out and again become a whole piece. Any number of examples of the same irreversibility can be given. Let us now try to figure out whether this irreversibility can be explained based on the principles of mechanics. Let us consider some movement that occurs in accordance with these principles in some closed mechanical system (i.e., under constant external conditions). Let the phenomenon consist in the fact that we throw a stone from some point $A$, which ultimately falls at point $B$. Let us assume for simplicity that the stone is moving in a vacuum (otherwise we would have to take into account the resistance of air to the flight of the stone, and this resistance is a thermal phenomenon; Since we want to compare thermal phenomena with such phenomena in which the applicability of the principles of mechanics is a basic and undoubted experimental fact, we must exclude in advance all thermal phenomena in our imaginary mechanical experience). Is the flight of the stone from $A$ to $B$ reversed? Undoubtedly, since the reverse process is very easy to carry out. To do this, you just need to stand at point $B$ and throw a stone, giving it the same absolute speed as it fell to point $B$, but directed in the opposite direction. From experience and from the principles of mechanics it follows that in this case the stone will hit exactly the same point that it described earlier, but in the opposite direction, and will fall at point $A$. The example discussed is usefully only part of the case of the general principle of mechanics, which states that if any movement can occur in a closed mechanical system, then the “reverse” movement can also occur. In order to carry out the reverse movement, it is enough just to leave all parts of our system at some point in time and then impart to them velocities equal in absolute values, but directly opposite in direction to the velocities that they have immediately before stopping. Thus, all mechanical phenomena are inverse. Why are warm events irreversible?

At first glance, it seems that the irreversibility of thermal processes is a result of mechanics, that all movements are reverse, so we might already doubt the validity of the statement that “the body is movement.” The contradiction we just talked about was debunked only in the second half of the 19th century by the German theorist Ludwig Boltzmann. Bolshan proved that this contradiction is only apparent.

To understand Bolshoi's idea, consider the following example: suppose that we are given a horizontal polished platform surrounded on all sides by barBEROM, and that on this platform there is a smoothly polished ball that slides along the platform, reflecting off barBERA when it comes into contact with it. The path of the ball over a long period of time will be a complex and intricate broken line. Let us assume that the entire surface of the platform is white, with the exception of only a few black spots applied to it. These spots can be of any shape, round, winding, elongated, etc., with the only condition that the total surface of all spots taken together constitutes an insignificant fraction of the surface of the entire platform. As the ball moves along the platform, it will only occasionally enter the territory of the spots. If we follow the movement of the ball for a sufficiently long period of time, we will find that it will be on the territory of the spots for only an insignificant fraction of this period of time, equal to the relative total area of ​​​​the black spots to the area of ​​the entire platform. This is because the movement of the ball is so confused and tortuous that it will pass through each square millimeter of the surface of the platform exactly as many times as through any other, if only we follow the movement of the ball long enough for such an equalization to occur. The ratio of the long stay of the ball on the black field for a given sufficiently large period of time to this period of time itself is called the *probability* of the ball staying in the territory of the black spots. This probability is very small, and the following statements are connected with this: if the position and speed of the ball at a given moment are not given to me with real accuracy so that I can calculate its movement for the next 10 seconds, then I can always say with a fairly high probability that after

For 10 seconds the ball will be on white, not black, territory. If anyone wants to make a pledge with me, then I can vouch for this statement with a large sum of money against a small pledge on the other side. Therefore, I have the right to make the following two statements:

1) if it is known that the ball is currently on a black field, then it is very likely that in 10 seconds it will be on a white field;

2) If it is known that the ball is currently on a white field, then it is very likely that in 10 seconds it will be on a black field.

These two statements can be replaced by the following brief formulation: the transition of a ball from a black field to a white field is very probable, but its transition from a white field to a black field is unlikely. If the ratio of the area of ​​black spots to the area of ​​the entire platform is really extremely small, for example, equal to one billionth, then the ball will be in the black territory for an average of one second for thirty years (thirty years is about a billion seconds). Therefore, for a number of years, perhaps, we generally do not worry about the ball ending up in black territory, unless we deliberately place it on one of the spots from the very beginning. (In our example, we are everywhere aware of the device for calculating the path of the ball from the direction of its movement; for this it is enough to assume that the ball receives its initial speed, for example, when the platform is accidentally shaken up and not under our control. Therefore, although we can direct the ball at the beginning of its movement to the black spot, we cannot make it get there subsequently.)

So, given the probability of the ball being on a black field, it is practically impossible for us to observe the transition of a ball from a white field to a black field, although it is very easy for us to make its transition from a black field: for this it is enough

We can therefore assert that thermal phenomena always occur in such a way that the probability of the state increases; reversal of thermal phenomena associated with a decrease in the probability of the state is impossible. This principle is called “multiple beginning thermodynamics” (the first principle, as we have already said, is the law of conservation of energy).

Let us turn to our consideration of the example of a gas in a vessel. We will be able to distinguish different states from each other by the number $x$, i.e. the number of molecules in the part $I$. The probability of a state with a given $x$, as we have seen, is equal to

$$\frac{N!}{x!(N-x)!2^N}.$$

Since we are only interested in the ratio of the probabilities of different states, we can reduce all probabilities to the same multiplier $\frac{N!}{2^N}$, which does not contain $x$ and is therefore the same for all states. What remains is the expression

$$\frac{1}{x!(N-x)!},$$

which is proportional to the probability of the state; it is called the “statistical weight” of a given state. The second law of thermodynamics can be formulated as follows: all thermal phenomena in a closed system occur in such a way that the statistical weight of the state returns. If the container with gas in question is divided not into two, but into a larger number of equal parts, the expression for the statistical weight can be written in a similar way. For example, if a vessel is divided into three parts and the first of them contains $x$, the second $y$, and the third - $z$ molecules ($x+y+z=N$), then the statistical weight of such a state is equal to

$$\frac{1}{x!y!z!}.$$

If a vessel is divided into $n+m$ equal parts containing $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n, y_1, y_2, \dots, y_m$ molecules, then the statistical weight is

$$\frac{1}{x_1!x_2! \dots x_n!y_1!y_2! \dots y_m!}.$$

Let the first $n$ of these parts turn into what we will call part $I$, the remaining $m$ parts turn into part $II$ (parts $I$ and $II$ are no longer equal to each other). Then the statistical weight of the part $I$, considered

This shows that the statistical weight of the entire gas is equal to the product of the statistical weights of its part $1$ and $11$.

And since the logarithm of the product is equal to the sum of the logarithms of the individual factors, then, consequently, the logarithm of the statistical weight of the entire gas is equal to the sum of the logarithms of the statistical weights of its part, i.e., the logarithm of the statistical weight has the property of aditivity. Thanks to this simple and important property, the logarithm of statistical weight received a special name - it is called entropy. Since the logarithm of a number is larger, the larger the number itself, then along with the increase in probability and statistical weight, entropy should also increase. Therefore, the second law of thermodynamics can be formulated as follows: there is a physical quantity called entropy, which has the property of aditivity and the property that the entropy of a closed system cannot decrease in any way, but can only increase or remain constant (when it has already reached its maximum). Note that in this formulation, the second law of thermodynamics was known from wholesale with tell phenomena long before Bolshman (about the seventies centuries passed) discovered the true meaning of entropy as the logarithm of a quantity proportional to the probability of a state. The first imperfect indication of the second law of thermodynamics can be found in the work of the French physicist Sadi Carnot (1824), and the formulation just given was clearly stated by Clausis (1850) and William Thomson (recalled by Lord Kelvin) (1851). Bolshman proved with the example of gases that the second law of thermodynamics, together with its characteristic irreversibility, is a consequence mechanics, and the American theorist Willard Gibbs was able to generalize this proof to any macroscopic bodies, constructing a mechanical theory of a system consisting of a huge number of parts (the so-called statistical mechanics).

The second law of thermodynamics, as we see, states that the entropy of a closed system invariably increases, tending to a maximum; reach this maximum, it will no longer be able to change further, as a result of which thermal phenomena associated with an increase in entropy will cease to occur. This state of the system, in which entropy reaches its maximum value, is called statistical equilibrium. The transition of a system without any shaking from a state of statistical equilibrium to a state that is very different from it, as we have seen, is practically impossible, since in order for it to take place, one must wait for truly miraculous periods of time. This, however, does not apply to transitions from a state of statistical equilibrium to a state that differs very little from it, that is, in a state in which entropy is at its maximum value by a very negligible value.

The system will spontaneously transition into such states completely freely, which is extremely characteristic of the second law of thermodynamics as a law that has a statistical meaning (statisticalism is called any law, the absence of which is not absolutely impossible, but only extremely unlikely). Since such absences are less probable the greater they are, more absences from the second law of thermodynamics will be almost absolutely impossible in practice; As for small absences, you can always find so small that they will not only be quite probable, but will actually almost always occur.
