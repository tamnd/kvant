M462. We intersect the plane with the lateral edges of a regular quadrangular paramid at points spaced from the vertex at distances a, b, c and d (Fig. 1). Prove that

$$\frac{1}{a} + \frac{1}{c} = \frac{1}{b} + \frac{1}{d}.$$

M463. Given natural numbers $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_m, y_1, y_2, \dots, y_n$. The sums $x_1 + x_2 + \dots + x_m$ and $y_1 + y_2 + \dots + y_n$ are equal to each other and less than mn. Prove that in the equality $x_1 + x_2 + \dots + x_m = y_1 + y_2 + \dots + y_n$ you can cross out some of the max terms to get a true equality again.

$$4 + 5 + 5 = 1 + 1 + 6 + 6 \quad (\text{неЛЬЗЯ}),$$
$$3 + 3 + 4 + 5 = 1 + 1 + 6 + 7 \quad (\text{МОЖНО})$$

Let us denote by $\alpha$ the angles formed by the height of the paramid with the side edges, by $A, B, C, D$ the intersection points of the cutting plane with the side edges and the height, by $H$ the vertex of the paramid (Fig. 1). It is clear that there must be some correspondence between the lengths $a, b, c$ and $d$: after all, a single plane passes through the three points $A, B, C$, therefore $d$ can be expressed through $a, b$ and $c$. Such a correspondence can be obtained by expressing the length of the common bisector $HK$ in each of the triangles $AHC$ and $BHD$ through the angle $\alpha$ and the lengths of their sides: $a, c$ (in one) and $b, d$ (in the other) and equating the results. This is exactly how you get a triangular equality.

In order to obtain the required formula for the length of the bisector $l = |HK|$ in the triangle $AHC$, we use the fact that $S(AHC) = S(AHK) + S(KHC)$ (Fig. 2). Multiplying by 2, we get

$$ac \sin 2\alpha = al \sin \alpha + cl \sin \alpha,$$

where

$$\frac{2\cos \alpha}{l} = \frac{1}{a} + \frac{1}{c}.$$

Similarly from triangle $BHC$

$$\frac{2\cos \alpha}{l} = \frac{1}{b} + \frac{1}{d}.$$

From here

$$\frac{1}{a} + \frac{1}{c} = \frac{1}{b} + \frac{1}{d}.$$

N. Vasiliev

We will carry out the proof by induction on $k = m + n$. Since $x_1 + x_2 + \dots + x_m \geq m, y_1 + y_2 + \dots + y_n \geq n$, then mn > m, mn > n, open $m \geq 2, n \geq 2, k \geq 4$. If $k = 4$, that is, $m = n = 2, x_1 + x_2 = y_1 + y_2 < 4$, then at least one of the numbers $x_1, x_2$ is equal to 1 and at least one of the numbers $y_1, y_2$ is equal to 1. So for $k = 4$ the statement of the problem is true. Let us assume that the statement has been proven for $m = n = k$ and prove it for $m + n = k + 1$.

Let $x_1$ be the most of the numbers $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_m, y_1$ be the most of the numbers $y_1, \dots, y_n$ ($m + n = k + 1$). If $x_1 = y_1$, then cross out $x_1$ and $y_1$. Let $x_1 > y_1$. Distance equality

$$(x_1 - y_1) + x_2 + \dots + x_m = y_2 + \dots + y_n,$$

(*) containing all $m + n - 1 = k$ terms. To bring an induction proposition to it, we need to check that $y_2 + \dots + y_n < m(n - 1)$. Let's put $S = y_1 + y_2 + \dots + y_n$. Then $y_1 \geq \frac{S}{n}$ and therefore

$$y_2 + \dots + y_n < S - \frac{S}{n} = S \cdot \frac{n - 1}{n}.$$

Since $S < mn$, then $y_2 + \dots + y_n < mn \cdot \frac{n - 1}{n} = m(n - 1)$.

By crossing out some of the terms in the equality (*) so that the correct equality is again obtained (this can be done by the induction hypothesis); we get either exclude or an equality containing the difference $x_1 - y_1$ on the left side. In the latter case, you need to transfer $y_1$ to the correct part.
