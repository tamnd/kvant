Much more interesting is the experiment with green glass or a vessel filled with chlorophyll solution. In these cases, the spectrum of the remaining status is no longer one, but two stripes: green and dark red. This means that green glass and chlorophyll solution transmit not only green, but also red rays.

Regarding chlorophyll, the remarks of the famous Russian botanist K. A. Timirzev are very interesting: “You can very easily make sure that chlorophyll transmits red rays: it is worth placing it on a landscape flooded with bright sunlight through a special blue glass (Fig. 3), which transmits red and blue rays, but retains green ones, so that before our studied eyes all nature is completely transformed - under the general photograph in the sky we will see blood-red vegetation. Is it not this peculiarity of the color of chlorophyll that lies behind the difficulties with which, very much, landscape painting has to struggle? On the painter’s palette, apparently, there are no those green tones that brightly lit greenery represents up close.”

Let us, however, leave painting aside for now and return to the natural wedge. We see a little bit about the filament of an incandescent lamp and between it and we recognize a pometiric wedge (Fig. 4). On the wall we again see two stripes - green and red, and the correspondence of these stripes will depend on the thickness of the wedge at the point where the light beam passes. If the beam passes through the thin part of the wedge, the green stripe is much brighter than the red one. As the thickness of the wedge increases, the brightness of both stripes becomes brighter, and, starting from a certain moment, the red stripe becomes brighter than the green one. When the green stripe is brighter than the red one, the thread is visible green; when the stripes correspond inversely, the thread is visible red. When the brightness of the stripes is equal, each thread is colorless.

It's as if the mystery of green glass has been solved. However, it remains to be explained why, with increasing glass thickness, the correspondence between the brightness of the red and green stripes is reversed. It turns out that the explanation follows from an important law of optics, discovered by one brave sailor years ago.

Sea captain and geometric progression

French sea captain Pierre Bouguer, who lived in the first half of the eighteenth century, was not, perhaps, a simple sailor. He wrote extensive treatises on ship design, navigation and other branches of maritime affairs. The French Academy of Sciences awarded Bouguer three prizes for his work in maritime affairs and elected him as a member. Buger inherited his taste for marine sciences from his father, a professor of hydrology.

If Buger was involved in the sea by inheritance, then he took up optics on his own initiative. Booger was the first to draw attention to the problems associated with changes in light and illumination. He invented the first instruments for measuring the light of light and established that the dreams of light from the Sun are 300 thousand times greater than the dreams of light from the Moon, and his “Optical Treatise” contained a very important law of attenuation of light in absorbing bodies.
