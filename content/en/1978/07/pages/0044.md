It turns out that light, like waves on the surface of water, is able to bend around obstacles encountered along its path.

Let's talk a little about waves on the water. As you know, large and small waves bend around obstacles differently. If, for example, such a thing sticks out of the water, then small waves (ripples) form a noticeable “shadow” (an area of ​​calm water surface) behind the table. Large waves do not cast a “shadow” in practice; they bend around obstacles well.

What do “small” and “large” waves mean? Why do they behave differently when faced with an obstacle? There is such a thing as wavelength. This is the distance between two adjacent crests (or rotations) of a wave. So, small waves have a much shorter wavelength compared to large ones. And the longer the wavelength (compared to the obstacle), the stronger the wave bends around the obstacle.

The same thing happens with light waves: if there is an obstacle on the path of light, the light bends around it. That's why you saw a beam of light emanating from the hole in the foil.

How did the multi-colored rings appear? The thing is that white light consists of rays of different colors. Following Newton, it is customary to distinguish seven colors: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo and violet. Each color has its own wavelength of light. The longest wavelength is for red light (≈ 7·10⁻⁷ m), and the shortest is for violet light (≈ 4·10⁻⁷ m). Now everything is clear to you, isn’t it?
