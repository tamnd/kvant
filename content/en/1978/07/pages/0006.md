Fig. 4. Everyday life not done by Newton. When white light passes through a pyrometric wedge, green and red rays will remain in the spectrum.

The battles are really a wedge of green glass, the thickness of which gradually increases from one end to the other. The wedge moves in a metal mandrel with deflection to observe the incandescent body. A temperature scale is marked along the edge of the wedge, with the temperature increasing from the thin end of the wedge to the thick one. Having pointed the rejection of the mandrel at the hot body, it is necessary to move the wedge in the mandrel until a change in the visible color of the body occurs. Then on the scale against which is indicated, connected to the mandrel, you can read the temperature of the hot body.

A pyrometric wedge is especially often used to determine the temperature of molten metal, for example, in open-hearth furnaces. Despite its simple design, the wedge in experienced hands gives high accuracy.

You have become acquainted with the principle of operation of a useful device, the properties of green glass used, but the mystery of the glass itself remains a mystery.

Everyday life not made by Newton and landscape painting

Probably, many of you remember Newton’s landmark experiment with the decomposition of a solar ray into a multi-colored spectrum using a glass prism. This experiment showed that sunlight is a mixture of rays of different colors: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo and violet. For some reason, Newton did not try to somewhat condition this experiment: to place colored glass or a vessel with a colored liquid in the path of the sun's ray. In any case, Newton does not describe such an experience in his writings.

Living with red glass, in fact, would not have given anything interesting. Instead of a multi-colored strip of the spectrum, only the section corresponding to the red rays would remain. The result could have been predicted in advance: red glass is red because it transmits only red rays and absorbs all others.
