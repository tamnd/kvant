⟦folio 41⟧

A. Dozorov

Optics without optics

In the last issue of our magazine, we advised you to check through experiments whether you know the lens well. Now we invite you to try a few more experiments in optics, but without special optical instruments.

1. Take the middle chocolate candy wrapper. Flatten it and poke a small wrap through it. Through this wrapping, placing it as close to the eye as possible, look at some brightly lit text, also placed close to the eye. Did you manage to see the greatly enlarged letters? It turns out that not only the lens can magnify!

Which image of the letter did you see—straight or inverted? It would seem that the letter should be upside down (look at figure 1) - on the retina, all images always appear upside down. But our brain “knows” about this and is “used to” it: we perceive objects around us upside down.

In opaque paper, pierce the wrap with a needle. In a darkened room, before the rejection, light a candle, and on the other side of the rejection, place a white sheet of paper - a screen. On the screen you will see a clear reverse image of the candle. This simple device is a model of the so-called camera obscura (observation camera), which has been used since ancient times to obtain various images. The camera obscura is the simplest camera.

2. The fact that the brain really “reverses” the image of an object can be verified by such a simple experiment. Between the rejection in the foil (in the middle piece of paper) and the eye, place a small object - the point of a pencil or a pinhead. The light from rejection creates an uninverted image of the object on the retina (Fig. 2). And the brain “by inertia” will turn this image upside down.
