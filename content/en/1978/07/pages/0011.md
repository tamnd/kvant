A. Gein

On the way to a solution

Sometimes a small change or generalization of the conditions of a school problem can lead to interesting theorems and even unsolved problems. Thus, several years ago, V. I. Arnold proposed the problem: “Describe all sets of $n$ natural numbers such that the product of any $n-1$ of them in sum with one is divisible by the remainder.” We also wrote about this problem in «Kvant» («Kvant», 1976, No. 12, p. 5). It comes from a modern school problem: find all triples of numbers different from one such that the product of any two numbers of the triple, added to one, is divisible by the third number (“Collection of problems of Moscow mathematical optimization”, M., “Prosveshchenie”, 1965, p. 70, problem 215). The answer contains a single set: (2, 3, 7). If we drop the requirement that all numbers be different from one, we get three more sets: (1, 1, 1), (1, 1, 2) and (1, 2, 3). It’s the same amount to describe all such pairs and quadruples of numbers. But it’s impossible to give an answer for arbitrary $n$ - this is still an unsolved problem.

In the wonderful book by D. Polya “How to Solve a Problem,” the “rules of research” are formulated. Let's list some of them.

Start with special cases - they may turn out to be easier. Review your results. Observe, generalize, check.

Analyze the solution method, break it down and see which parts of it can be intersected in the general case.

Approach the problem from different angles, examine different details; examine the same details, but from different points of view, try to see in each detail some new interpretation of the problem. Look for points of contact with your previously acquired knowledge. Try to see the familiar in what you explore and the useful in what turns out to be familiar.

Take a closer look at the method. Try to highlight the main thing in it. Try to improve it.

Let's try to use the “rules of research” and carefully look at what is known about problems with $n=2, 3$ and 4; Maybe this will help us get at least a little closer to solving the common problem.
