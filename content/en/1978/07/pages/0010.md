Old Russian inkly and observations of Leonardo da Vinci

On some ancient Russian inklets, the unusual pattern of the saints’ robes is striking. The folds are painted with paint that has a sharply different color from the color of the smooth parts of the robe. For example, red folds on a green robe (Fig. 6) or orange folds on a blue robe. The keen eye of the ancient Russian rich man noticed that non-woven fabrics have two colors and in the folds acquire a different color than on the flat surface. The reason for the two-color fabric is the same as in the experiment with a metric wedge.

If a beam of light reflected from a two-color fabric is passed through a prism, then two colored stripes will definitely remain in the spectrum. For green two-color fabric, the picture will be the same as with green glass: red and green stripes will remain, the rest of the rays will be absorbed.

Two-color green fabric reflects red rays better than green ones, but with a single reflection from the smooth surface of the fabric, the green rays are more bright in the incident light. Therefore, in the once reflected light, green rays are still present.

In the folds of fabric, light experiences at least two successive reflections. During the second reflection, the red rays are again reflected stronger than the green ones, and as a result of double reflection, the same thing happens as in thick green glass: the brightness of the red rays becomes greater than the brightness of the green rays, and the fabric changes color. Multiple reflections will hear this effect.

Most ordinary fabrics have exactly the opposite properties. The folds produce a more saturated, but the same color tone as the flat surface. This is explained again by repeated reflections. Light reflected from such fabrics, after decomposition of the characteristics, gives only one stripe in the spectrum of two stripes in two-color fabrics. For example, light, once reflected from yellow velvet, produces a broad band in the spectrum with the brightest part in the yellow part. In addition, the spectrum also contains green and blue rays. With double reflection, the band in the spectrum becomes narrower, since the blue rays practically disappear completely, and the green rays are greatly weakened. The same law of geometric progression works. As a result, the yellow-orange reflected light becomes more saturated.

One of the most remarkable geniuses who ever lived, Leonardo da Vinci, not only noticed this feature of the folds of fabric with his artist’s eye, but also, as a scientist, gave a completely correct explanation of the observed phenomenon. In his Treatise on Painting, he writes: “Reflected colors have a much greater beauty than the natural color of these bodies, as can be seen in the opening folds of golden fabric... when one surface is reflected in another, facing direction, and this one in it, and so on successively ad infinitum.”

The same “Treatise on Painting” says: “Reflexions (reflections) from a living body receiving light from another living body are redder and more perfectly telephone in color than any other part of a living body that a person can have.” Contemplating with pleasure the amazing paintings of Van Dyck and Rubens in the halls of the Leningrad Hermitage, it is easy to notice that even for these great masters the effect of multiple reflections was not a secret.
