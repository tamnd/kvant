In this article we will look at the sequence

1, 2, 5, 14, 42, 132, 429, ...
having the number 577 in the directory. The members of this sequence are called Catalan numbers. They are not as well known as Fibonacci numbers, but they have the same charming property of appearing unexpectedly in the most unexpected places, especially in combinatorial problems. In 1971, a mathematician from the University of Western Europe. Virginia Henry Gould published a bibliography containing 243 references to the use of Catalan numbers; in many cases, the authors of the papers had no idea that they were dealing with a sequence that had been known for more than two centuries. In 1976, Gould increased the number of references to 450. In general, Catalan numbers were a frequent sequence; nevertheless, it is sufficiently unknown to force a mathematician, deprived of access to Sloan's reference book, to spend more than a number on rediscovering formulas derived many years ago.

Leonhard Euler was the first to encounter Catalan numbers. He calculated how many ways a convex polygon can be divided into triangles without intersecting diagonals. An example is the case of triangle, square, pentagon and hexagon (Fig. 1). Note that in each of these cases, regardless of the number of sides of the $n$-gon, the number of diagonals is $n-3$, and the number of triangles is $n-2$. It is easy to prove that this correspondence holds in general for all polygons.
