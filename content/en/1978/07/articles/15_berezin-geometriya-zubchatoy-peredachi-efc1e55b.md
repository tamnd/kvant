Gear geometry

And what kind of mechanisms do not have gears? And they are in the watches, and in the engine room of the corps, and in the presses...

How does drive gear A transmit motion to driven gear B? Let us turn to figure 1: the “lever A₁” rigidly connected to A is only the “lever B₁” rigidly connected to B and thereby turns B.

Are straight tooth profiles good? It's immediately obvious that it's not very good. First, even if drive gear A rotates evenly, the same cannot be said about driven gear B (why?), and if gear B, in turn, transmits motion to gears C, D, etc., then the unevenness of the transmitted movement can be heard. Secondly, the angle φ between the cylinder F, which drives gear A, and the direction of rotation of gear B is variable. Therefore, only the driven gear is variably stronger than F₁, and the teeth of both gears wear unevenly.

Let's turn to figure on the fourth cover page. It shows a diagram of the operation of an oil pump operating with the help of two somewhat unusual gears (each with only two teeth). The profile of the head of each tooth is very epicycliodic, the profile of the stem is hypocycliodic (these two curves were described, for example, in «Kvant», 1978, No. 4, p. 13.). figure 2 shows how such a profile can be drawn. A circle $\omega$ of radius $R/4$ rolls along a fixed circle (O, R) from the outside. Then point $M \in \omega$ describes the profile of the gear tooth head (part of the epicycliode). Circle $\omega'$, congruent to $\omega$, rolls along (O, R) inside. Point $M' \in \omega'$ describes the heads of two adjacent teeth (often hypocycliodes).

figure 3 shows the position of the two gears in which the tooth head of the lower gear touches the profile of the upper one in the middle of the space between its two teeth. If the top gear is rotated by θ clockwise, and the bottom gear is rotated counterclockwise by the same angle, then they will still have a common point K and, moreover, a common tangent at it. Try to show this and establish the following important property of such a pair of gears: the normal to the profiles of the teeth at the point of their contact passes unchanged through a fixed point P (independent of the position of the point of contact).

Having made a model of such a pair of gears, you will notice its interesting property. In order for the pair to work continuously, the gears must become driving alternately. This means that this pair of gears cannot act as a mechanism that transmits motion. It is not for nothing that they are used to pump oil, and two auxiliary gears of a different type are used to transmit movement from one axis to another (see figure on the cover). Of course, if you make gears with a large number of teeth in a cyclondal profile, you can achieve transmission of motion from the drive gear to the driven gear. But other shortcomings remain. Such gears require, in particular, very precise installation so that the epicyclondal profile is always in contact with the hypocyclondal one. In addition, cyclondal teeth are difficult to cut.

Therefore, in practice, gears are usually used whose tooth profiles are cut along the evolutes of the circle. To explain what this is, we introduce two concepts: the center of curvature of the curve γ

and the radius of curvature of the curve γ at a given point.

Let γ be a smooth curve, that is, a continuously turning curve, without kinks and pointed. A tangent and a normal can be drawn to such a curve at each point in a similar manner. Let $M_0 \in \gamma$ be a fixed point, point $M \in \gamma$ a variable, α the angle between the tangents to γ ​​at points $M_0$ and $M$ (Fig. 4). The angle between the tangents is concomitant with the angle between the normal ones to γ ​​at the same points. Let's direct the point $M$ to $M_0$. Then the intersection point of the normal $N$ will go to the limit position $N_0$. This point $N_0$ is called the center of curvature of the curve γ at the point $M_0$, the length of the segment $M_0N_0$ is called the radius of curvature $ρ_0$ of the curve γ at the point $M_0$. For each point $M_0$ of a smooth curve γ, the center of curvature $N_0$ and the radius of curvature $ρ_0$ are uniquely determined.

The set γ₁ of centers of curvature of a curve γ is called its evolute (translated from Latin as “developing curve”). The curve γ in relation to its evolute γ₁ is called its evolute (translated from Latin as “sweep”). For example, the evolution of a circle (O, |OT₀|) is a point - the center of the circle O (Fig. 5). Shown here is a curve whose evolute is the circle γ, that is, its evolute; it is constructed as a result of rolling without sliding along a circle γ of a non-tortic straight line l with a displaced point Q; this point describes the involution of the circle γ; on figure 5 the positions Q₁, Q₂, Q₃, ..., corresponding to the positions of the point of tangency T₁, T₂, T₃, ... of the straight line l with the circle, are removed. Let us make sure that the evolution of the curve γ is indeed a circle at one of these points, say, T₄, the straight line at this moment rotates around T₄; from here we can see that the angle T₄Q₄R₄ is right. Consider the normals to γ ​​at the points Q₃ and Q₄ and direct the point Q₃ to the point Q₄. It is clear that in the limit of intersection of these normals will lie on the circle (O, |OT₀|) at point T₄, which is required.

It is according to such involutions of circles that the profiles of star gears are cut.

The involute of a circle intersects the tangent to this circle at a right angle; in part, so it also intersects the common tangent of the two circles shown in figure 6. Let us imagine that gears with tooth profiles cut along the involutions A₁ and A₂ are mounted on these circles. Then the driving gear A₁ only pushes the driven gear A₂ along T₁T₂ in the direction of the common normal to γ ​​and γ' at their point of contact. Taking T₁T₂ crosses the line. The centers of the gears are O₁O₂ at a fixed point P - the so-called gear mesh pole. The movement occurs as if the drive wheel A₁, not with teeth, but with the help of gearing, transmitted movement to the wheel A₂. Let us draw a perpendicular PU through point P to the line of centers O₁O₂ and consider the angle φ=UPT₁. It is called the engagement point. Unlike the pair of gears discussed in figure 1, and the pair of cyclone gears, here this angle remains constant. Hence the benefits of the tooth profile in the form of circumferential involutions, allowing for a constant speed of rotation of the driven gear and silent operation.

figure 7 shows what an involute link looks like. The legs of the teeth do not have an involute profile until the very end of the groove, but only where the blue line is drawn. This is the so-called “gearing worker”.
