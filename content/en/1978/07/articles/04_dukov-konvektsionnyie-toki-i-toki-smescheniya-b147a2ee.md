Convection and mixing currents

To the question “What is electric current?” They usually answer this way: this is the directed movement of charges along a conductor. However, there is only a grain of truth in this answer. To formulate an accurate answer, let's turn to the source of the question.

In 1800, the Italian physicist Alexandro Volta discovered the possibility of producing direct current using an electrical source. d.s., which we now call a galvanic element. At that time, there was a hypothesis according to which electricity is a weightless liquid capable of penetrating through the pores of the body. Naturally, electric current began to be represented as the movement of this fluid along a circuit. Physics had a long way to go before it was established that charged particles of matter move inside conductors.

The first step was taken by Faraday, who showed that in electrical power supplies the current represents the movement of ions. An ion is an atom that has an excess or deficiency of charge of a determinable sign. Thus, it has two characteristics - mass and electric charge. During electrification, it separates the charge from the electrode and settles on it. The collection of settled honons forms a sediment, the mass of which can be easily changed.

From Faraday’s numerous experiments it followed that they of the same valency carry the same portion of charge. By the way, it was this conclusion that later (in 1881) led Helmholtz to the idea of ​​the existence of an elementary charge (physicists learned that it is associated with an elementary particle - an electron - only at the end of the 19th century).

Note that if Faraday had proceeded from the idea of ​​an elementary charge and the laws of conservation of charge and matter, then he could have obtained his two laws, which required many years of difficult concepts, from very simple calculations. Let us assume that $N$ ions move in the electric power supply, each of which carries a charge $Ze_0$, where $Z$ is the valency, $e_0$ is the magnitude of the elementary charge. Then the mass of ions deposited on the electrode will be

$$M = Nm_0,$$

where $m_0$ is the mass of the ion. These ions bring a charge to the electrode

$$q = NZe_0.$$

Dividing (1) by (2), we get

$$M = \frac{m_0}{Ze_0} q.$$

Denoting $\frac{m_0}{Ze_0} = k$, we obtain Faraday's first law:

$$M = kq.$$

Multiply and divide the expression for $k$ by Avogadro’s number $N_A$:

$$k = \frac{N_A \cdot m_0}{Ze_0 N_A} = \frac{\mu}{Ze_0 N_A} = \frac{1}{F} \frac{\mu}{Z},$$

where $\mu$ is the molar mass of the substance, $F = N_Ae_0$ is the Faraday number.

This results in Faraday's second law.

Thus, Farad's laws are consequences of conservation laws and the fact of the existence of an elementary charge. Expression (3) reveals the physical meaning of the Faraday electrochemical equivalent of a substance: the effective mass of a substance deposited during an electric pulse depends on the ratio of the charge of the ion to its mass. From (3) and (4) it follows that to obtain a precipitate with a larger mass, there must be a substance with a higher molar mass and a lower valency.

The ratio of the charge of a body to its mass in physics is usually called specificity.

At the same time, a current of one ampere passes through a wire with a diameter of a fraction of a millimeter, only heating it. This is explained precisely by the fact that microelectrics (electrons) have a large specific charge.

Let us return again to the question of the nature of the current. Current is created by the movement of charged particles. Not charges, but charged particles! And although we write $I = q/t$, by charge $q$ we mean the sum of the charges of particles having a certain mass. Therefore, electric current has mechanical properties. They were first discovered in the experiments of Tolman and Stewart. The solenide, to the ends of which a galvanometer was attached, was set into rapid rotation and then abruptly stopped. The galvanometer recorded the current, and the direction of this current corresponded to the movement of charged particles. In these experiments, the specific charge of particles, the movement of which determined the current, was changed. It turned out that the value found coincided with the value of the specific charge of the electron, found from the deviation in the electric and magnetic fields. Experiments by Tolman and Stewart revealed for the first time the existence of free electrons in metals. Being weakly connected to the crystal lattice, after the solenide stopped, the electrons continued to move by inertia - a short-term current arose.

In power supplies, the current is a double flow of currents moving in opposite directions. Ions have the same charges but different masses. A jar with two electrodes is filled with copper sulfate solution. Light bodies, such as small pieces of paper, are deposited on the surface of the liquid. Direct current is switched on. If you bring a permanent magnet (Fig. 1) close to the jar, the pieces of paper begin to rotate. This effect is explained by the fact that the Lorentz force acting on the moving ions from the magnetic field twists them. The rotating ones carry water molecules with them, and the entire liquid begins to rotate. The pieces of paper floating on the surface are fixed effectively. Naturally, the Lorentz force twists both them C++ and they SO₄⁻. However, the direction of rotation of the liquid is determined by the direction of movement of heavier ions (SO₄⁻ ions). The rotation caused by the increase in light C++ onns is “suppressed” by the rotation of heavier SO₄⁻ onns. Changing the direction of current or polarity of the magnet leads to a change in the direction of rotation of the electrolyte.

A similar effect occurs in the case of charge “draining” from the tip.

It is known that the density of the loaded parts increases sharply in the direction of the tip, therefore there is a significantly strong electric field around it. Neutral air molecules in such a field are non-ionized. If the tip has a positive charge, then the electrons in the air will flow onto the tip, and the positive ions will move in the opposite direction. The flow of these onns entrains neutral air molecules, just as the flow of onons in an electrolyte entrains water molecules. The so-called “electric wind” is created. Franklin demonstrated his experience of blowing out a candle with such a wind in the middle of the 18th century.

In the cases considered, we are dealing with convection flows of liquid and air. It was the historical experience with blowing out a candle that led to an understandable convection current. It was believed that, along with ordinary convection - the flow of neutral particles - there is electric convection - the flow of charged particles. Indeed, further research has shown that currents in metals, electrolytes and gases are susceptible to the flow of charged particles. Since particles have mass, there is no fundamental difference between ordinary convection and electric convection. In a work of 1875, Helmoholtz wrote: “I use this word [convection - V.D.] in the sense in which it is used in the theory of heat, to designate the spread of electricity through the movement of electrically charged bodies.” Helmoholtz posed a fundamentally important question: can the motion of charged macroscopic bodies (for example, our aluminum balls) be considered as an ordinary convection current.

The answer to this question could be given by an experiment similar to that carried out by the Danish physicist Hans Christan Oersted in 1820. He discovered that if a magnetic needle is placed near a conductor with current, it deviates. The pointer is acted upon by a force proportional to the magnitude of the current. The direction of this force depends on the direction of the current.

The experience described was known back in the last century. They explained it this way: the source of E.m.f. causes charged particles to oscillate in the conductor connecting the plates, they “run” from plate to plate, while nothing happens between the plates, or there is a vacuum. In these arguments, current is represented as a mechanical process of movement of charged particles.

The brilliant English physicist James Clerk Maxwell introduced a fundamentally new idea. The movement of charged particles is associated with electric and magnetic fields. A change in current leads to a change in fields. Faraday discovered the phenomenon of electromagnetic induction, which, according to Maxwell, boils down to the appearance of an electric field when the magnetic field changes. Confident in the symmetrical electromagnetic phenomenon, Maxwell proposed that changes in the electric field generate a magnetic field.

In the circuit shown in figure 3, the electric field changes between the plates of the capacitor. According to Maxwell, this process leads to the appearance of a magnetic field. But the magnetic field is caused by current. Consequently, the process that occurs between the plates of the capacitor can be interpreted as the flow of current. There are no "ends" of current. If the circuit is broken by a dielectric or vacuum, the current continues its path, only it will be of a different nature. Maxwell calls this current a mixing current. The origin of the term is as follows. Maxwell suggests that space, which appears empty to us, is filled with a material medium with special properties - ether. Ether has a cellular structure (such as, for example, a crystal lattice), and under the influence of electric field forces, the cells can be deformed - shifted, just as dielectric particles shift.

The current can be caused by either convection or mixture. In the case of convection current, its magnitude is proportional to the speed of movement of charged particles; The mixing current is determined by the mixing speed, and it is naturally proportional to the oscillation frequency. The larger the capacitance of the capacitor, the greater the volume of ether (at a given distance between the plates), therefore, the greater the mixing effect. This is the physical picture.

Maxwell expressed the mathematical properties of the hypothesis as follows:

$$i_{\text{CM}} = \varepsilon_0 S \frac{dE}{dt}$$

— the mixing current is proportional to the area S of the circuit break (or the area of ​​the capacitor plane) and the rate of change in the electric field strength ($\varepsilon_0 = 8,86 \cdot 10^{-12} \text{ Ф}/\text{м}$).
