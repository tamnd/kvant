Optics without optics

In the last issue of our magazine, we advised you to check through experiments whether you know the lens well. Now we invite you to try a few more experiments in optics, but without special optical instruments.

1. Take the middle chocolate candy wrapper. Flatten it and poke a small wrap through it. Through this wrapping, placing it as close to the eye as possible, look at some brightly lit text, also placed close to the eye. Did you manage to see the greatly enlarged letters? It turns out that not only the lens can magnify!

Which image of the letter did you see—straight or inverted? It would seem that the letter should be upside down (look at figure 1) - on the retina, all images always appear upside down. But our brain “knows” about this and is “used to” it: we perceive objects around us upside down.

In opaque paper, pierce the wrap with a needle. In a darkened room, before the rejection, light a candle, and on the other side of the rejection, place a white sheet of paper - a screen. On the screen you will see a clear reverse image of the candle. This simple device is a model of the so-called camera obscura (observation camera), which has been used since ancient times to obtain various images. The camera obscura is the simplest camera.

2. The fact that the brain really “reverses” the image of an object can be verified by such a simple experiment. Between the rejection in the foil (in the middle piece of paper) and the eye, place a small object - the point of a pencil or a pinhead. The light from rejection creates an uninverted image of the object on the retina (Fig. 2). And the brain “by inertia” will turn this image upside down.

hole to a sufficiently bright light source. Just be careful not to overstrain your eyes. You will see light rays emanating from the hole and around the hole - rings painted in all the colors of the rainbow. How can this be explained?

It turns out that light, like waves on the surface of water, is able to bend around obstacles encountered along its path.

Let's talk a little about waves on the water. As you know, large and small waves bend around obstacles differently. If, for example, such a thing sticks out of the water, then small waves (ripples) form a noticeable “shadow” (an area of ​​calm water surface) behind the table. Large waves do not cast a “shadow” in practice; they bend around obstacles well.

What do “small” and “large” waves mean? Why do they behave differently when faced with an obstacle? There is such a thing as wavelength. This is the distance between two adjacent crests (or rotations) of a wave. So, small waves have a much shorter wavelength compared to large ones. And the longer the wavelength (compared to the obstacle), the stronger the wave bends around the obstacle.

The same thing happens with light waves: if there is an obstacle on the path of light, the light bends around it. That's why you saw a beam of light emanating from the hole in the foil.

How did the multi-colored rings appear? The thing is that white light consists of rays of different colors. Following Newton, it is customary to distinguish seven colors: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo and violet. Each color has its own wavelength of light. The longest wavelength is for red light (≈ 7·10⁻⁷ m), and the shortest is for violet light (≈ 4·10⁻⁷ m). Now everything is clear to you, isn’t it?

4. Use a safety razor blade to cut a narrow slit in the foil: cut along the foil with a razor. Move the slit 10 centimeters away from your eye and look through it at the window. You will see several dark stripes parallel to the crack. If

They are hard to see, don’t be upset. It's just that your gap is too wide. Smooth out the foil with the gap - the gap will become narrower and the picture will look better. If this doesn't help, run the blade over the foil again.

Once you have a good slit, look through it (bring the slit towards your eye) at a table lamp with clear glass or - be careful! - to the Sun. Look just below the light source. You see, two pillars of light have appeared perpendicular to the slit, on which bright rainbow stripes are visible. If the slot is cut successfully, then there are a lot of shelves, and their color is deep and rich. Pay attention to the location of the brushes.

Both experiments, like the previous one, are explained by light waves bending around the edges of the slit.

A very good slit can be made from a safety razor blade. From thick paper you need to cut out two rectangles slightly larger than the blade, and the middle of the rectangles also needs to be cut out. Insert the blade broken in half into this frame so that it stays sharp
