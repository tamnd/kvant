green glass

What color is green glass?

This question can cause a feeling of natural homelessness. The reader will say with irritation: green glass is called green because it... However, there is no need to rush into condescending explanations. A simple experience will show you that the question of the color of green glass is not at all so simple.

If you have a piece of green glass, carefully break it into several not very small pieces. Then, look through one of them at the filament of the incandescent lamp. As you would expect, the thread will appear green (Fig. 1). Place a second piece of glass on this piece and look at the thread again.

You probably won't notice a change in the color of the thread; it will still be green. But if you put a third one on two pieces of glass and look through all three pieces at the thread, you will see it already uncolored and whitish in color. Through four pieces the thread will appear reddish, and through the heels of the pieces it will appear ruby ​​red!

The result is completely unexpected and very impressive. It turns out that the color of glass depends on thickness, and glass that is green in a thin layer becomes red when the layer is thick enough. Of course, not every green glass has this property, but just the most common two types of green glass.

Everyday life not made by Newton and landscape painting

Probably, many of you remember Newton’s landmark experiment with the decomposition of a solar ray into a multi-colored spectrum using a glass prism. This experiment showed that sunlight is a mixture of rays of different colors: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo and violet. For some reason, Newton did not try to somewhat condition this experiment: to place colored glass or a vessel with a colored liquid in the path of the sun's ray. In any case, Newton does not describe such an experience in his writings.

Living with red glass, in fact, would not have given anything interesting. Instead of a multi-colored strip of the spectrum, only the section corresponding to the red rays would remain. The result could have been predicted in advance: red glass is red because it transmits only red rays and absorbs all others.

Much more interesting is the experiment with green glass or a vessel filled with chlorophyll solution. In these cases, the spectrum of the remaining status is no longer one, but two stripes: green and dark red. This means that green glass and chlorophyll solution transmit not only green, but also red rays.

Regarding chlorophyll, the remarks of the famous Russian botanist K. A. Timirzev are very interesting: “You can very easily make sure that chlorophyll transmits red rays: it is worth placing it on a landscape flooded with bright sunlight through a special blue glass (Fig. 3), which transmits red and blue rays, but retains green ones, so that before our studied eyes all nature is completely transformed - under the general photograph in the sky we will see blood-red vegetation. Is it not this peculiarity of the color of chlorophyll that lies behind the difficulties with which, very much, landscape painting has to struggle? On the painter’s palette, apparently, there are no those green tones that brightly lit greenery represents up close.”

Let us, however, leave painting aside for now and return to the natural wedge. We see a little bit about the filament of an incandescent lamp and between it and we recognize a pometiric wedge (Fig. 4). On the wall we again see two stripes - green and red, and the correspondence of these stripes will depend on the thickness of the wedge at the point where the light beam passes. If the beam passes through the thin part of the wedge, the green stripe is much brighter than the red one. As the thickness of the wedge increases, the brightness of both stripes becomes brighter, and, starting from a certain moment, the red stripe becomes brighter than the green one. When the green stripe is brighter than the red one, the thread is visible green; when the stripes correspond inversely, the thread is visible red. When the brightness of the stripes is equal, each thread is colorless.

It's as if the mystery of green glass has been solved. However, it remains to be explained why, with increasing glass thickness, the correspondence between the brightness of the red and green stripes is reversed. It turns out that the explanation follows from an important law of optics, discovered by one brave sailor years ago.

Sea captain and geometric progression

French sea captain Pierre Bouguer, who lived in the first half of the eighteenth century, was not, perhaps, a simple sailor. He wrote extensive treatises on ship design, navigation and other branches of maritime affairs. The French Academy of Sciences awarded Bouguer three prizes for his work in maritime affairs and elected him as a member. Buger inherited his taste for marine sciences from his father, a professor of hydrology.

If Buger was involved in the sea by inheritance, then he took up optics on his own initiative. Booger was the first to draw attention to the problems associated with changes in light and illumination. He invented the first instruments for measuring the light of light and established that the dreams of light from the Sun are 300 thousand times greater than the dreams of light from the Moon, and his “Optical Treatise” contained a very important law of attenuation of light in absorbing bodies.

To understand the meaning of this law (it is also called Bouguer’s law), we will use a not very plausible, but visual analogy from the field of sports. Let's return from sports to optics. Take a piece of painted glass. Let's say that it transmits one third of the light incident on it. Let's add a second similar piece. It will transmit one-third of the luminous flux that passed through the first piece, that is, one-ninth of the luminous flux incident on the first piece. Putting another piece, we get one twenty-seventh part and so on.

It is clear that the same result would be obtained simply by doubling the thickness of a piece of glass, twice, etc. As the thickness of the glass increases, the proportion of transmitted light decreases exponentially.

This is the law discovered by Bouguer. In the example of runners, we have already seen how the numbers quickly decrease exponentially.

There is a complete analogy in the behavior of the green and red rays and the “green” and “red” runners (Fig. 5). Green glass transmits dark red rays better than green glass, and, according to Bougard’s law, the difference in transmission of these rays increases rapidly with increasing thickness of the glass layer (“long distance”).

But then the question naturally arises: why is there glass in a thin layer of each green, if it transmits dark red rays better than green? This is explained by the spectral characteristics of the light source with which the experiment was carried out: the green part of the city’s spectrum is brighter than the dark red part (the team of “green” and numerous “red”). In a thin layer of glass (“short distance”) the difference in the absorption of dark red and green rays is not yet so great as to cover the initial intensity of the green rays. The main role is played by green rays, which gives the corresponding color.

With increasing glass thickness, according to Bougar's law, the transmission of green rays decreases much faster than dark red rays (the number of “green” and “red” runners at long distances). With a sufficiently large thickness, the difference in the transmission of the beam is so great that it overlaps the initial transition of the green rays into rage, and only the dark red half of the entire practical spectrum remains.

It remains only to determine what role the temperature of the red-hot body on which we look at the glass plays. It is known that the hotter we heat any metal object, the more light we give them. No wonder they say: “Bring it to white heat.” So, with insufficient heat, an incandescent light bulb produces a reddish light, while with normal heat, the horizons are whiter. This is explained by the fact that as the temperature rises, the intensity of the green and blue rays grows faster than the red ones.

This means that at a higher temperature the difference in the brightness of the green and dark red parts of the spectrum is greater, and it is more difficult to cover it by absorption in the glass. This is why at higher temperatures of a hot body, glass is no longer needed to change the color of the observed radiation.

Old Russian inkly and observations of Leonardo da Vinci

On some ancient Russian inklets, the unusual pattern of the saints’ robes is striking. The folds are painted with paint that has a sharply different color from the color of the smooth parts of the robe. For example, red folds on a green robe (Fig. 6) or orange folds on a blue robe. The keen eye of the ancient Russian rich man noticed that non-woven fabrics have two colors and in the folds acquire a different color than on the flat surface. The reason for the two-color fabric is the same as in the experiment with a metric wedge.

If a beam of light reflected from a two-color fabric is passed through a prism, then two colored stripes will definitely remain in the spectrum. For green two-color fabric, the picture will be the same as with green glass: red and green stripes will remain, the rest of the rays will be absorbed.

Two-color green fabric reflects red rays better than green ones, but with a single reflection from the smooth surface of the fabric, the green rays are more bright in the incident light. Therefore, in the once reflected light, green rays are still present.

In the folds of fabric, light experiences at least two successive reflections. During the second reflection, the red rays are again reflected stronger than the green ones, and as a result of double reflection, the same thing happens as in thick green glass: the brightness of the red rays becomes greater than the brightness of the green rays, and the fabric changes color. Multiple reflections will hear this effect.

Most ordinary fabrics have exactly the opposite properties. The folds produce a more saturated, but the same color tone as the flat surface. This is explained again by repeated reflections. Light reflected from such fabrics, after decomposition of the characteristics, gives only one stripe in the spectrum of two stripes in two-color fabrics. For example, light, once reflected from yellow velvet, produces a broad band in the spectrum with the brightest part in the yellow part. In addition, the spectrum also contains green and blue rays. With double reflection, the band in the spectrum becomes narrower, since the blue rays practically disappear completely, and the green rays are greatly weakened. The same law of geometric progression works. As a result, the yellow-orange reflected light becomes more saturated.

One of the most remarkable geniuses who ever lived, Leonardo da Vinci, not only noticed this feature of the folds of fabric with his artist’s eye, but also, as a scientist, gave a completely correct explanation of the observed phenomenon. In his Treatise on Painting, he writes: “Reflected colors have a much greater beauty than the natural color of these bodies, as can be seen in the opening folds of golden fabric... when one surface is reflected in another, facing direction, and this one in it, and so on successively ad infinitum.”

The same “Treatise on Painting” says: “Reflexions (reflections) from a living body receiving light from another living body are redder and more perfectly telephone in color than any other part of a living body that a person can have.” Contemplating with pleasure the amazing paintings of Van Dyck and Rubens in the halls of the Leningrad Hermitage, it is easy to notice that even for these great masters the effect of multiple reflections was not a secret.
