M463. Given natural numbers $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_m, y_1, y_2, \dots, y_n$. The sums $x_1 + x_2 + \dots + x_m$ and $y_1 + y_2 + \dots + y_n$ are equal to each other and less than mn. Prove that in the equality $x_1 + x_2 + \dots + x_m = y_1 + y_2 + \dots + y_n$ you can cross out some of the max terms to get a true equality again.

$$4 + 5 + 5 = 1 + 1 + 6 + 6 \quad (\text{неЛЬЗЯ}),$$
$$3 + 3 + 4 + 5 = 1 + 1 + 6 + 7 \quad (\text{МОЖНО})$$

Let us denote by $\alpha$ the angles formed by the height of the paramid with the side edges, by $A, B, C, D$ the intersection points of the cutting plane with the side edges and the height, by $H$ the vertex of the paramid (Fig. 1). It is clear that there must be some correspondence between the lengths $a, b, c$ and $d$: after all, a single plane passes through the three points $A, B, C$, therefore $d$ can be expressed through $a, b$ and $c$. Such a correspondence can be obtained by expressing the length of the common bisector $HK$ in each of the triangles $AHC$ and $BHD$ through the angle $\alpha$ and the lengths of their sides: $a, c$ (in one) and $b, d$ (in the other) and equating the results. This is exactly how you get a triangular equality.

In order to obtain the required formula for the length of the bisector $l = |HK|$ in the triangle $AHC$, we use the fact that $S(AHC) = S(AHK) + S(KHC)$ (Fig. 2). Multiplying by 2, we get

$$ac \sin 2\alpha = al \sin \alpha + cl \sin \alpha,$$

where

$$\frac{2\cos \alpha}{l} = \frac{1}{a} + \frac{1}{c}.$$

Similarly from triangle $BHC$

$$\frac{2\cos \alpha}{l} = \frac{1}{b} + \frac{1}{d}.$$

From here

$$\frac{1}{a} + \frac{1}{c} = \frac{1}{b} + \frac{1}{d}.$$

N. Vasiliev

M464. On a given plane there are 1000 squares with sides parallel to the coordinate axes. Let $M$ be the set of centers of these squares. Prove that it is possible to mark a part of the squares so that any point in the set $M$ falls into at least one and at most four marked squares.

Let's prove this lemma. We can assume that the common point is the origin $O$. Of the path of the centers of these squares, at least two centers must lie in one of the four planes defined by the coordinate axis (the four are joined by the rays that limit them). Let the centers $O_1$ and $O_2$ of the squares $S_1$ and $S_2$ lie in the first quarter, and $(x_1; y_1)$, $(x_2; y_2)$ be the coordinates of the points $O_1$ and $O_2$. Let's choose the most from the numbers $x_1, y_1, x_2, y_2$;
empty, we will direct, $x_1$. Let us show that then $O_2 \in S_1$. Indeed, it is clear that the point is $(x_1; x_2) \in S_1$. Since $O \in S_1$, it follows that every point $(x; y)$ such that $0 \leq x \leq x_1$, $0 \leq y \leq x_1$ belongs to $S_1$. In particular, $(x_2; y_2) \in S_1$, which is what was required.

Let us now describe the correct “marked” squares. Let $K_1$ be the most of the given 1000 squares (hereinafter, if the most is not several, we take one of them). Let $K_2$ be the largest of those squares of a given family that have centers outside $K_1$; $K_3$ is the most of those squares of a given family that have centers outside $K_1$ and $K_2$, etc. This process ends with the construction of a square $K_n$ such that there are no points of the set $M$ outside $K_1$, ..., $K_n$ (the total number of finite squares). The squares $K_1$, ..., $K_n$ marked in this way satisfy very many of the conditions of the problem: they cover the set $M$. To show that they satisfy the second condition, it is enough to verify that no five of them have a common point. Indeed, according to the lemma, if such a point was found, one of the marked squares would contain the center of the other. It remains to note that this is not the case for the squares we have chosen. Indeed, if $i < j$, then the center of $K_j$ is outside $K_i$ by construction; Moreover, $K_j$ is not greater than $K_i$, therefore, the center $K_i$ is also outside $K_j$.

A. Plotkin

F478. On a cube lying on a smooth horizontal surface, fly one of the same cubes, and the cubes are struck with their parallel edges. The attack speed of the estimated cube before the collision was directed at an angle of $\alpha$ to the side edges (Fig. 3). How will the cubes move after a collision if the coefficient of friction between the sides of the cups is $\mu$?

Fig. 3.

Let us denote $F$ the absolute value of the elastic forces with which the cubes act on each other when colliding. Then the absolute value of the friction force between the cups is $\mu F$ (Fig. 4).

During time $\Delta t$, the projection of the momentum of each cup onto the axis $Y$ changes in absolute value by $F\Delta t$. Therefore every cup of cups

$$|\Delta (mv_y)| = F\Delta t.$$

The absolute value of the change in the projection of the momentum of each cup onto the $X$ axis is equal to $\mu F\Delta t$:

$$|\Delta (mv_x)| = \mu F\Delta t.$$

From these relations it is clear that for each cube

$$|\Delta (mv_x)| = \mu |\Delta (mv_y)|.$$

This relationship is valid for any time intervals. Therefore, if we denote $v_x$ and $v_y$ the projections of the speed of the cube on the axes $X$ and $Y$ after the collision of the cups, $v_x$ and $v_y$ are the values ​​of its projections before the collision, then

$$\mu m |v'_x - v_y| = m |v'_x - v_x| \Rightarrow \mu' |v'_x - v_y| = |v'_x - v_x|.$$

Let us denote by $u$ and $w$ the speeds of the cups $I$ and $II$ before the collision ($w = 0$), $u'$ and $w'$ the speeds of the cups $I$ and $II$ after the collision. Assuming that the loss of energy during a collision occurs only due to the action of the friction force, we obtain for the cube $I$ ($u_x = u \cos \alpha$, $u_y = u \sin \alpha$)

$$u'_y = 0, u'_x = u \cos \alpha - \mu u \sin \alpha = u (\cos \alpha - \mu \sin \alpha),$$
for cube II ($w_x = 0, w_y = 0, w = |\vec{w}|$)
$$w'_y = u \sin \alpha, w'_x = \mu u \sin \alpha \quad (u = |\vec{u}|).$$
For an absolutely inelastic (along the Y axis) impact
$$u'_y = w'_y = \frac{1}{2} u \sin \alpha,$$
$$u'_x = u \cos \alpha - \frac{1}{2} \mu u \sin \alpha.$$
(We assumed that the speed $\vec{u}$ is directed parallel to the line connecting the centers of mass of the cubes, and the area of ​​contact upon impact is equal to the area of ​​the contacting faces. Under these conditions, the cubes do not rotate after the impact.)

F479. To determine the unknown volume $V_1$ of cylinder I, assemble the diagram shown in figure 5. The volume of cylinder II is $V_2$ and $V_2 \leq V_1$. First, the system is pumped down to a pressure of $p_0$, close to zero. Then close tap B and fill cylinder II with gas to pressure $p_0$. After this, close valve A, open valve B and measure the pressure established in the system $p_1$ with pressure gauge C. Then, with valve B closed, remove the gas cylinder to pressure $p_a$, open valve B and measure the pressure established in the system $p_2$. This operation is repeated n times. Show that the ratio $\frac{p_n - p_0}{np_a}$ is equal to and equal to $\frac{V_2}{V_1 + V_2}$.

From the experimental conditions, using the Boyle-Marport law, a system of n equations is obtained
$$p_0 V_1 + p_a V_2 = p_1 (V_1 + V_2),$$
$$p_1 V_1 + p_a V_2 = p_2 (V_1 + V_2),$$
$$p_{n-1} V_1 + p_a V_2 = p_n (V_1 + V_2).$$
This system allows all $p_i$ ($i = 1, 2, \dots, n$) to be expressed in terms of $p_0$ and $p_a$. Really,
$$p_i = \frac{p_{i-1} V_1 + p_a V_2}{V_1 + V_2},$$
where
$$p_i - p_a = \frac{V_1}{V_1 + V_2} (p_{i-1} - p_a) = \alpha (p_{i-1} - p_a),$$
where $\alpha = \frac{V_1}{V_1 + V_2}$. Hence,
$$p_{i-1} - p_a = \alpha (p_{i-2} - p_a), p_{i-2} - p_a = \alpha (p_{i-3} - p_a)$$, etc.
Thus, $p_n - p_a = \alpha^n (p_0 - p_a)$, from where
$$p_n = p_a + \alpha^n (p_0 - p_a),$$
$$p_n - p_0 = (p_a - p_0)(1 - \alpha^n).$$
But $1 - \alpha^n = (1 - \alpha)(1 + \alpha + \alpha^2 + \dots + \alpha^{n-1}) \approx n \frac{V_2}{V_1 + V_2}$ (since $V_1 \gg V_2$ and therefore $\alpha$ is close to one). Means,
$$p_n - p_0 \approx (p_a - p_0) n \frac{V_2}{V_1 + V_2},$$
$$\frac{p_n - p_0}{n (p_a - p_0)} \approx \frac{V_2}{V_1 + V_2}.$$
If $p_0 \ll p_a$, then
$$\frac{p_n - p_0}{np_a} \approx \frac{V_2}{V_1 + V_2}.$$

F480. The ship is driven by a water jet, which throws a string of water from the hull at a speed of $u$. Every second a mass of water $m$ is released, which is taken from the river. At what speed of the ship $k$, $n$, $d$. maximum movement? The force of friction and the resistance of water make a claim.

The total work $A$ performed by the engine in a short time $\Delta t$ is equal to the kinetic energy that is imparted to the water ejected from the ship's engine. Since the mass of water released during this time is equal to $\mu \Delta t$, then

$$A = \frac{(\mu \Delta t) u^2}{2}.$$

The useful work done during this time is equal to the change in the kinetic energy of the ship. If the mass of the ship is $M$, then

$$A_{\text{пол}} = \frac{M (v + \Delta v)^2}{2} - \frac{M v^2}{2} = M v \Delta v,$$

where $v$ is the absolute value of the ship’s speed. Neglecting the small square $(\Delta v)^2$, we obtain $A_{\text{пол}} = M v \Delta v$. In order to find $M \Delta v$, you can use the law of conservation of momentum. Since the friction and resistance are small, the impulse of the water-ship system does not change. Hence,

$$M v = M (v + \Delta v) - \mu \Delta t (u - v).$$

From here

$$M \Delta v = \mu \Delta t (u - v).$$

The engine efficiency is equal to

$$\eta = \frac{A_{\text{пол}}}{A} = \frac{2 M v \Delta v}{(\mu \Delta t) u^2} = \frac{2 (u - v) v}{u^2}.$$

This is the expression for the maximum at $v = \frac{u}{2}$.

When a battery of batteries is shorted to resistance $R$, current flows through the circuit

$$I_1 = \frac{n \mathcal{E}}{(n - 1) r + 10 r + R},$$

and power is released at the resistance $R$ of the load

$$W_1 = I_1^2 R = \frac{n^2 \mathcal{E}^2}{[(n - 1) r + 10 r + R]^2} R.$$

After the damaged battery is short-circuited, current will flow through the circuit

$$I_2 = \frac{(n - 1) \mathcal{E}}{(n - 1) r + R},$$

and power will be released at the load

$$W_2 = I_2^2 R = \frac{(n - 1)^2 \mathcal{E}^2}{[(n - 1) r + R]^2} R.$$

Equating $W_1$ and $W_2$, we get

$$\frac{n^2}{[(n - 1) r + 10 r + R]^2} = \frac{(n - 1)^2}{[(n - 1) r + R]^2}.$$

From here

$$R = 9 r (n - 1).$$
