Kepler spent ten years in Prague. This was the most creatively fruitful period of his life. Here, in Prague, Kepler wrote two fundamental treatises on optics: “Addition to Vitell, which expounds the optical part of astronomy” (1604) and “Dioptrics” (1611).

The first book was written, indeed, as an addition to the well-known treatise of the useful scientist of the 12th century, Vitellus, which was well known at that time. Kepler is left with many questions concerning geometric and physiological optics. He introduced the terms “optical axis” and “meniscus”, “convergence” and “divergence” of light beams into optics, created a theory of the mechanism of vision, which basically coincides with the modern one, explained the action of converging and diverging spectacle lenses that correct farsightedness and myopia, and came close to developing the theory of optical instruments.

The second book (its full title is "Dioptrics, or the proof of how an image becomes visible by means of the newly invented visual tour") is the result of further research related to optical instruments, especially after the creation of the first telesekon by Galileo in 1609. Kepler used the term “dioptrics” to refer to the branch of optics that deals with the phenomenon of refraction of light (in contrast to ενClidean catoptrics, which studies the reflection of light). It is amazing that, without knowing the correct formulation of the law of refraction of light (this law was found for a particular case by Sneglin in 1617, and for the general case by Descartes in 1637), Kepler managed to construct a generally correct theory of the operation of optical instruments and, in particular, visual labor.

Kepler lived in Linz for 14 years. In addition to the fact that he was listed as an imperial mathematician, he was called the mathematician of the province of Upper Austria. His main task was to continue compiling tables of planetary motions (based on Tycho Brahe's observations). But Kepler was also interested in purely mathematical problems. This is, first of all, the mathematics of variables and the theory of regular polygons and polyhedra.

In 1615, Kepler’s book “Stereometry of Wine Barrels” was published, which laid the foundation for a whole series of studies in the mathematics of variables, which led to the creation of the theory of differential and integral calculus by Newton and Leibniz.

The reason for writing the book (with the words of Kepler himself) was the following story. One day in the fall of 1613, when Kepler was buying wine, he was amazed at how the merchant determined the compatibility of the barrel. He took a stick on which divisions were marked, placed it in the filling hole of the barrel and measured the distance from the hole to the farthest point of the barrel - to the edge of the bottom. Having made this one measurement, he immediately said how much wine was in a given barrel. Kepler was intrigued by how accurately the merchant determined the volume of the barrel using a single measurement.

By the end of the year, the results of this study were received. First, Kepler found a formula for calculating the volume of a barrel, and then - other rotating bodies. To find the volumes of these irregular bodies, he used the “exhaustion” method, filling the bodies with figures whose volumes were calculated.

Finding the volume of a body as the sum of the elements of volumes filling the body, Kepler often used the Latin expression Summa opinium - the sum of all. As is known, one of the creators of integral calculus, Leibniz, used the integral $\int$ (removed letter S) precisely for the abbreviated notation of the expression Summa opinium.

The topic of regular polygons and polyhedra was developed in Kepler’s five-volume work “The Harmony of the World.”

Kepler was an outstanding mathematician not only by his official title, but also by his very essence. He made a great contribution to the theory of finite sections and introduced the term “focus” (parabolas, hyperbolas, ellipses). His introduction of the concept of an infinite distant point contributed to the creation of projective geometry. He was a good calculator, took part in the development of the theory of logarithms, compiled his own tables of logarithms, and finally, he contributed to the invention of the first computer.

In Linz, Kepler works a lot and fruitfully. This greatly contributes to his second-in-command, Susana Reittinger, whom he married in 1613. This is a capable, kind and hardworking woman. She patiently and with dignity endures adversity and deprivation, rejoices at her husband’s scientific successes and knows how to support him in difficult times.

However, capable work in Linz is interrupted by a letter from his sister, who reports that Kepler's mother will be accused of witchcraft.
