Symmetry in physics problems

With understandable symmetrical matter - in nature, technology, art, science. Let us recall the symmetry characteristic of the apple and the maple leaf, the symmetry of the forms of automation and the airplane, the symmetry of the classical construction of a poem or musical phrase, the symmetry of ornaments, the symmetry in the atomic structure of molecules and crystals. In particular, symmetry is quite common in physics problems.

What is symmetry? Many find it difficult to answer this question, although manifestations of symmetry in various specific cases are perceived by almost everyone. In everyday practice, understandable symmetry is usually compared with something balanced, harmonious, and having a good ratio of proportions. In the specific application of this concept, they speak, for example, of symmetry about an axis, symmetry when reflected in a mirror, symmetry of a square, regular hexagon, etc.

When solving problems, we often use symmetry without stating this fact, as something taken for granted. Thus, it seems very clear that the center of a gravity-homogeneous rectangular plate is located at the point of intersection of its diagonals, that the field outside a charged metal ball can be considered as the field of an exact charge located in the center of the ball, that mathematically the magnet makes oscillations symmetrical with respect to the direction of the gravity force.

In solutions to problems, you can find phrases like “from the reported symmetry it is clear that...” or “based on the symmetry one can conclude...”. Here are some specific examples.

Problem 1. Four identical powerful charges q are placed at the vertices of a square. What charge Q of the opposite sign must be placed in the center of the square so that the system of charges is in equilibrium?

Equilibrium means that the sum of the forces applied to each charge is zero. In this case, it is enough to consider the equilibrium of one of the five charges. He asks which one? FROM the reported symmetry it is clear that it makes no sense to consider the charge Q, since it will be in equilibrium regardless of its value. Again, from symmetry it is very clear that all charges q are equivalent, so that any of them can be chosen with equal reason. Distance for example, charge located at point A (Fig. 1). Problem 2. Four cuttings are located at the vertices of the square on the side $a$. They begin to move simultaneously at an externally constant speed $v$, with the first gear always heading towards the second, the first towards the first, the first towards the fourth, and the fourth towards the first. Will the Cherenakhs meet? If so, after how long?

First of all, we note that from the point of view of the conditions of the problem, all the steps are completely equal and equivalent to each other. From the symmetry messages it is clear that at any moment of time the cuttings will form a square, and their velocities will be directed along the sides of the square (Fig. 2). The orientation and dimensions of such squares will change over time - until the square contracts to point $O$

(Then the Cherenakhs will meet). Again, from the messages, the symmetry point $O$ is the common center of all the mentioned squares. In order to find the time $t$ after which the oncoming Cherenakh will occur, we decompose the velocity vector $v$ Cherenakh into a component $\vec{v}_1$, directed towards the center $O$, and a perpendicular component $\vec{v}_2$. Then
$$t = \frac{a/\sqrt{2}}{|\vec{v}_1|} = \frac{a/\sqrt{2}}{|\vec{v}|/\sqrt{2}} = \frac{a}{|\vec{v}|}.$$

Problem 3. A homogeneous block of mass $m$ rests on three vertical wires of equal length, located symmetrically (Fig. 3). Determine the tension of the wires if the middle wire is steel and the other two are copper. Assume that the Young's modulus of steel is twice the Young's modulus of copper, and the cross-sections of the wires are the same.

From the symmetry messages it is clear that the elongation $\Delta l$ of all three wires is the same. According to Hooke's law, the tensile forces of steel and copper wires are equal, respectively

Problem 7. Balls are thrown against the ground surfaces at an angle $\alpha$ with an initial speed $\vec{v}_0$ (Fig. 7, $a$). There is a vertical wall at a distance $l$ from the throwing point. The balls stubbornly hit the wall and bounce back. At what distance $x$ from the wall does he land?

Let's mentally remove the wall and continue the flight path of the ball (dashed line at Fig. 7, 6). During an elastic impact on a wall, the absolute value of the ball’s speed does not change, and the angle of incidence $\beta$ is equal to the angle of reflection $\beta_1$. Consequently, sections of the trajectory $AB$ (in the absence of a wall) and $AC$ (in the presence of a wall) with it are metric with respect to the straight line $AA_1$ (relative to the wall). From here we get:

$$x = OB - l = \frac{v_0^2}{|g|} \sin 2\alpha - l.$$

Problem 8. The ball with relics $A$ and $B$ has resistance $R$. In addition to the power between each pair of relics, including the soda and relics $A$ and $B$, a resistance $R$ is also attached. The name that results in the total resistance is the relics $A$ and $B$.

Let us mentally place the points $N-2$ indicated on the line $OO_1$, relative to which the points $A$ and $B$ are symmetrical. Thus we will move from the asymmetrical picture shown at Fig. 8, $a$, to the symmetrical picture shown at Fig. 8, $b$ (at Fig. 6). From the symmetry messages it is clear that the potentials of all points on the straight line $OO_1$ are the same, so these points can be separated. Then the required resistance $R_{AB}$ can be found using the formula:

$$\frac{1}{R_{AB}} = \frac{1}{R} + \frac{N-2}{2R}.$$

From here

$$R_{AB} = 2R/N.$$

Let's return to the question posed earlier: what is symmetry? By what main sign can one establish its presence in a particular case? The famous German mathematician Hermann Weyl proposed an excellent and simple definition of symmetry, according to which an object that can be somehow changed is called symmetrical.
