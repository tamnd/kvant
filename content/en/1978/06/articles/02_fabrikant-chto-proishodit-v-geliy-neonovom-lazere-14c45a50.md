What happens in a helium-neon laser?

The helium-neon laser is becoming a propagation device, widely used in a wide variety of fields of technology and science. Such lasers have appeared in many schools, and with their help the properties of coherent light are studied.

The heart of each laser is its own so-called active medium, which absorbs the light beam passing through it. In a helium-neon laser, such a medium is plasma, which is formed when an electric current passes through a mixture of helium and neon in a gas-discharge tube.

Typically, when passing through a medium, a light beam experiences attenuation - part of the energy of the beam is absorbed by the medium. However, it is possible to create an environment in which, when light passes through someone, it will not be weakened, but will be compressed. The possibility of creating such an environment was first indicated in 1939. Of course, in the process of the success of light, the law of conservation of energy is not violated. The light beam draws additional energy from the internal energy of the atoms of the medium.

In order to understand how the light-harvesting medium “works,” we need to consider in more detail the interaction of light with atoms. For the first time such consideration was carried out by Albert Einstein's theory of relatives. It is amazing how many fundamental results in various fields of modern physics are associated with the name of Einstein!

In 1916, Einstein, developing Max Planck's ideas about the quantum nature of the interaction of light with atoms, for the first time clearly formulated the existence of three elementary optical processes in which atoms participate.

As is known, according to quantum concepts, the internal energy of atoms can only change in jumps. An atom, as we say, can be in a Stationary state at one of the energy levels (Fig. 1), corresponding to a wave of certain reserves of internal energy. Optical transitions of an atom from one level to another are accompanied by the emission or absorption of photons by the atom. According to the law of conservation of energy, the equality must be fulfilled

$$hv = E_2 - E_1,$$

(1)

where $hv$ is the photon energy ($h$ is Planck’s constant, $v$ is the frequency of light), $E_1$ and $E_2$ are the energies of the Stationary states of the atom, between which a transition is passed. It is clear that to move from one energy level to a higher one, an atom must absorb photons. The energy of absorbed photons goes to increase the internal energy of the atom. On the contrary, during transitions to a lower level, the atom must absorb photons that carry away the excess internal energy of the atom.

Einstein pointed out that there is one type of photon absorption processes and two types of photon emission processes (Fig. 2). In the process of absorption, the atom, as it were, swallows the photon that approaches it and moves to a higher energy level (Fig. 2, a). The process of emission of a photon by an atom can occur spontaneously - spontaneously, without any external influence (Fig. 2, b). Of course, in order for spontaneous emission of a photon to occur, the atom must first be excited. Spontaneous emission fails due to the instability of the excited state of the atom. The lifespan of isolated excited atoms is different for different states. (For shorter lifespans of excited atoms, we are satisfied - on the order of $10^{-8}$ s.)

The most important thing for lasers was Einstein’s discovery of the existence of a second type of processes using

releasing photons - the so-called forced, or stimulated, emission. (We deliberately deviated somewhat from the systematics of optical processes given by Einstein himself. He called stimulated emission negative absorption, thereby combining this process with the absorption process. Below we will see that there are reasons for this, but in modern literature the terminology we use has been established.)

Stimulated emission occurs when a photona collides with an excited atom. As Einstein showed, a forced transition of an atom from level $E_2$ to a lower level $E_1$ can only stimulate a photon with an energy that satisfies equality (1). Photon is, as it were, just an already unstable excited atom, and it “falls” to a lower energy level, emitting a new photon (Fig. 2, b). In this case, the photon, stimulated by the transition, does not change its energy and direction of its flight. It is important to emphasize that the newly separated photon

So, after the first photoTON meets the excited atom, two perfect identical photoTons fly further, and the atom moves to a lower energy level. The absolute identity of these two photons is manifested not only in the equality of energy and in the coincidence of the direction of their flight, but also in a property called coherence. There is a quantum theory of coherence, especially developed after the advent of lasers; however, this property is described more clearly on the basis of classical, wave concepts without any phase jump or change in direction.

Now we understand why the Eischstein process of stimulated emission is called targeted absorption. After all, absorption and stimulated emission are two “mutually inverse” processes. The initial state in the first process coincides with the final state of the second and vice versa (Fig. 3). In addition, when absorbed, the intensity of the incident light decreases, but its coherence is not disrupted; As a result of stimulated emission, the intensity of the incident light is increased by stimulated emission, which is coherent with the incident light.

Thus, if in the medium there are excited atoms capable of stimulated emission, then the passage of the same level of light during the flow increases its intensity, remaining coherent, and this process is opposite to the absorption process.

It is the coherent absorption of light during stimulated emission that underlies the creation of optical quantum generators—lasers. The word “laser” itself is formed from the initial letters of the English words light amplification by stimulated emission of radiation.

The thing is that under normal conditions there are always more atoms at lower energy levels than at higher ones (Fig. 4). Therefore, in the medium there are more atoms capable of “swallowing” rather than emitting photons, and the number of absorption events exceeds the number of stimulated emission events.

To obtain an amplifying medium, it is necessary to create an inversion of the populations of atoms in energy levels—to create a situation where there are more atoms at the upper levels than at the lower ones (Fig. 5). The resulting avalanche of photons encounters one of the laser mirrors and, having been reflected, goes back through the active medium, which is accompanied by an increase in the avalanche. Then reflection occurs from the second mirror and again the avalanche grows, and so on. With the help of a mirror, we seem to extend the active medium. However, one should not think that even with ideal mirrors that reflect all the photons falling on them, the avalanche will grow indefinitely. The avalanche itself spoils the growth conditions. At a sufficiently high photon flux density, they will “knock down” the excited atoms to a lower energy level so quickly that the population inversion will disappear. But along with stimulated emission, the process of absorption and spontaneous emission occurs. In the process of spontaneous emission, emerging photons propagate in space uniformly in all directions, and their contribution to the photon avalanche is very small and represents non-coregent light. The spontaneous emission of atoms creates a small amount of so-called “noise” in the laser. When, as a result of the disappearance of population inversion, an equilibrium occurs between the number of absorption acts and the number of spontaneous and stimulated emission acts, further amplification of light will cease. (According to the law of conservation of energy, the power released during the emission process becomes the power supplied to excite Ne atoms.)

Now let's find out the role of real mirrors with a reflection coefficient less than one. Without a mirror, a gas-discharge tube with a helium-neon mixture would work as a light amplifier. Mirrors turn the device into a light generator. Using radio engineering terminology, we can say that the mirror provides positive feedback.

When the light wave described above reaches one of the mirrors, it will be partially reflected, travel in the opposite direction through the active medium, again amplifying, and reach the second mirror, where the same thing will be repeated. The wave will “throw” between the mirrors, like a tiger in a cage.

Let us remind you that one of the mirrors is a little bit pronounced. With each reflection from this mirror, a small part of the light wave will be released. The laser beam transforms the sum of these parts into a light wave rushing around in a mirror cage. And if the losses of light in the mirrors (and there are no mirrors without losses) are compensated by the amplification of light in the active medium, a stationary lasing mode will be established. The active medium of a helium-neon laser has a very weak amplifying effect (the intensity of the light flux increases by approximately 10% per meter of path in the active medium). Therefore, the mirrors in such a laser

The glow of the active medium is generally distinguished from the glow of light sources such as an incandescent lamp or a fluorescent lamp. In ordinary light sources, complete chaos reigns - here and there separate flashes of light appear, flowing into a general glow. These light flashes are separate groups of waves emitted by different sources (different excited atoms), and the initial phases of these waves are completely arbitrary, not coordinated in any way. The glow of ordinary light sources resembles the roar of a disorganized, somewhat excited crowd. A completely different picture in the laser. Thanks to acts of stimulated emission, a light wave orders the radiation of individual atoms. Here it resembles a harmonious choir - first one member of the choir enters, then others, the strength of the sound increases. The choir is conducted by the light wave it generates. The phases of individual groups of waves emitted by different atoms are consistent with each other. This is what leads to the coherence of the light emitted by the laser.

So, the laser beam is the sum of the parts of the light wave emerging through a translucent mirror. And since all these particulars are coherent with each other, interface occurs as a result of their addition. As is known, the maximum amplitude of the resulting wave will be obtained if the path differences of the added waves are equal to an integer wavelength. The path length traveled by a laser wave between two mirrors originating from the same mirror is equal to 2L, where L is the distance between the mirrors. Therefore, to obtain a bright output beam, the following conditions must be met:

$$2L = n\lambda,$$

where n is an integer, λ is the light wavelength. Condition (3) has a simple meaning: an integer number of half-waves must be placed along the length of the laser tube (resonant). When vibrating a string with fixed ends, an integer number of half-waves are also placed along the length of the string. This is the condition for the establishment of standing waves. And a standing light wave is established inside the laser (more precisely, almost standing - due to the transparency of one of the mirrors).

Condition (3) can be rewritten as follows:

$$v = n \frac{c}{2L},$$

where c is the speed of light, v = \frac{c}{\lambda} is the frequency.

But the frequency of light emitted by neon atoms during the transition from the E₃ level to the E₂ level is equal to

$$v = \frac{E_3 - E_2}{h}$$

Thus, the frequency of the light generated by the laser must simultaneously satisfy both equation (4) and equation (5). The first equation determines the condition of the resonance between light and the resonance and is classical in nature. Since the mirror resonant has maximum dimensions, light interacts with it as a light wave. Equation (4) is of a quantum nature and determines the condition of the resonance between light and atoms of the active medium, which are represented by microresonants as a flux of photons.

Thus, both wave and quantum luminosities of light appear simultaneously in a laser.

Let us now realize that we are practically familiar with radiation that has a specific frequency $v_0$ (Fig. 12, a), we had to select the size of the resonance - the distance $L$ between the mirrors - such that the natural frequency of the resonance (determined by the conditions for the formation of waste waves) was exactly equal to $v_0$. And this distance would have to be maintained with an accuracy of small fractions of the radiation length. In practice, it is very difficult to satisfy this requirement. Fortunately, this task is made easier by the fact that in fact the frequency of radiation of an atom is not exactly equal to $v_0$, but lies in a certain range of values ​​$v_0 \pm \Delta v$. However, the reason why this happens is the thermal movement of atoms. Since the speeds of individual atoms can be greater or less than the speed of thermal motion, the energy of atoms at a given energy level is not exactly equal to $E_n$, but lies in a certain range of values ​​$E_n \pm \Delta E$ (Fig. 12, b). The frequency interval $|(v - \Delta v), (v + \Delta v)|$ contains several values ​​of resonance natural frequencies (Fig. 13). Therefore, for a given radiation frequency, a powerful laser beam is obtained by tuning the resonance to a non-octoral set of frequencies (corresponding to different values ​​of integers $n$ in conditions (4)). If special measures are not taken, a helian-neon laser emits this entire set of frequencies, although concentrated in a rather narrow region (the width of the spectral line of a neon atom).

There are also single-particle lasers, but they have a special device that selects one of the narrow resonances.

The beam power of a helian-neon laser is low—on the order of one decimal. Despite this, if the beam hits the eye, it can damage your vision. The laser discharge tube consumes power from 20 to 100 W to excite the active medium. Thus, a gel-neon laser converts electrical energy into the energy of a light beam with an efficiency no higher than a fraction of a percent.

At the same time, the laser beam has a number of valuable properties that compensate for low efficiency. First of all, this is the direction of the beam. When we talked about the origin of a photon avalanche, we pointed out that the first photon should fly out parallel to the axis of the discharge tube. The “obliquely” emitted photon and the photons generated by it will quickly go “out” through the side wall of the tube, which does not experience amplification. Reflection from mirrors will only enhance the effect. So a directed beam is preserved inside the tube, the angular divergence of which is only of the order of $10^{-3}$ radius. The high directivity of the beam of a gel-neon laser makes it indispensable for accurately establishing the direction during the construction of various structures and when laying tunnels. The high monochromaticity of the radiation of a gel-neon laser, even not a single-particle one, makes this laser scale a convenient source for the study and practical application of various interference phenomena. In addition to the helian-neon laser, there are a large number of other types of gas lasers that use various atomic and molecular gases as active media.
