Are you familiar with the lens?

There is probably no boy in the world who would not play with a magnifying glass (lens). However, girls too. Do you know the lens well? This can be easily verified experimentally.

1. If you have a glass lens bounded by two convex surfaces (one of which may be flat or even concave), use it to create an image of the Sun on the face of white paper. Rays from the Sun, which is very far from the Earth, fall on the Earth in an almost parallel beam. The lens collects these rays at a point called its focus. The distance from the lens to the focus is called the focal length. Determine the focal length of the lice lens. Think about why a glass lens is sometimes called a “burning glass”?

2. Go to turn on the TV, place the lens between the screen and a sheet of white paper. By mixing the sheet, you will get a clear image of the television screen on it. What will this image be like?

By the way, they say that a camera works in exactly the same way, only there the role of a white sheet of paper is played by photographic film or photographic plastic.

It is not difficult to obtain an image of a window, a lamp turned on, or a distant building. maybe you even managed to “photograph” your product in this way.

Try to rotate the lens along an axis perpendicular to the cross-sectional plane of the lens. Does the image rotate?

4. In principle, any product of a body with a non-planar surface forms a lens. So, glass balls magnify very well, and it turns out that the smaller the balls, the greater his notice. It was the small ball that was the main part in the first microscope.

Glass rods (you can find them in the school chemistry room) are also lenses.

Fermat's theorem helps to search for extremum points of the function $f$ as follows. According to this theorem, the extremum points can only be those internal points of the region $D(f)$ at which the derivative exists and is equal to zero, or points at which the derivative is not defined. All such points are called “critical” for a given function.

Having found the critical points and monotonicity intervals of a given function, it is often possible to find the maximum or minimum points of this function. Thus, in the example considered above, at the critical point $x$ 1 the function has a minimum due to the fact that it kills on the interval [0; 1] and returns to [1; +∞].

In most practical problems, however, it is required to find not extrema, but the smallest value of some function (raw materials, materials, costs) or the smallest (products, approximations, etc.). What is the difference between these concepts?

When we say that the function $f$ has an extremum at the point $x_0$, let’s say, a maximum, that we thereby describe the behavior of the function in close_TO, that it kills on the interval [0; 1] and returns to [1; +∞].

Note also that if a function is given on an interval, then it may not have a minimum or least value on this interval. This is, for example, the function $y(x)$ on the interval [0; 1]: it reaches neither the smallest nor the smallest value. Indeed, at the left end of the interval $y(x)$ becomes arbitrarily small, but does not take on the value 0, and on the right “does not reach” the value 1. Another example: the function

$$y(x) = \frac{x-2}{(x-1)(x-3)},$$

specified on the interval [1; 3]. As $x$ approaches 1, the values ​​of the function increase without limit (the function is said to approach the “infinity plane”). When $x$ approaches 3, the values ​​of the function will increase indefinitely (the function tends to “minus infinity”!) There are not even values ​​here that the function could “reach”.

Note that for differentiable functions defined on an interval, such inconsistency cannot arise.

Example 2 (MEIS *, 1977). Find the smallest and largest values ​​of the function $y(x)=x-2\ln x$ on the interval [1; $e$].

Here $y'=1-2/x$. Equating the derivative to zero and solving the resulting equation, we find: $x=2$. Since $1<2<e$, this point is critical. Further, analyzing the formula $y'=1-2/x$, we see that the derivative is not defined at $x=0$, but this point is outside the interval [1; $e$]. This means that the function is defined and differentiable on the interval [1; $e$] and therefore, according to the above rule, is obliged to take the largest and smallest value, and these values ​​will each be at one of three points: $x=1$, $x=e$ (border points), $x=2$ (critical point). We have: $y(1)=1$, $y(2)=2-2\ln 2$, $y(e)=e-2$. Having estimated these numbers, we can show that $y(2)$ is the smallest, and $y(1)$ is the largest value of this function on the interval [1; $e$], but comparing $2-2\ln 2$ and $e-2$ is not easy. Let us examine the behavior of functions on this segment. Constructing a diagram of the signs of the derivative and the increase and decrease of the function $y$ (Fig. 2), we find that the function $y$ kills on [1; 2] and increases by [2; $e$]. This means that the minimum value of the function $y$ exists and is achieved at $x=2$ (and this is the minimum point). The highest value also exists and is achieved at one of the points $x=1$, $x=e$ (but none of them is the maximum point!). Comparing $y(1)=1$ and $y(e)=e-2$, we find that $e-2<2,9-2=0,9<1$. This means that the largest value of the function is $y(1)=1$.

In a similar way (by the signs of the derivative) all critical points of the function are examined when it is necessary to find all the extrema of the function and indicate their nature. The general rule here is this.

If at the point $x_0$ the derivative changes the sign from “plus” to “minus”, then $x_0$ is the power of the maximum of the function;

If at the point $x_0$ the derivative changes the sign from “minus” to “plus”, then $x_0$ is the power of the minimum of the function;

3. "Text" problems on extrema

Above we focused on the study of functions using derivatives. The need for such research will arise when solving a wide variety of applied “text” problems. The general scheme for solving these problems is as follows. We describe the conditions of the problem with variables and equations and calculate the function whose extremum must be found. This function usually depends on many variables, but with the help of an equation we express all the variables in terms of one, substitute them into the expression for the function and obtain a function of one variable, after which we carry out the study using the standard method.

Example 4 (MEIS, 1977). Find the cone of the largest volume, forming a Comorodo we have the given length $l$.

The volume of a cone is expressed in terms of the height $H$ and the radius of the base of the cone $R$ as follows:

$$V = \frac{1}{3} \pi R^2 H.$$

In principle, it makes no difference, but in practice we must take into account that we will have to cut the data from the equation $V'=0$, so it is better to leave the variable through which V is expressed most simply. In this case we have: $R^2+H^2=l^2$, hence $R^2=l^2-H^2$, and also $H^2=l^2-R^2$. We get:

$$V(H) = \frac{1}{3} \pi (l^2-H^2) H,$$

$$V(R) = \frac{1}{3} \pi R^2 \sqrt{l^2-R^2}.$$

It can be seen that it is better to leave H, then

$$V(H) = \frac{1}{3} \pi l^2 H - \frac{1}{3} \pi H^3,$$

$$V'(H) = \frac{1}{3} \pi l^2 - \pi H^2.$$

The function V is defined (in the sense of the problem) at $H \in [0; l]$. There is only one critical point: $H = l/\sqrt{3}$. Constructing a diagram of the signs of an arbitrary function and the behavior of the function (Fig. 4), we see that $H = l/\sqrt{3}$ is the maximum point, and at it the maximum value of the function V is achieved, since before this point V returns, and after it it decreases.

Note that if the problem asked at what radius of the base the volume of the cone is greatest, our solution would not change. We would first find $V(H)$, the point of maximum (and greatest value)

$$H = l/\sqrt{3}, \text{ а потом}$$
$$R = \sqrt{l^2 - (l/\sqrt{3})^2} = l\sqrt{2/3}.$$
