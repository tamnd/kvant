Fig. 7. Energy distribution of electrons.

All electrons have the same energy, equal to the average energy $E$ of their thermal motion, then none of them could excite the atom. At a given temperature (at a given value $E$), the energies $\frac{mv^2}{2}$ of the thermal motion of individual electrons differ from the value $E = \frac{mv^2}{2}$ - there is, as they say, a spread of electron energies. figure 7 shows a graph of the electron energy distribution at a given temperature. It can be seen that only a small part of the electrons have an energy above the excitation threshold atom.

The population level depends on events, but also on the life expectancy of the atom at a given energy level. It is clear that the longer the life expectancy at a given level, the more atoms accumulate on it. Therefore, to obtain population inversion, it is advantageous to have an upper level with a longer life expectancy, and a lower level with a shorter one. For a laser operating in continuous mode, this condition is strict.

figure 8a shows the control methods diagram of intersuccesses at the neon atom levels. Transitions between levels $E_3$ and $E_2$ are accompanied by the emission of a red line with a wavelength of 0.6328 μm. The lifespan of atoms at the $E_3$ level is approximately $2 \cdot 10^{-7}$ s, and at the $E_2 = 10^{-8}$ s level, i.e., approximately five times less, which is favorable for obtaining an inversion. However, up to the $E_3$ level, atoms can only arise from electrons with energies exceeding $E_3 - E_1$, and to the lower level $E_2$ - by electrons with energies exceeding $E_2 - E_1$*). Since $E_3 - E_1 > E_2 - E_1$, this situation does not favor inversion. The higher the level, the more difficult it is for electrons to excite atoms to this level. As a result of the play of these two factors, it is very difficult to obtain the inversion in pure neon. This forces the use of a mixture of gel and neon, and there is approximately ten times more gel in this mixture than neon.

*) The following situation is also possible: an atom, which has joined an electron and moved to an excited level, manages during its lifetime to collide with a second electron and move to a higher level (such excitation is called stepwise). However, the concentration of excited atoms is small (due to the very short lifespan of atoms in an excited state), and therefore, under normal conditions, the probability of such an event is nothing.
