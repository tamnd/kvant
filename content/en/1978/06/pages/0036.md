Problem 1. Prove that a mapping of a plane onto itself if and only if is a symmetry with center $O$ when its only fixed point is the point $O$ (i.e. $O'=O$ and for $A \neq O$ we have $A' \neq A$), it transforms any line into a parallel line and there is a point $B$ for which $|B'O|=|BO|$.

The theorem established in Problem 1 allows us to give the concept of central symmetry a second definition: a mapping of a plane onto itself is called a symmetry with center $O$ if its only fixed point is the point $O$, it transforms any line into a parallel line and there is a point $B$ for which $|B'O|=|BO|$.

In the first definition, we constructed a certain object (a mapping of a plane onto itself) and then a contortion. The second definition looks like this: an object of a neo-city is called *mak-mo* if it has such and such properties.

Definitions of the first type are called constructive or direct, definitions of the second type are called descriptive (from the Latin descriptio - “description”) or indirect.

Let me immediately emphasize: it was not for nothing that we declared the concept of constructive and descriptive definitions only by example - they hardly allow a strict description. Despite their vagueness and vagueness, the opposition of constructive and descriptive definitions is often useful.

If a concept is defined descriptively, then the corresponding object does not necessarily exist. Let us say, for example, that a natural number other than 1 is perfect if it is equal to the sum of its divisors. In this case, the definition we gave did not define any object, because the number of divisors of the number $n$ also includes $n$ itself; There are no perfect numbers*).

Thus, unlike constructive definitions, any descriptive definition also requires proof that the corresponding object exists.

Let's consider another example of a descriptive definition: straight lines $a$ and $b$ lying in the same plane are called *converging* if they do not have common points and when a point moves along one of these straight lines in one of the directions, the distance from it to the other straight line is unlimited (Fig. 3). Does this definition define any object? In other words, are there straight lines? It turns out that the answer to this question depends on what geometry we “are in”, what geometry we are considering.

In included geometry (or, which is the same for this question, with the axiomatics that are accepted in the school textbook), convergent lines, of course, do not exist (that is why we were forced to depict the lines on figure 3 as artificial). In Lobachevsky’s geometry, straight lines also exist (they are called *parallel*).
