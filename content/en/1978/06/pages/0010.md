The resulting avalanche of photons encounters one of the laser mirrors and, having been reflected, goes back through the active medium, which is accompanied by an increase in the avalanche. Then reflection occurs from the second mirror and again the avalanche grows, and so on. With the help of a mirror, we seem to extend the active medium. However, one should not think that even with ideal mirrors that reflect all the photons falling on them, the avalanche will grow indefinitely. The avalanche itself spoils the growth conditions. At a sufficiently high photon flux density, they will “knock down” the excited atoms to a lower energy level so quickly that the population inversion will disappear. But along with stimulated emission, the process of absorption and spontaneous emission occurs. In the process of spontaneous emission, emerging photons propagate in space uniformly in all directions, and their contribution to the photon avalanche is very small and represents non-coregent light. The spontaneous emission of atoms creates a small amount of so-called “noise” in the laser. When, as a result of the disappearance of population inversion, an equilibrium occurs between the number of absorption acts and the number of spontaneous and stimulated emission acts, further amplification of light will cease. (According to the law of conservation of energy, the power released during the emission process becomes the power supplied to excite Ne atoms.)

Now let's find out the role of real mirrors with a reflection coefficient less than one. Without a mirror, a gas-discharge tube with a helium-neon mixture would work as a light amplifier. Mirrors turn the device into a light generator. Using radio engineering terminology, we can say that the mirror provides positive feedback.

When the light wave described above reaches one of the mirrors, it will be partially reflected, travel in the opposite direction through the active medium, again amplifying, and reach the second mirror, where the same thing will be repeated. The wave will “throw” between the mirrors, like a tiger in a cage.

Let us remind you that one of the mirrors is a little bit pronounced. With each reflection from this mirror, a small part of the light wave will be released. The laser beam transforms the sum of these parts into a light wave rushing around in a mirror cage. And if the losses of light in the mirrors (and there are no mirrors without losses) are compensated by the amplification of light in the active medium, a stationary lasing mode will be established. The active medium of a helium-neon laser has a very weak amplifying effect (the intensity of the light flux increases by approximately 10% per meter of path in the active medium).
