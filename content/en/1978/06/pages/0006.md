THE TONE IS THE “TWIN” OF THE PHOTON THAT STIMULATED ITS BIRTH - it carries the same energy and flies in the same direction.

So, after the first photoTON meets the excited atom, two perfect identical photoTons fly further, and the atom moves to a lower energy level. The absolute identity of these two photons is manifested not only in the equality of energy and in the coincidence of the direction of their flight, but also in a property called coherence. There is a quantum theory of coherence, especially developed after the advent of lasers; however, this property is described more clearly on the basis of classical, wave concepts without any phase jump or change in direction.

Now we understand why the Eischstein process of stimulated emission is called targeted absorption. After all, absorption and stimulated emission are two “mutually inverse” processes. The initial state in the first process coincides with the final state of the second and vice versa (Fig. 3). In addition, when absorbed, the intensity of the incident light decreases, but its coherence is not disrupted; As a result of stimulated emission, the intensity of the incident light is increased by stimulated emission, which is coherent with the incident light.

Thus, if in the medium there are excited atoms capable of stimulated emission, then the passage of the same level of light during the flow increases its intensity, remaining coherent, and this process is opposite to the absorption process.

It is the coherent absorption of light during stimulated emission that underlies the creation of optical quantum generators—lasers. The word “laser” itself is formed from the initial letters of the English words light amplification by stimulated emission of radiation.

Now we can explain why different environments usually weaken rather than enhance light. The thing is that under normal conditions there are always more atoms at lower energy levels than at higher ones (Fig. 4). Therefore, in the medium there are more atoms capable of “swallowing” rather than emitting photons, and the number of absorption events exceeds the number of stimulated emission events.

To obtain an amplifying medium, it is necessary to create an inversion of the populations of atoms in energy levels—to create a situation where there are more atoms at the upper levels than at the lower ones (Fig. 5).
