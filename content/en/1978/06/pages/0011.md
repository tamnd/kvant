The glow of the active medium is generally distinguished from the glow of light sources such as an incandescent lamp or a fluorescent lamp. In ordinary light sources, complete chaos reigns - here and there separate flashes of light appear, flowing into a general glow. These light flashes are separate groups of waves emitted by different sources (different excited atoms), and the initial phases of these waves are completely arbitrary, not coordinated in any way. The glow of ordinary light sources resembles the roar of a disorganized, somewhat excited crowd. A completely different picture in the laser. Thanks to acts of stimulated emission, a light wave orders the radiation of individual atoms. Here it resembles a harmonious choir - first one member of the choir enters, then others, the strength of the sound increases. The choir is conducted by the light wave it generates. The phases of individual groups of waves emitted by different atoms are consistent with each other. This is what leads to the coherence of the light emitted by the laser.

So, the laser beam is the sum of the parts of the light wave emerging through a translucent mirror. And since all these particulars are coherent with each other, interface occurs as a result of their addition. As is known, the maximum amplitude of the resulting wave will be obtained if the path differences of the added waves are equal to an integer wavelength. The path length traveled by a laser wave between two mirrors originating from the same mirror is equal to 2L, where L is the distance between the mirrors. Therefore, to obtain a bright output beam, the following conditions must be met:

$$2L = n\lambda,$$

where n is an integer, λ is the light wavelength. Condition (3) has a simple meaning: an integer number of half-waves must be placed along the length of the laser tube (resonant). When vibrating a string with fixed ends, an integer number of half-waves are also placed along the length of the string. This is the condition for the establishment of standing waves. And a standing light wave is established inside the laser (more precisely, almost standing - due to the transparency of one of the mirrors).

Condition (3) can be rewritten as follows:

$$v = n \frac{c}{2L},$$

where c is the speed of light, v = \frac{c}{\lambda} is the frequency.

But the frequency of light emitted by neon atoms during the transition from the E₃ level to the E₂ level is equal to

$$v = \frac{E_3 - E_2}{h}$$

Thus, the frequency of the light generated by the laser must simultaneously satisfy both equation (4) and equation (5). The first equation determines the condition of the resonance between light and the resonance and is classical in nature. Since the mirror resonant has maximum dimensions, light interacts with it as a light wave. Equation (4) is of a quantum nature and determines the condition of the resonance between light and atoms of the active medium, which are represented by microresonants as a flux of photons.

Thus, both wave and quantum luminosities of light appear simultaneously in a laser.
