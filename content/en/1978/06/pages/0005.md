As is known, according to quantum concepts, the internal energy of atoms can only change in jumps. An atom, as we say, can be in a Stationary state at one of the energy levels (Fig. 1), corresponding to a wave of certain reserves of internal energy. Optical transitions of an atom from one level to another are accompanied by the emission or absorption of photons by the atom. According to the law of conservation of energy, the equality must be fulfilled

$$hv = E_2 - E_1,$$

(1)

where $hv$ is the photon energy ($h$ is Planck’s constant, $v$ is the frequency of light), $E_1$ and $E_2$ are the energies of the Stationary states of the atom, between which a transition is passed. It is clear that to move from one energy level to a higher one, an atom must absorb photons. The energy of absorbed photons goes to increase the internal energy of the atom. On the contrary, during transitions to a lower level, the atom must absorb photons that carry away the excess internal energy of the atom.

Einstein pointed out that there is one type of photon absorption processes and two types of photon emission processes (Fig. 2). In the process of absorption, the atom, as it were, swallows the photon that approaches it and moves to a higher energy level (Fig. 2, a). The process of emission of a photon by an atom can occur spontaneously - spontaneously, without any external influence (Fig. 2, b). Of course, in order for spontaneous emission of a photon to occur, the atom must first be excited. Spontaneous emission fails due to the instability of the excited state of the atom. The lifespan of isolated excited atoms is different for different states. (For shorter lifespans of excited atoms, we are satisfied - on the order of $10^{-8}$ s.)

The most important thing for lasers was Einstein’s discovery of the existence of a second type of processes using

releasing photons - the so-called forced, or stimulated, emission. (We deliberately deviated somewhat from the systematics of optical processes given by Einstein himself. He called stimulated emission negative absorption, thereby combining this process with the absorption process. Below we will see that there are reasons for this, but in modern literature the terminology we use has been established.)

Stimulated emission occurs when a photona collides with an excited atom. As Einstein showed, a forced transition of an atom from level $E_2$ to a lower level $E_1$ can only stimulate a photon with an energy that satisfies equality (1). Photon is, as it were, just an already unstable excited atom, and it “falls” to a lower energy level, emitting a new photon (Fig. 2, b).
