⟦folio 24⟧

The third law was found later, in 1618, it is given in Kepler’s book “The Harmony of the World,” published in 1619. Now this law is formulated as follows: the squares of the periods of revolutions of the planets around the Sun are proportional to the cubes of the semi-major axes of their elliptical orbits.

Kepler spent ten years in Prague. This was the most creatively fruitful period of his life. Here, in Prague, Kepler wrote two fundamental treatises on optics: “Addition to Vitell, which expounds the optical part of astronomy” (1604) and “Dioptrics” (1611).

The first book was written, indeed, as an addition to the well-known treatise of the useful scientist of the 12th century, Vitellus, which was well known at that time. Kepler is left with many questions concerning geometric and physiological optics. He introduced the terms “optical axis” and “meniscus”, “convergence” and “divergence” of light beams into optics, created a theory of the mechanism of vision, which basically coincides with the modern one, explained the action of converging and diverging spectacle lenses that correct farsightedness and myopia, and came close to developing the theory of optical instruments.

The second book (its full title is "Dioptrics, or the proof of how an image becomes visible by means of the newly invented visual tour") is the result of further research related to optical instruments, especially after the creation of the first telesekon by Galileo in 1609. Kepler used the term “dioptrics” to refer to the branch of optics that deals with the phenomenon of refraction of light (in contrast to ενClidean catoptrics, which studies the reflection of light). It is amazing that, without knowing the correct formulation of the law of refraction of light (this law was found for a particular case by Sneglin in 1617, and for the general case by Descartes in 1637), Kepler managed to construct a generally correct theory of the operation of optical instruments and, in particular, visual labor.

Kepler's relatively prosperous ten-year period in Prague was over. At the end of 1610, his wife Barbara fell ill. The couples find her changing linaradka. Kepler's children fell ill with wasp, and in February 1611 his son Friedrich died.

These days, Prague becomes the scene of military action. It is Rudolf II and his brother Matvey who are fighting for the commonwealth. On May 23, 1611, Rudolf II renounced prostitution. Matvey becomes Queen of the Czech Republic, and soon the Emperor of the Holy Roman Empire. Kepler is left without his high patron, and although Matvey leaves the scientist in the position of court mathematician, he decides to leave Prague. At the end of May, Kepler was already in Linz, the capital of Upper Australia, where he offered his services to the class assembly as a teacher and provincial mathematician. His proposal is accepted, and Kepler returns to Prague for his family. At home he finds him getting married
