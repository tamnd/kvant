M466. Average 1977 coins 50 counterfeit. Each counterfeit coin differs from the real one by 1 g (in one direction or the other). There are bowl scales with an arrow showing the difference in mass between one bowl and another. In one weighing, you need to find out about one selected coin whether it is counterfeit or tuned. How to do this?

M467. Points D and E divide the sides AC and AB of the correct predecessor ABC in the relations |AD|:|DC| = |BE|:|EA| = 1:2. Receivers BD and CE intersect at point O. Prove that AOC is straight.

Let there be 2n+1 coins, of which 2k are counterfeit (k ≤ n), and each counterfeit coin differs from the real one by 1 g (in one direction or the other). Let the mass of a real coin be a grams. Let's set aside one selected coin, and from the remaining 2n coins, put n coins on the first pan of the scales and n coins on the second. Note that the mass of gry on each scale differs from na (the mass of n real coins) by an integer number of the same parity as the number of counterfeit coins on this scale.

If the set aside coin is in tune, then the total number of counterfeit coins on the cups is 2k (even), which means that the number of them on both cups has the same parity, therefore, the difference in the masses of one and the other cup will be even. If the chosen coin is counterfeit, then the number of counterfeit coins lying on the scales is odd - (2k-1); then the reading of the scales is odd.

So, we see that the chosen coin is in tune or counterfeit depending on whether the scale reading is even or odd.

MS·MD are not equal to another friend. Let us prove that $\overrightarrow{AC} = \overrightarrow{DB}$. Is the opposite statement true?

Let $M$ be an arbitrary point on the plane, $(x; y)$ its coordinates. Then the vectors $\overrightarrow{MA}, \overrightarrow{MB}, \overrightarrow{MC}, \overrightarrow{MD}$ have coordinates $(-x; -y), (b_1-x; b_2-y), (c_1-x; c_2-y), (d_1-x; d_2-y)$ respectively. The equality $\overrightarrow{MA} \cdot \overrightarrow{MB} = \overrightarrow{MC} \cdot \overrightarrow{MD}$ will be written in coordinates as follows:
$$-x (b_1-x) - y (b_2-y) = (c_1-x)(d_1-x) + (c_2-y)(d_2-y),$$
or
$$(c_1 + d_1 - b_1) x + (c_2 + d_2 - b_2) y = c_1 d_1 + c_2 d_2.$$ (*)

The statement “for any point $M$ the scalar products $\overrightarrow{MA} \cdot \overrightarrow{MB}$ and $\overrightarrow{MC} \cdot \overrightarrow{MD}$ are not equal to each other” means that equality (*), considered as an equation with two unknowns $x, y$, has no solutions. This is possible if and only if
$$\begin{cases} c_1 + d_1 - b_1 = 0, \\ c_2 + d_2 - b_2 = 0, \\ c_1 d_1 + c_2 d_2 \neq 0. \end{cases}$$
Rewriting the first two equalities in the form
$$\begin{cases} c_1 = b_1 - d_1, \\ c_2 = b_2 - d_2 \end{cases}$$
and noting that $(c_1; c_2)$ are the coordinates of the vector $\overrightarrow{AC}$, and $(b_1-d_1; b_2-d_2)$ are the coordinates of the vector $\overrightarrow{DB}$, we will see that this system of equalities is equivalent to the equality of the vectors $\overrightarrow{AC}$ and $\overrightarrow{DB}$.

The condition $c_1 d_1 + c_2 d_2 \neq 0$ means that $\overrightarrow{AC} \cdot \overrightarrow{AD} \neq 0$, that is, the vectors $\overrightarrow{AC}$ and $\overrightarrow{AD}$ are not mutually perpendicular.

So, the condition "for any point $M$ the scalar products $\overrightarrow{MA} \cdot \overrightarrow{MB}$ and $\overrightarrow{MC} \cdot \overrightarrow{MD}$ are not equal to each other" is equivalent to the following: "$\overrightarrow{AC} = \overrightarrow{DB}$ and $\overrightarrow{AC} \cdot \overrightarrow{AD} \neq 0$".

If the points $A, B, C, D$ do not lie on the same line, then the equality $\overrightarrow{AC} = \overrightarrow{DB}$ means that the quadrilateral $ACBD$ is a parallelogram, and the inequality $\overrightarrow{AC} \cdot \overrightarrow{AD} \neq 0$ means that this parallelogram is not a straight line.

F483. What horizontal acceleration must be caused by a wedge with an angle of $\alpha$ (Fig. 5) so that the load lying on it rises upward if the coefficient of friction between the load and the wedge is $\mu$?

Fig. 5.

Obviously, the acceleration $a$ of the wedge is directed to the left (otherwise the load would slide off the wedge rather than rise along it).

Let's consider the forces acting on the load. This is the gravitational force $mg$, the normal reaction force $N$ and the friction force $F_{Тр}$ as well as $|F_{Тр}| = \mu |N|$.

Let us write down the equations of motion of the load in projections on the $X$ and $Y$ axes. In order for the load to rise upward, the acceleration projection of the load onto the $X$ axis must be less than or equal to the corresponding acceleration projection of the wedge. Taking into account Newton's second law, we obtain

$$\frac{\mu |N| + m |g| \sin \alpha}{m} \leq |a| \cos \alpha .$$

The projection of the acceleration of the load onto the $Y$ axis is the same as for the wedge (the load does not come off the wedge):

$$\frac{|N| - m |g| \cos \alpha}{m} = |a| \sin \alpha .$$

Solving (1) and (2) together, we find

$$|\vec{a}| \geq \frac{\sin \alpha + \mu \cos \alpha}{\cos \alpha - \mu \sin \alpha} |\vec{g}|.$$

F486. figure 11 shows a diagram of the mass spectrometer. In conzamore A they form sons, which are accelerated at the applied $U = 10$ kV and enter through the target $C$ into a magnetic field with induction $|\vec{B}| = 0,1$ T. After turning, the sons fall on the phomoeffic plate $D$ and cause it to blacken. At what distance from each other will the son stripes $H^+$, $2H^+$, $3H^+$, $He^+$ be located on the plate? What must be the width of the targets for the son bands $^{16}O^+$ and $^{15}N^+$ to separate?

Fig. 11.

Accelerated by an electric field, they acquire kinetic energy

$$\frac{mv^2}{2} = qU,$$

where $m$ is the mass of the son and $q$ is its charge. Consequently, they enter the mass spectrometer chamber at a speed

$$|\vec{v}| = \sqrt{\frac{2qU}{m}}.$$

In this chamber they are acted upon by the Lorentz force

$$|\vec{F}| = |\vec{v}| |\vec{B}| q,$$

due to which they move in a circle of radius $R$. According to Newton's second law

$$\frac{mv^2}{R} = |\vec{v}| |\vec{B}| q,$$

where

$$R = \frac{m |\vec{v}|}{|\vec{B}| q} = \frac{1}{|\vec{B}|} \sqrt{\frac{2mU}{q}}.$$

Ions of the same type are collected on photoplastic at a distance of $l = 2R$ from the targets. The distance between sweet sons with the same charges $q$, but different masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ is

$$\Delta l = 2\Delta R = \frac{2}{|\vec{B}|} \sqrt{\frac{2U}{q}} (\sqrt{m_2} - \sqrt{m_1}).$$

So, for dreams $H^+$ and $2H^+$

$$\Delta l = \frac{2}{|\vec{B}|} \sqrt{\frac{2U}{q}} (\sqrt{2m_p} - \sqrt{m_p}) \approx 0,12 \text{ м},$$

where $q = 1,6 \cdot 10^{-19}$ K is the charge of monovalent sons, $m_p = 1,67 \cdot 10^{-27}$ kg is the proton mass.

For dreams $2H^+$ and $3H^+$

$$\Delta l = \frac{2}{|\vec{B}|} \sqrt{\frac{2U}{q}} (\sqrt{3m_p} - \sqrt{2m_p}) \approx 0,09 \text{ м},$$

and for $3H^+$ and $He^+$

$$\Delta l = \frac{2}{|\vec{B}|} \sqrt{\frac{2U}{q}} (\sqrt{4m_p} - \sqrt{3m_p}) \approx 0,08 \text{ м}.$$

Obviously, the son traces on the photoplastic will not overlap if the son width $d$ is less than $\Delta l$. In particular, for sones $^{16}O^+$ and $^{15}N^+$

$$d < \frac{2}{|\vec{B}|} \sqrt{\frac{2U}{q}} (\sqrt{16m_p} - \sqrt{15m_p}) \approx 0,04 \text{ м}.$$
