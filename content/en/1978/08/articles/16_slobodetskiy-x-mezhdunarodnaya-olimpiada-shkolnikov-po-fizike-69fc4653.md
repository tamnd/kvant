X International Olympiad for Schoolchildren in Physics

Last July, the X International Physics Olympiad took place in the city of Hradec Kralove, Czechoslovakia. Schoolchildren from 12 European countries took part in it: Bulgaria, Hungary, East Germany, Poland, Romania, USSR, Finland, France, Germany, Czechoslovakia, Sweden and Yugoslavia. Each country's team consisted of five participants.

The USSR team included the winners of the XI All-Union Schoolchildren Olympiad

Andrey Ganopolsky - graduate of secondary school No. 88 in Minsk,
Vladimir Reshetov is a graduate of boarding school No. 18 at Moscow State University,
Konstantin Tremyachenko - graduate of secondary school No. 145 in Kyiv,
Ruslan Sharipov is a graduate of secondary school No. 18 in Karakul, Bukhara region. UzSSR.
Vitaly Shukin is a graduate of Physics and Mathematics School No. 45 at Leningrad State University.
The team leaders were the head of the laboratory of the Research Institute of Content and Teaching Methods of the USSR Academy of Pedagogical Sciences O. F. Kabarin and the inspector of the USSR Ministry of Education M. V. Grabilenkov.

Before the Olympics, members of the Soviet team attended a three-week training camp, held at the experimental school No. 82 of the Academy of Pedagogical Sciences of the USSR in the village of Chernogolovka, Moscow region. During the training camp, the children were given lectures, mainly on those issues that are included in the program of the International Olympiad, but are not studied in the USSR school. They also solved problems and performed laboratory work.

The X International Olympiad was organized by the Ministry of School of the Czech Socialist Republic together with the Ministry of School of the Slovak Socialist Republic, the Socialist Youth Union, and the Society of Human Mathematicians and Physicists. The actual conduct of the Olympiad was led by the Organizing Committee and the International Commission (which included team leaders). The Chairman of the Organizing Committee and the International Commission was Corresponding Member of the Academy of Sciences of the Czechoslovak Socialist Republic M. Matyash.

On July 8, the grand opening of the Olympiad took place; on July 9 and 11, the Olympiad participants solved the problems of the theoretical and experimental rounds. In the theoretical type, it was proposed to solve three problems (they were given five hours), and in the experimental type, one problem was given (in four hours). These are the tasks.

Experimental tour

As usual at International Olympiads, the correct solution to a theoretical problem was scored 10 points, and an experimental one - 20 points. The results of each participant were determined by summing up the points they scored.

We will not present solutions to theoretical problems here, since two of them - the second and third - are placed in the Problem Book «Kvant» (F488 and F489)*). Let us only note that the Olympiad participants coped quite successfully with the third and first tasks. The average score for solving the third problem was 8.1, and for solving the first - 6. The second problem turned out to be the most difficult: more than half of the participants received less than 3 points for solving it (the average score was only 3.8 points). When solving this problem, many were unable to correctly determine the path difference of the interfering rays. The best performers in this task were Czechoslovakian (41 points out of 50) and Soviet (32 out of 50) schoolchildren.

The Olympiad participants coped with the experimental task very well: a third of the participants scored 19-20 points for solving the problem (while last year only 8% of the participants successfully completed the experimental task).

Let us dwell in more detail on the solution of the experimental problem. As can be seen from her condition, the contents of the “black box” were not secret. The only requirements are to determine which terminals of the box are connected to which circuit elements and what are the parameters of these elements. To solve the problem, a modification scheme was proposed and the sequence of changes and processing of the received data was indicated. The main difficulty was the theoretical understanding of the obtained graphs of the dependence of impedances on frequency.

figure 6 shows graphs of the dependence of impedances $Z_{AB}, Z_{BD}$ and $Z_{AD}$ on frequency v. The graphs are plotted on a logarithmic scale. The dots indicate specific results of changes. How to use these curves to solve the remaining points of the problem?

Consider a circuit located in a black box (see Fig. 4). If it is connected to the circuit by points 2 and 3, then

$$Z_{23} = \frac{1}{\omega C_{06\pi}} = \frac{1}{2\pi v} \left( \frac{1}{C_1} + \frac{1}{C_2} \right).$$

Soviet team at the X International Physical Olympiad for schoolchildren. From left to right: R. Sharipov, O. F. Kabardin and M. V. Grabilenkov (team leaders), K. Tretyachenko, V. Shukin, A. Gonopolsky and V. Reshetov.

From here

$$\lg Z_{23} = \lg \left( \frac{C_1 + C_2}{2\pi C_1 C_2} \right) - \lg v.$$

This means that $\lg Z_{23}$ depends linearly on the logarithm of frequency $v$. This dependence of $Z$ on $v$ corresponds to the red line on figure 6.

For impedances $Z_{12}$ and $Z_{13}$ we have:

$$Z_{12}^2 = R^2 + \frac{1}{(2\pi v)^2 C_1^2},$$

$$Z_{13}^2 = R^2 + \frac{1}{(2\pi v)^2 C_2^2}.$$

At low frequencies, when $2\pi v \ll \frac{1}{RC_1}$ and $2\pi v \ll \frac{1}{RC_2}$,

$$Z_{12} \approx \frac{1}{2\pi v C_1} \text{ и } Z_{13} \sim \frac{1}{2\pi v C_2},$$

where

$$\lg Z_{12} \approx \lg \frac{1}{2\pi C_1} - \lg v,$$

$$\lg Z_{13} \approx \lg \frac{1}{2\pi C_2} - \lg v.$$

Thus, at low frequencies the logarithms of impedances $Z_{12}$ and $Z_{13}$ also depend linearly on the logarithm of frequency. The matching graphs should be parallel to the graph of $Z_{23}$ versus $v$.

At high frequencies, when $v \to \infty$

$$\left( \frac{1}{2\pi v} \to 0 \right),$$

$$Z_{12} \to R \text{ и } Z_{13} \to R,$$

or

$$\lg Z_{12} \to \lg R \text{ и } \lg Z_{13} \to \lg R.$$

Therefore, at high frequencies, the plots of $Z_{12}$ and $Z_{13}$ versus $v$ should have a common asymptote $Z = R$.

This dependence of $Z$ on $v$ corresponds to the black and blue graphs on figure 6. From this we can conclude that point $I$ of the circuit is connected to terminal $D$ of the box, and points $2$ and $3$ are connected to terminals $A$ and $B$.

The values ​​of capacitor capacitances and resistor resistances can be found in different ways. For example, it is enough to determine $Z_{AB}, Z_{AD}$ and $Z_{BD}$ with a known frequency $v$. Then from the control system

$$\begin{cases} Z_{AD}^2 - Z_{BD}^2 = Z_{12}^2 - Z_{13}^2 = \\ = \frac{1}{(2\pi v)^2 C_1^2} - \frac{1}{(2\pi v)^2 C_2^2}, \\ Z_{AB} = Z_{23} = \frac{1}{2\pi v} \frac{C_1 + C_2}{C_1 C_2} \end{cases}$$

it is not difficult to find $C_1$ and $C_2$. Then from the expression

$$Z_{AD} = Z_{12} = \sqrt{R^2 + \frac{1}{(2\pi v)^2 C_1}}$$

can be found $R$.

It is convenient to use a graphical method for determining the parameters of the circuit.

As we have already said, at low frequencies the graphs of the dependence of $Z_{AD}$ on $v$ and $Z_{BD}$ on $v$ are parallel to the graph of the dependence of $Z_{AB}$ on $v$.

Pozny, if the asymptotes of the first two graphs are drawn parallel to the third graph (black and blue dotted lines), they will intersect the $v$ axis at points $v_1$ and $v_2$, for which the approximate equalities are valid

$$\lg Z_0 = \lg \frac{1}{2\pi C_1} - \lg v_1$$

And

$$\lg Z_0 = \lg \frac{1}{2\pi C_2} - \lg v_2$$

where $Z_0 = 1$ kOhm (see Fig. 6). From here we find $C_1$ and $C_2$.

To determine $R$, it is necessary to carry out the general asymptote of the graphs of the dependence of $Z_{AD}$ on $v$ and $Z_{BD}$ on $v$ for $v \rightarrow \infty$. This asymptote will intersect the $Z$ axis at $Z = R$.

Was it necessary to take into account the current; current through the voltmeter (see Fig. 5)? If it is not taken into account, the resulting impedance values ​​are somewhat underestimated compared to the true ones. However, this should only have an effect at such frequency values ​​$v$ at which the current consumed by the “black box” is comparable to the current sought through the voltmeter. The current consumed by the voltmeter at a high deflection of the instrument needle was known from the condition, so it was not difficult to assess the influence of this current on the accuracy of measurements. It turns out that the presence of current through the voltmeter was significant only when assessing low frequencies ($v \leq 0,5$ Hz).

On July 13, all participants and organizers of the Olympiad moved to the capital of Czechoslovakia - Prague. On July 15, in the ancient building of one of the first universities in Europe, Charles University, the ceremonial closing of the Olympiad and the presentation of prizes and certificates took place.

1st prize

7 Olympiad participants received: P. Glaser (GDR), P. Dinev (Bulgaria), J. Matena (Czechoslovakia), P. Meiel (GDR), F. Pardo (France), J. Svoboda (Czechoslovakia), P. Sharinov (SSR).

2nd prize

16 Olympiad participants received awards; Among them are Soviet schoolchildren: A. Ganopolsky, V. Reshetov, K. Tretyachenko and V. Shukin.

III prize

16 participants also received.

Another 12 participants were loaded with certificates.

Special prizes were also awarded at the Olympics. Thus, A. Ganopolsky received a prize for the best solution to the second theoretical problem, and P. Sharinov - as the youngest participant in the Olympiad.

It is pleasant to note that the organizers of the Olympiad did everything to ensure that the Olympiad passed the maximum limit. From the first day of work, relations of mutual trust and goodwill were established between the members of the International Commission and the Organizing Committee, which, without a doubt, helped to correctly assess the solution to the problem and sum up the results of the Olympiad.

Meeting with each other, the team leaders discussed various issues related to holding the Olympics in their countries. This exchange of views is very important; it helps to borrow the best experience and use it in your work.

No less important are the first professional meetings of the Olympiad participants - future engineers and physicists. During such meetings, schoolchildren exchanged souvenirs and served various tasks.

Both the Olympiad participants and team leaders left Prague with a feeling of gratitude to the organizers of the Olympiad, who managed to make the Olympiad a showcase of scientific creativity among schoolchildren.

Without a doubt, the International School Olympids are an important example of cooperation between different countries in the field of public education, helping to develop methods for developing the creative activity of schoolchildren.
