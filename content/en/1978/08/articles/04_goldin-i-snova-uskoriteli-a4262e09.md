And again accelerators

In this article we continue the conversation about accelerators that began in No. 4, 1977. Then we did not have time to talk about giant modern accelerators - synchropons with tight focusing - and about a number of issues that play a vital role in accelerator - and not only in accelerator - physics. Among them, the central place is occupied by the problem of traffic stability. Our article will be devoted to these issues. But before moving on to the main topic, we will briefly recall how the simplest cyclic accelerator, the cyclotron, is designed and works.

Particles accelerated in a cyclotron move in a vacuum chamber shaped like a giant tin can (Fig. 1). The vacuum chamber is enclosed between strips of an electromagnet, which creates an almost uniform vertical magnetic field in it. The trajectories of the particles lie in the horizontal plane and, therefore, are perpendicular to the direction of the magnetic induction vector. Charged particles moving in a magnetic field are subject to the Lorentz force, which is perpendicular to both the magnetic field and the speed. This force turns the trajectories of particles into a ring (if they move at a constant speed).

Particles in a cyclotron are accelerated by an electric field. This field is created by electrodes located inside the vacuum chamber -

$$\frac{q \overrightarrow{v} \overrightarrow{B}}{m} = \frac{v^2}{R}.$$

The period $T$ of revolution of a particle moving along a circle of radius $R$ with a speed $\overrightarrow{v}$ is equal to $2\pi R / |v|$. Thus,

$$T = \frac{2\pi R}{|v|} = \frac{2\pi R}{qR|\overrightarrow{B}|/m} = 2\pi \frac{m}{q|\overrightarrow{B}|}.$$

With increasing energy (phasotrons), the mass of the particle returns in accordance with the formulas of the relative theory. Their circulation period increases. The frequency of the accelerating voltage in cyclotrons is constant, but in phasotrons it pulsates, decreasing as a given batch of particles is accelerated and increasing when the next one is captured.

Transverse source problem

Until now we have considered the motion of particles in a cyclotron as if they could move equal to the frequency of revolution of the accelerated particles, so that the particles pass the gap between the two at the moment when the electric field has the desired magnitude and direction. The frequency of the accelerating voltage in cyclotrons is constant, but in phasotrons it pulsates, decreasing as a given batch of particles is accelerated and increasing when the next one is captured.

In the case of a cyclotron, this means that the particle “flies” out of the horizontal plane or “jumps” from its radius.

It is clear that only such accelerators can work successfully if appropriate measures have been taken to a) ensure the acceleration of particles that from the very beginning do not move along the calculated trajectory, but close to it, b) ensure the acceleration of those particles that, for some reason, move from the horizontal plane from the calculated trajectory in the process of acceleration.

From figure it is clear that such a “concave” field does not eliminate us - if a particle, according to some principles, deviates up or down from the horizontal plane in which its trajectory lies, then it will no longer return to it and will “die” on the walls of the vacuum chamber. The influence of the considered inexpensive magnetic fields on the trajectories of charged particles is similar to the effect of optical lines on the propagation of light rays.

Indeed, a “concave” field acts on a moving particle in the same way as a scattering lens acts on light rays, and a “convex” field acts like a converging lens. This analogy suggests that such magnetic fields are called magnetic lenses.

So, with stable motion, any particle that deviates upward from the average horizontal plane must be acted upon by a force directed downward. With the field shape depicted in figure 2, in this case it is zero in the middle plane and increases as it deviates from it. In formula language, this can be written like this:

$$f = -az.$$

In this formula, $z$ is the distance at which the particle deviated from the mean plane, $a$ is the proportionality coefficient, depends on the magnitude of the field induction. The minus sign shows that the force is always “returning” - it is directed towards the middle plane. The forces that obey such laws are well known to us. In mechanics, this is the elastic force of the rukkina. The outer body makes the motion of the accelerated particle: moving in a circular orbit, it accelerates near the middle plane of the accelerator. We now see how to ensure the stability of movement - this movement must be converted into oscillatory.

How can we prevent particles from reaching the walls of the chamber even with a noticeable increase in the energy of transverse (in our case, vertical) vibrations? Using a mechanical analogy, we can answer this way: you need to make the gun stiffer. Of course, there are no springs in the vacuum chamber of the accelerator. What do you need to remember under the “hard spring”? All other things being equal, by increasing the stiffness of the spring on which the load oscillates, we increase the frequency of oscillations of the load around the equilibrium position (recall that the spring stiffness coefficient $k$, the mass of the load $m$ and the oscillation frequency $\omega$ are related by the relation $\omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}$). This conclusion can be directly applied to the theory of accelerators. The stability of motion is ensured the better, the deviations from the equilibrium position (in our case from the “ideal” orbit) are smaller, the higher the frequency of particle oscillations around the equilibrium orbit! In accelerator technology they usually talk about how many periods of oscillations occur in one period of revolution of a particle.

Let us return once again to the movement of particles in a magnetic field, the plum lines of which are curved as shown in figure 2, $\theta$. It would seem that from an installation point of view also the “convex” field eliminates us completely. However, so far we have only talked about the vertical arrangement of particles. And what will happen to particles that, remaining in the horizontal plane, for some reason “jump off” from their trajectory - deviate along the radius? Will radial installation be ensured?

To better understand the existing issues, let us first consider a simplified problem - radial motion in a uniform magnetic field (which, as we found out, does not provide vertical alignment). Let us also abstract from the acceleration process and consider the movement of particles with constant energy. The trajectories of such parts in a uniform field, as we know, are circles. Let's choose one of them as the “main” one. If the particle from the very beginning does not move along the main circle, but near it, then it describes another circle, as depicted in figure 3. The trajectory of this particle does not go far from the main circle. Radial alignment is thus ensured. During the orbital period, the particle crosses the main circle twice; it is first outside and then inside it (or vice versa). This means that one radial oscillation occurs per revolution.

Now let's see how things will be with a radially installed spring in a “convex” field, the magnitude of the induction decreases towards the periphery. A qualitative answer to this question is not difficult to give. At point $A$ (see Fig. 3) the field will be weaker than before, the trajectory of the deflected particle will bend less and, therefore, approach the main one more slowly. At point $B$, on the contrary, the magnitude of the field induction will be greater than before, the trajectory of the circle will bend and, again, will approach the main one more slowly. Consequently, part of the radial vibrations, and hence the stability of radial movement, decreases.

It turns out that vertical and radial stability are related to each other. And this is not only in our consideration of the “convex” magnetic field. Research into sustainability issues

ultimately led to the following conclusion: when charged particles move in fields with axial symmetry, the numbers $n_z$ and $n_r$ of vertical and radial oscillations around the main trajectory during the period of revolution of the relationship

$$n_z^2 + n_r^2 = 1.$$

This formula is very important in accelerator physics. Its conclusion is complex; however, we can now understand it qualitatively. In a uniform field there are no vertical oscillations - $n_z = 0$, and the number of radial oscillations - $n_r = 1$. By weakening the magnetic field induction to the periphery of the vacuum chamber, we increase the vertical stability - we increase $n_z$, or, as they say, we increase the “focusing rigidity” vertically; At the same time, we reduce the stability along the radius - we reduce $n_r$. What happens if you make $n_z$ or $n_r$ more than ones? Then equation (2) has no solutions. Physically, this means that the movement becomes completely unstable. (If we return to the analogy with a spring, this means that the gravitating spring will be replaced by a repulsive one.)

Thus, we come to the conclusion that the magnetic fields used in cyclic accelerators must be such that the magnitude of the induction decay along the radius and not too fast.

Hard acceleration

After formula (2) was obtained, some despondency set in in the world of accelerator physics. The presence of transverse vibrations is necessary for the stability of particle motion. In addition, care must be taken to ensure that the amplitudes of these oscillations around the equilibrium orbit are smaller. (A large amplitude of oscillations requires a wide vacuum chamber and, therefore, large magnets. Large ones mean expensive to manufacture and operate.) The amplitude of oscillations can be reduced only by becoming “hard springs,” that is, by increasing the frequency. And there is a limitation on the oscillation frequency (2). How, after all, do we build double-barreled accelerators?

A way out of the apparent impasse was found by the American physicists Livingston, Courant and Snyder when they proposed a new system for the arrangement of magnetic fields. This is how accelerators with hard focusing appeared. To obtain the operating principle of such accelerators, let us turn to the design of synchrotrons.

In a synchrotron, the vacuum chamber looks like fields of rams. The chamber is located between strips of magnets, the surface of which has the shape of a flask (Fig. 4). The main trajectory of particles in the synchrotron remains unchanged during the entire acceleration time - it is a circle passing in the middle of the chamber. As the particles accelerate, their energy increases. To “keep” particles at the trajectory transition, an increasingly large magnetic field is needed. Therefore, during the acceleration cycle, the magnetic field on the synchrotron ring track continuously increases. The frequency of the accelerating electric field also increases: as the particles accelerate, they spend less and less time traveling the same path. When the acceleration of a certain batch of particles is normal, the magnetic field decreases, the frequency of the accelerating electric field decreases - the synchrotron is ready to receive a new batch of particles. Starting with the newest acceleration cycle.

The length of the magnetic tracks of large synchrotrons varies in climeters. A ring electromagnet is assembled from hundreds—or even thousands—of individual magnetic blocks. During the acceleration cycle, particles undergo hundreds of thousands, if not millions, of revolutions in a vacuum chamber.

In accelerators with hard acceleration, electromagnets are alternately placed on a magnetic track, creating highly curved magnetic fields. Longitudinal stability; principles of autophasing

figure 11 shows a graph of the voltage in the accelerating gap of the cyclic accelerator. the voltage changes over time according to a sinusoidal law. The voltage amplitude is chosen higher than the voltage value $U_0$, which provides the necessary energy increase (for one period of revolution of the particle). From figure it is clear that a “push” is needed to obtain particles arriving at the gap at times $t_1$ and $t_2$. These particles are called synchronous. We will henceforth call synchronous particles $A$ and $B$. How will the particles move as they approach?

the cutting gap not exactly at moments $t_1$ and $t_2$, but with a slight advance or delay? Will these particles “catch up” with the synchronous ones at the next revolutions? In other words, does the continued motion of particles have the necessary stability?

Let's consider a particle that is slightly ahead of the synchronous one. If we are talking about a particle $A'$ (we will call this the particle that approaches the gap at time $t_1'$), then it comes to the gap when the direction changes from $u_0$. If we are talking about a particle $B'$ (approaching the gap at time $t_2'$), then at the moment of its arrival at the gap the direction is higher than $u_0$. As a result, particle $A'$ will receive insufficient energy, and particle $B'$ will receive excess energy, and hence speed. How will this deficiency or excess energy affect the time of the next arrival of a particle to the scraping gap? If a particle tends to approach (in time) a synchronous particle, then the motion turns out to be stable, and if it moves away, it becomes unstable.

At what point in time will the particle approach the scraping gap next time? As we have already seen, when particles move in a cyclotren, the period of revolution does not depend on the energy of the particles. This conclusion is valid for nonrelativistic particles (whose speed is much less than the speed of light) moving in a uniform magnetic field. None of these conditions are synchronous. The orbital period $T$ depends on both the orbital length $L$ and the particle speed $v: T = L/v$. Particles with different energies have different speeds and different orbital lengths. (We noted above that in a synchronous position, and therefore, the length of the main orbit does not change during acceleration. This happens because during the acceleration cycle the direction of the magnetic field increases. But we are not talking about this now. We are considering particles with slightly different energies at the same moment in time, and therefore at the same magnetic field. Particles with different energies pass through such a field for a full revolution of paths of different lengths.)

It turns out that, depending on the design of the synchronon, particles with higher energy can have a revolution period either greater or less than synchronous particles.

Let, for the purpose of definition, particles with a higher speed return to the gap earlier, and particles with a lower speed return later than synchronous. In this case, the sewing particle $A'$, which received insufficient energy during its first passage through the gap, arrives too early next time and, therefore, approaches (in time) particle $A$. Particle $B'$, which has received excess energy, will arrive even earlier and will “lag” (in time) even further behind particle $B$. This means that the motion of particle $A'$ will be stable, and that of particle $B'$ will be unstable. It is not difficult to arrive at the same result by considering the retarded particles $A''$ and $B''$.

If, due to an increase in the path length, particles with excessive speed spend more time on completing a revolution than synchronous ones, the movement of particles $A$ will turn out to be unstable, and particles $B$ - stable.

Let's form a conclusion. If the amplitude of the direction in the accelerating gap exceeds the value that provides the necessary increase in energy, and if the particle’s orbital time depends on their energy, then there are two “classes” of particles, for one of which the movement is continuously stable.

This statement was expressed independently by two scientists - the Soviet physicist Academician V.I. Veksler and the American physicist McMillan - and is called the principle of astrophysics or physical stability. The stability of all accelerators is based on the use of this principle, except for isochronous ones (accelerators in which the circulation time does not depend on energy).

Conclusion

Anyone who, in addition to this article, has read the already mentioned article from the fourth issue «Kvant» for 1977 and the article on accelerators at oncoming points («Kvant» No. 5, 1978), now has an idea about all types of modern accelerators. It remains to add that accelerator technology is experiencing a new rise. In the process of construction, storage rings with counter electron and positron points with an energy of 15 GeV are found.

In the USA, construction of the first proton accelerator with superconducting magnets (with an energy of 1000 GeV) began. Installations with colliding points for electrons and protons are being designed. The creation of storage rings at collateral points of protons and antiprotons is becoming more and more feasible.

The construction of large accelerators is so expensive that even large countries cannot afford it. The states that are members of the CMEA (Council for Mutual Economic Assistance) participate in the financial support of the accelerator work at the Joint Institute for Nuclear Research in Dubna. Western countries created the European Nuclear Research Center, built on the border of Switzerland and France. This center houses the largest accelerators in Europe—a proton accelerator with an energy of 400 GeV and storage rings with colliding points for protons with an energy of 2×30 GeV.
