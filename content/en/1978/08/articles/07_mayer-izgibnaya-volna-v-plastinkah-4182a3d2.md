Flexural wave in plates

The waves generated in the plates have very interesting properties. This property can be discovered experimentally. But first you need to learn how to excite a wave. This can be conveniently done, for example, using a sounding glass tube.

Take a thin and long glass tube and press it in the middle in the foot of the shativa. Using a linen pad moistened with cologne or alcohol, smoothly move it from the end of the tube to its middle - you will hear a fairly strong sound. It was a standing wave that arose in the tube. At the place of fastening, a displacement node is formed, and at the ends - antinodes (Fig. 1).

The oscillation frequency v and the length λ of the sound wave are related to the length l of the tube:

$$v = \frac{v}{2l}$$

And

$$\lambda = 2l.$$

Here v is the speed of sound propagation in the tube. It is determined primarily by the material of the tube and does not depend on the vibration frequency. Thus, to obtain sound frequencies, tubes of different lengths should be used *). For example, if you want to get a sound twice as high in frequency, the glass tube must be shortened by half.

Now do the following. From dioral 1.5-2 mm thick, cut a disk with a diameter of about 100 mm. Place it horizontally on a layer of foam rubber and sprinkle it evenly with potassium manganese powder. There are several explanations for the result of this experiment. A circular wave propagates across the disk from the end of the tube. Having reached the edge of the disk, it is reflected from it - a reflected wave appears. As a result of the interference of the incident and reflected waves, a standing wave is established in the disk, and the powder is collected at its nodes. The distance between two adjacent nodes, veryindo, is equal to half the wavelength in the disk.

A standing wave in a glass tube is longitudinal. What about on disk? It would seem that a transverse wave should propagate in the disk, since the source—the end of the tube—oscillates perpendicular to the disk. It turns out that this is not the case. Research has shown that the wave in the disk is both longitudinal and transverse (Fig. 3). This wave is called an izbeb wave. For comparison, figure 4 shows a purely transverse wave.

A bending wave differs in many ways from an ordinary (longitudinal or transverse) wave. Thus, the speed $v_n$ of a bending wave does not remain constant when the oscillation frequency changes.

ny. It varies proportionally to the square root of the frequency:

$$v_n \sim \sqrt{v}.$$

Let us note by the way that the phenomenon of the dependence of the speed of wave propagation on frequency is called dissertion.

Since the bending wave length $\lambda_n$, speed $v_n$ and frequency $v$ are related by the relations $v_n = \lambda_n v$ (common for waves of any nature), we can write

$$\lambda_n = \frac{v_n}{v} \sim \frac{1}{\sqrt{v}}.$$

Substituting here the value for frequency $v$ from formula (1), we obtain

$$\lambda_n \sim \frac{1}{\sqrt{v}} = \sqrt{\frac{2l}{v}},$$

or

$$\lambda_n = k\sqrt{l},$$

(3)

where $k$ is a constant proportional coefficient.

Relationship (3) can be verified experimentally. Let us present the results of one of our experiments. We used a dioral disc with a thickness of 1.7 mm and a diameter of 120 mm. Bending waves in the disk were generated by glass tubes 60 and 30 cm long. The inner diameter of the tubes was 0.5 mm, the outer diameter was 4.5 mm.

In the experiment with the first tube (the length of the bending wave turned out to be equal to $\lambda_{n1} = 50,5$ mm, and in the experiment with the second tube - $\lambda_{n2} = 34,5$ mm. Hence

$$\frac{\lambda_{n1}}{\lambda_{n2}} = 1,46.$$

According to formula (3), the wavelength ratio should be equal to the ratio

$$\frac{\sqrt{l_1}}{\sqrt{l_2}} = \sqrt{2}.$$

Since $1,46 \approx \sqrt{2}$, experience, with some degree of accuracy, confirms both the very fact of the existence of bending waves; So relation (3) is true.
