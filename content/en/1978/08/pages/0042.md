F483. What horizontal acceleration must be caused by a wedge with an angle of $\alpha$ (Fig. 5) so that the load lying on it rises upward if the coefficient of friction between the load and the wedge is $\mu$?

Fig. 5.

Obviously, the acceleration $a$ of the wedge is directed to the left (otherwise the load would slide off the wedge rather than rise along it).

Let's consider the forces acting on the load. This is the gravitational force $mg$, the normal reaction force $N$ and the friction force $F_{Тр}$ as well as $|F_{Тр}| = \mu |N|$.

Let us write down the equations of motion of the load in projections on the $X$ and $Y$ axes. In order for the load to rise upward, the acceleration projection of the load onto the $X$ axis must be less than or equal to the corresponding acceleration projection of the wedge. Taking into account Newton's second law, we obtain

$$\frac{\mu |N| + m |g| \sin \alpha}{m} \leq |a| \cos \alpha .$$

The projection of the acceleration of the load onto the $Y$ axis is the same as for the wedge (the load does not come off the wedge):

$$\frac{|N| - m |g| \cos \alpha}{m} = |a| \sin \alpha .$$

Solving (1) and (2) together, we find

$$|\vec{a}| \geq \frac{\sin \alpha + \mu \cos \alpha}{\cos \alpha - \mu \sin \alpha} |\vec{g}|.$$

F484. Inside a dielectric ball of radius $R_0$, uniformly charged with a volume density of $\rho$, we find a grounded metal sphere of radius $r$ (Fig. 6). The dependence of the room and the electrical field of this system depends on the distance to the center of the sphere.

Let us denote the charge of the grounded metal sphere by $q$ (later we will find its value); obviously $q < 0$. This charge outside the sphere (at $R \geq r$) creates a field the same as the exact charge $q$ placed at the center of the sphere: the projection of the charge $\vec{E}$ in a chosen direction from the center $E = \frac{q}{4\pi \varepsilon_0 R^2}$. There is no field inside the sphere (at $R < r$): $E = 0$. The graph of $E$ versus $R$ for the field of a charged sphere is shown on figure 7 blue lines.

The potential $\varphi$ of this field outside the sphere changes according to the law $\varphi = \frac{q}{4\pi \varepsilon_0 R}$, and inside the sphere it is equal to the potential of the surface of the sphere: $\varphi = \frac{q}{4\pi \varepsilon_0 r}$. figure 8 blue lines show a graph of $\varphi$ versus $R$.
