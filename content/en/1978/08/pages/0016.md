Collecting optical lines direct rays to the optical axis in both transverse directions simultaneously, while magnetic lines collect particles in one direction and scatter them in the other. In the infinite sequence of lines (magnetic lines are arranged in a circle), the upper and lower parts of figure 6 are no different from each other - this is the same picture, shifted by “half a step”. What focusing properties does this sequence of collecting and scattering lasers have? At first glance, in such a system neither focusing nor defocusing will occur. In fact, this is not true at all. The sequence of such lyises has noticeable “assembling” properties. Let us demonstrate this using a system composed of two sides of equal optical power, one after the other (Fig. 7). Let a beam parallel to the optical axis fall on this system from the left. The virtual image of this beam is at the focus of the scattering (defocusing) laser. Let us denote by $l$ the distance between the lenses and by $F$ their focal lengths. As can be seen from figure, the minimum image of the beam created by the scattering lens is located at a distance of $F+l$ from the collecting lens and serves as a source for it. Let us find at what distance $d$ from the collecting lens the image of this source is located, that is, at what distance from it is the image of the beam passing through the entire system. According to the lyisa formula

$$\frac{1}{d} + \frac{1}{F+l} = \frac{1}{F},$$

where

$$d = \frac{F(F+l)}{l} > 0.$$

Since $d$ turned out to be greater than zero, our system is focusing.

Let's make two comments. It can be shown that the focusing (or defocusing) properties of a system composed of two “opposite” lines depends on the order of their arrangement. We invite the reader to verify for himself that this is not so. If the first one puts not a scattering, but a collecting lens, then the system as a whole still remains focusing (it is assumed that $F > l$). The second point is this. The degree of focusing in our system depends on the distance between the lines. If you decrease this distance, the focusing becomes weaker and in the limit at $l \rightarrow 0$ disappears altogether. Let's try to understand why this happens. To do this, let's turn to figure 8. This figure depicts rays passing through the collecting lisa. The ray passing through the center of the lisa does not change its direction. The beam passing from the side of the central one deflects the more strongly the farther away from the axis axis they are. This statement is fully applied to scattering lysis. Let's return to figure 7. From the path of the rays it is clear that in a scattering lens the rays pass significantly closer to the optical axis of the system than in a collecting lens. This difference in the path of the rays explains why a system composed of collecting and scattering rays having the same optical power turns out to be focusing as a whole. It is also easy to see that the difference in the distance to the optical axis decreases as the distance between the lenses decreases—that’s why the focus decreases.
