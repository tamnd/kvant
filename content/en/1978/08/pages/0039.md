MS·MD are not equal to another friend. Let us prove that $\overrightarrow{AC} = \overrightarrow{DB}$. Is the opposite statement true?

Let $M$ be an arbitrary point on the plane, $(x; y)$ its coordinates. Then the vectors $\overrightarrow{MA}, \overrightarrow{MB}, \overrightarrow{MC}, \overrightarrow{MD}$ have coordinates $(-x; -y), (b_1-x; b_2-y), (c_1-x; c_2-y), (d_1-x; d_2-y)$ respectively. The equality $\overrightarrow{MA} \cdot \overrightarrow{MB} = \overrightarrow{MC} \cdot \overrightarrow{MD}$ will be written in coordinates as follows:
$$-x (b_1-x) - y (b_2-y) = (c_1-x)(d_1-x) + (c_2-y)(d_2-y),$$
or
$$(c_1 + d_1 - b_1) x + (c_2 + d_2 - b_2) y = c_1 d_1 + c_2 d_2.$$ (*)

The statement “for any point $M$ the scalar products $\overrightarrow{MA} \cdot \overrightarrow{MB}$ and $\overrightarrow{MC} \cdot \overrightarrow{MD}$ are not equal to each other” means that equality (*), considered as an equation with two unknowns $x, y$, has no solutions. This is possible if and only if
$$\begin{cases} c_1 + d_1 - b_1 = 0, \\ c_2 + d_2 - b_2 = 0, \\ c_1 d_1 + c_2 d_2 \neq 0. \end{cases}$$
Rewriting the first two equalities in the form
$$\begin{cases} c_1 = b_1 - d_1, \\ c_2 = b_2 - d_2 \end{cases}$$
and noting that $(c_1; c_2)$ are the coordinates of the vector $\overrightarrow{AC}$, and $(b_1-d_1; b_2-d_2)$ are the coordinates of the vector $\overrightarrow{DB}$, we will see that this system of equalities is equivalent to the equality of the vectors $\overrightarrow{AC}$ and $\overrightarrow{DB}$.

The condition $c_1 d_1 + c_2 d_2 \neq 0$ means that $\overrightarrow{AC} \cdot \overrightarrow{AD} \neq 0$, that is, the vectors $\overrightarrow{AC}$ and $\overrightarrow{AD}$ are not mutually perpendicular.

So, the condition "for any point $M$ the scalar products $\overrightarrow{MA} \cdot \overrightarrow{MB}$ and $\overrightarrow{MC} \cdot \overrightarrow{MD}$ are not equal to each other" is equivalent to the following: "$\overrightarrow{AC} = \overrightarrow{DB}$ and $\overrightarrow{AC} \cdot \overrightarrow{AD} \neq 0$".

If the points $A, B, C, D$ do not lie on the same line, then the equality $\overrightarrow{AC} = \overrightarrow{DB}$ means that the quadrilateral $ACBD$ is a parallelogram, and the inequality $\overrightarrow{AC} \cdot \overrightarrow{AD} \neq 0$ means that this parallelogram is not a straight line.

We will provide various proofs for points a) and b). The idea is based on the following statements: if all the roots of a polynomial are real and different, then all the roots of the derivative of a polynomial are also real and different. Indeed, a polynomial of degree $n$ is a continuous (and differentiable) function on the line. Consequently, at some internal point of the interval, the ends of which are any two adjacent roots of the original polynomial, it takes either the maximum or minimum value (Fig. 4, a). At the extreme point, the derivative of the polynomial vanishes. Thus, in any interval any two adjacent roots of a polynomial have its derivative (Fig. 4, b). Consequently, the derivative of a polynomial of degrees $n$, for which all roots are real and distinct, has exactly $n-1$ real roots, all of which are distinct.
