Let's find the values ​​of the angles $PY_1X_1$ and $QX_1Y_1$. Since $Z\hat{X}_1Y_1 = 60^\circ$ (angle of an equilateral triangle), $Z\hat{X}_1B = X_1\hat{Z}U + X_1\hat{U}Z = 30^\circ + (30^\circ + \gamma)$ (external angle $\Delta ZX_1U$) and $Q\hat{X}_1B \cong Z\hat{X}_1B$ (these angles are symmetrical with respect to straight line BU),

$$Q\hat{X}_1Y_1 = 360^\circ - 60^\circ - (60^\circ + \gamma) - (60^\circ + \gamma) = 180^\circ - 2\gamma.$$

The equality $PY_1X_1 = 180^\circ - 2\gamma$ is proved in exactly the same way.

Thus, $P\hat{Y}_1X_1 = Q\hat{X}_1Y_1$; in addition, $[PY_1] \cong [Y_1Z] \cong [ZX_1] \cong [X_1Q]$ (the first two symmetrical segments with respect to the straight line AU, and the last two with respect to the straight line BU). It follows that the quadrilateral $PY_1X_1Q$ is an equilateral grapetz (with angles $Y_1$ and $X_1$ equal to $180^\circ - 2\gamma$, and therefore with angles P and Q equal to $2\gamma$).

Let us now describe a circle around the trapezoid $PY_1X_1Q$. Since the chords $[PY_1]$, $[Y_1X_1]$ and $[X_1Q]$ of this circle are congruent, the inscribed angles subtended by these chords are congruent; therefore $P\hat{QY}_1 = Y_1\hat{PX}_1 = \hat{X}_1PQ = \gamma$. But $P\hat{CQ} = 3\gamma$;

the circle S described in a posewise manner around $PY_1X_1Q$ will also pass through the point C. And now from the congruence (subtracted by congruent chords) of each $PY_1$, $Y_1X_1$ and $X_1Q$ of the circle S it follows that $P\hat{CY}_1 = Y_1\hat{CX}_1 = X_1\hat{CQ} = \gamma$, which implies the coincidence of $\Delta X_1Y_1Z$ with $\Delta XYZ$.

This completes the proof of Morley's theorem.

The second proof (computational - it is close to the proof of M. Satyanarayan). We will continue to assume that $\hat{A} = 3\alpha$, $\hat{B} = 3\beta$, $\hat{C} = 3\gamma$; We denote the lengths of the sides BC, CA and AB of triangle ABC by a, b and c, and the diameter of the circle circumscribed around it by d (Fig. 6).

Let's try to calculate the length $|XY| = z$ of side $[XY]$ of triangle XYZ. To do this, we will find, first of all, the segments $|CX| = m$ and $|CY| = n$; after this, $|XY|$ can be found by the cosine theorem from $\Delta CXY$. To calculate m and n, we use the theorem of sines. Applying this theorem to $\Delta AYC$ from the sides $|AC| = b$ and $|CY| = n$ and the angles $\hat{A} = \alpha$, $\hat{C} = \gamma$ and $\hat{Y} = 180^\circ - (\alpha + \gamma) = 180^\circ - (60^\circ - \beta) = 120^\circ + \beta$ (here we again use the estimated equality $\alpha + \beta + \gamma = 60^\circ$), we obtain

$$\frac{n}{\sin \alpha} = \frac{b}{\sin (120^\circ + \beta)}$$

or

$$n = b \sin \alpha / \sin (120^\circ + \beta).$$

But by virtue of the same theorem of sines (which we will now apply to the “big” $\Delta ABC$)

$$b / \sin \hat{B} = d$$

or

$$b = d \sin \hat{B} = d \sin 3\beta.$$

Pos that

$$n = d \sin \alpha \sin 3\beta / \sin (120^\circ + \beta) = d \sin \alpha \sin 3\beta / \sin (60^\circ - \beta).$$
