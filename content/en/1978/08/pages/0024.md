It should be almost indistinguishable from zero.

Fermat reasoned something like this. Let $x = x + \varepsilon$ be the new value of $x$. Then $z$, the new value of the function $z$, is expressed by
$$z = (x + \varepsilon)(C - x - \varepsilon) = Cx - x^2 - 2x\varepsilon + C\varepsilon - \varepsilon^2.$$
Let's find the difference between $z$ and $z$.
$$z - z = -2x\varepsilon + C\varepsilon - \varepsilon^2.$$
The ratio of $z - z$ to $\varepsilon$ is the tangent of angle $\varphi$ (Fig. 3):
$$tg\varphi = \frac{z - z}{\varepsilon} = \frac{-2x\varepsilon + C\varepsilon - \varepsilon^2}{\varepsilon} = C - 2x - \varepsilon.$$

If $\varepsilon$ is very small, then the secant $AB$ becomes indistinguishable from the tangent to the curve $z = x(C - x)$ at the point $x$. And tangent $\varphi$ becomes the tangent of the angle of inclination of the tangent to the horizontal. To obtain the tangent of the angle of inclination of this tangent, let us put $\varepsilon = 0$ in the last expression; we will get
$$tg\varphi = C - 2x.$$
It remains to equate this tangent to zero (since at the maximum point the tangent is horizontal - see Fig. 3):
$$C - 2x = 0,$$
and we get the solution: $x = C/2$. Indeed, for a given sum, the product of two numbers becomes maximum when these numbers are equal to each other. Or, in the language of geometry, of all rectangles with a given perimeter, the square has the most area.

Fermat's method gives the correct result when finding the extremum of the function

cyni of one variable. And yet there is a flaw in this method that does not allow Fermat to be considered the creator of differential calculus. With the position of general algebra, beyond which Fermat did not dare to go, one cannot do this: we divided by the quantities $\varepsilon$, and then set this quantity equal to zero. It turns out we divided by zero! To overcome this contradiction, Newton and Leibniz had to take the next step - to create an analysis of the infinitesimal. But this was done only half a century after the events described, and Newton noted the merits of Fermat.

What should have been done? In formula (*) it was necessary to go to the limit at $\varepsilon \rightarrow 0$:

$$tg\varphi = \lim_{\varepsilon \rightarrow 0} \frac{-2x\varepsilon + C\varepsilon - \varepsilon^2}{\varepsilon} = C - 2x.$$

The quantity $\lim_{\varepsilon \rightarrow 0} \frac{z - z}{\varepsilon}$ is called by Leibniz through $z'$. Equating $z'$ to zero in our case, we get the same result: $x = C/2$. Thus, from an algorithmic point of view, Fermat's method is similar to equating an arbitrary one to zero, but this method lacks rigor.

Finding the minima and maxima of a function using differential calculus is used in engineering, economics and management. In this case, it is convenient to use the rules of differentiation of functions, which are now being studied at school. But in a number of cases, Fermat’s method is not inferior to them: try to find the most path from Amber to Benay using an arbitrary one; You will see that in this problem direct differentiation of the function leads to success, not faster.

The story of Pierre Criomont is fictitious: in his extensive correspondence with friends, Fermat does not devote a word to his activities as an adviser to the corner chamber. But this story reflects the true conditions of the judicial procedure of that time - the era of Cardinal Richelieu, in which Fermat served.
