Having traced the successive reflections of the beam from mirrors $3_2$ and $3_3$, we will be convinced that after the third reflection (from $3_3$) the beam goes in the direction specified by the unifying vector $s''$, the components of which are equal to the correspondence $-s_x, -s_y, -s_z$. This means that any ray falling on a system of three mutually perpendicular mirrors is reflected by this system in the direction opposite to the original one.

Such a system of mirrors is formed by three mutually perpendicular gray faces of the corner reflector-pyramid (see Fig. 1, b). On the face of the corner-closing face ABC does not interfere with the action of the reflective faces AOB, BOC and AOC.

We have already talked about the widespread use of corner reflectors. The “oldest” of them, cadafts, can be imagined as a set of pyramidal reflectors, copied in such a way that the edges of the dihedral angles formed by the reflective edges lie in the same plane and are rotated relative to each other at all possible angles. Each such prism reflects “back” rays perpendicular to the edge of the dihedral angle. That's why kadafot and each one is glowing.

In advanced practice, corner reflectors of the type shown in figure 1, in the radar used. For example, buoys and buoys equipped with such reflectors become more visible to radars. Using reflectors attached to weather balloons, wind speed and direction at higher altitudes are determined by radar.

Optical corner reflectors are unique in their application in space research. A reflector placed on an artificial satellite or a spacecraft makes it possible to determine the distances to these objects with very high accuracy. At the same time, as a laser beam.

In 1969, the corner reflector was placed on the Moon by the crew of the American Apollo II spacecraft. A year later, a second corner reflector appeared on the Moon - installed on a Soviet automatic self-propelled vehicle.
