Satellites and the flattening problem

Most of the problems that schoolchildren encounter in competitions, competitions, and the pages of mathematical books and magazines are problems of the so-called Olympid type: their solution, as a rule, is based on one single idea. In the work of a professional mathematician, problems also arise quite often, but often a mathematician has to deal with problems in the solution of which it is necessary to use several different types of reasoning.
In this article we want to introduce the reader to the solution to one such non-Olympic problem.

1. Flattening and oriented object

Sometimes three celestial bodies find themselves on the same straight line. If this is the Sun, Earth and Moon, then astronomers call such a phenomenon an eclipse, and if there are three arbitrary bodies, then a size.

Eclipses, eclipses and other rare arrangements of celestial bodies have long been of interest to astronomers and mathematicians. In this article we will deal with one of these rare phenomena - the occurrence of four celestial bodies in the same plane; Let's call this phenomenon flattening. More precisely, we will solve the problem of flattening in a cosmic system consisting of a planet and its three satellites.

Problem. Around the planet O, in those different circular orbits with center O, they are uniformly aligned with its satellites $P_1, P_2$ and $P_3$ with angular velocities $\omega_1, \omega_2$ and $\omega_3$. Is there necessarily a moment of flattening that occurs sooner or later for any initial positions of the satellites, and at some other angular velocities it is possible to select such initial positions of the satellites that the moment of flattening does not occur at all.

The above question is for three specific values ​​of angular velocities:
a) $\omega_1 = \omega_2 = \omega_3 = 1$;
b) $\omega_1 = \omega_2 = 1, \omega_3 = 2$;
b) $\omega_1 = 2, \omega_2 = 3, \omega_3 = 4$

When starting to study flattening, we must first of all learn to describe the flattening of algebraic ones. Arises

Question 1. How, knowing the coordinates of three lobes in space, determine whether these lobes lie in a plane passing through the origin of coordinates?

Let's look at an easier question first.

Question 2. How, knowing the coordinates of two lobes on a plane, determine whether these lobes lie on a straight line passing through the origin of coordinates?

Answer. Let's denote the data coordinates of points $M_1$ and $M_2$ by $(x_1; y_1)$ and $(x_2; y_2)$. We claim that the straight line $M_1M_2$ model and the molky model pass through the origin when the expression $x_1y_2 - y_1x_2$ vanishes.

Indeed, if $x_1y_2 = y_1x_2$, then for $x_1 \neq 0$ and $x_2 \neq 0$ we can write $\frac{y_1}{x_1} = \frac{y_2}{x_2} = k$, i.e. the points $M_1, M_2$ are located on the straight line $y = kx$.

Conversely: if $M_1$ and $M_2$ lie on the line $y = kx$ and $x_1 \neq 0, x_2 \neq 0$, then $\frac{y_1}{x_1} = \frac{y_2}{x_2} = k$ and $x_1y_2 - y_1x_2 = 0$. (You can figure out the case of $x_1 = x_2 = 0$ and $x_1 = 0, x_2 \neq 0$ yourself.)
