⟦folio 14⟧

Bending a match seems to be justified. Let's try to make a quantitative assessment. To do this, we will use the following model.

Let us conditionally divide the match into two parts by a horizontal plane. During combustion, the temperature of the upper part is higher than that of the lower part, but their lengths are the same. When cooling, the upper, hotter part shrinks (shortens) more than the lower part, since the temperature difference between this part of the match and the surrounding air is greater. Therefore, the length of the upper part of the cooling match will be less than the length of its lower part, and the match will bend with its convexity downwards (towards the colder part when burning). This model fills a bimetallic plate - a plate of two welded metals with different coefficients of thermal expansion. Based on this model, we will estimate the temperature difference between the upper and lower parts of the match in the red belt area.

Let $l_0$ be the length of the match, $d$ its thickness. For a rough estimate, we can assume that as a result of combustion these dimensions remain almost unchanged. However, the length of the top and bottom of the burnt bent match is not the same. The difference in the lengths of the average sections of these parts is equal to

$$l_2 - l_1 = l_0 \alpha \Delta t,$$

(1) where $\alpha$ is the coefficient of thermal expansion, $\Delta t$ is the difference between the maximum temperatures of the upper and lower parts of the match;

$$l_1 = \beta \left( R - \frac{d}{4} \right), \quad l_2 = \beta \left( R + \frac{d}{4} \right),$$

$$l_2 - l_1 = \frac{\beta d}{2} = \frac{l_0}{R} \frac{d}{2}$$

(2) (see Fig. 2). Comparing (1) and (2), we obtain the expression for $R$:

$$R = \frac{d}{2\alpha \Delta t}.$$

(3)

It is not easy to measure the quantities $R$, but through $R$ one can express the easily changeable quantities $h$ (see Fig. 2):

$$h = R (1 - \cos \beta) = R \left( 1 - \cos \frac{l_0}{R} \right).$$

For our estimate, with a fairly good approximation, we can assume that $1 - \cos \beta = \frac{\beta^2}{2}$*, and $h = \frac{l_0^2}{2R}$.

Substituting the value $R$ into this expression (see (3)), we obtain

$$h = \frac{l_0^2 \alpha \Delta t}{d}.$$

From here we find $\Delta t$:

$$\Delta t = \frac{h}{l_0^2} \frac{d}{\alpha}.$$

(4)

So, to estimate the values ​​of $\Delta t$, we need to experimentally find the ratio $h/l_0^2$. The results of the experimental approximation can be considered

$$L/l_0^2 = \text{const} \approx 10^{-2}.$$

Taking $d = 1$ mm, $\alpha = (5 \div 10) \cdot 10^{-5}$ deg$^{-1}$, using formula (4) we find $\Delta t = (100 \div 200)$ (deg).

Measurements of $\Delta t$ taken with a thermocouple for value $t_{\text{верхн}} = (730 \pm 10)^\circ \text{C}, \ t_{\text{низн}} = (650 \pm 10)^\circ \text{C}$ and $\Delta t = (80 \pm 20)^\circ \text{C}$. As you can see, our theoretical estimate is in good agreement with these data. So we can assume that the considered model, which does not take into account the chemical nature of the combustion process, is correct.

Perhaps, watching a burning match, you will suggest some other explanation for the reasons for its bending. Then set out to justify and test your assumptions. And as you conduct your experiments, remember: BE CAREFUL!

*) The error of the formula does not exceed 1% at $\beta < 38^\circ$ and 10% at $\beta \leq 60^\circ$.
