At this point in time, all points of the flame were illuminated by rays emanating in all directions from the satellites. What is the minimum number of sun satellites required? (It is believed that the planet does not rotate; the sun satellite is cut to launch at any greater distance from the planet, however, it illuminates the “cap” on it, which is always less than a hemisphere; see Fig. 7.)

The essential conditions in the formulation of the problem are the condition of the position of the position of the nano simpler and less interesting problem of illuminating a spherical planet with several fixed points by projectors. The smallest number of projectors required in this case is four.

The problem of sun satellites is a purely mathematical one. A similar practical problem - creating a global radio communication system on Earth - is solved by launching three satellites; however, it does not provide communication with the polar regions of the Earth.

Solving the problem of satellites-suns. It is not difficult to say that not one or two satellites will be enough to illuminate the planet - at every moment there will be unlit points (prove this). Let us show that three satellites are not enough.

Indeed, at every moment of time on the planet there will be two diametrically opposite points $A$ and $B$, which are not illuminated at this moment by satellites $P_1$ and $P_2$. Such points, for example, are the ends diametrically opposite! Therefore, any of the points $A$ or $B$ will not be illuminated by any of the satellites at this moment.

Are four sun satellites enough? If it turns out that at some point in time some three

of the four satellites $P_1$, $P_2$, $P_3$, $P_4$ will be in the same plane with the planet $O$, then the calculations of the previous paragraph are fully applied. If such flattening does not occur, we must create an example of the movement we need or prove that it does not exist after all. So, in the case when the flattening of at least one trio of satellites occurs, at the moment of flattening the planet is not completely illuminated. This means that we need to find out whether there are such positive angular velocities $\omega_1$, $\omega_2$, $\omega_3$ and $\omega_4$ at which no flattening of any trio of satellites occurs. The answer to this question is given to us by the theorem formulated above: in any triple chosen from the numbers $\omega_1$, $\omega_2$, $\omega_3$, $\omega_4$, the maximum number must be equal to the sum of the two links. Let, for definition, $0 < \omega_1 \leqslant \omega_2 \leqslant \omega_3 \leqslant \omega_4$. Then for a triple $\omega_1$, $\omega_2$, $\omega_3$ should be $\omega_3 = \omega_1 + \omega_2$, for a triple $\omega_1$, $\omega_2$, $\omega_4$ should be $\omega_4 = \omega_1 + \omega_2$, hence $\omega_3 = \omega_4$. But for a triple, $\omega_1$, $\omega_3$, $\omega_4$ must be $\omega_4 = \omega_3 + \omega_1$, hence $\omega_1 = 0$, which contradicts the condition $\omega_1 > 0$. This proves that the required four positive numbers do not exist. Consequently, it is impossible to construct an example of the motion of four satellites in circular orbits that we need, in which the planet was illuminated at every moment; four satellites are also not enough to achieve the goal set in the task.

However, with satellites rotating in circular orbits, there is already enough space for complete illumination of all planets at every moment of time (we invite readers to construct an appropriate example on their own).

In addition, we invite readers to prove the statement of paragraph 2) of the theorem for a satellite when $\omega_1$, $\omega_2$, $\omega_3$ are integers. Apparently, this can be done quite simply.
