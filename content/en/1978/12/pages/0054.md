The spider's legs (their points) correspond to the brightest stars. Gradical divisions are applied to the outer spine of the spider; a long rectangular plate with two slots (alidade) rotates along it - see figure on the second page of the cover.

Let's see how the coordinates of stars were measured using an astrolabe - their angular height (above the horizon) and azimuth (angular deviation from North to West or East). Let us consider the Earth as the center O of the celestial sphere, on which the luminary C (Fig. 6) that interests us is located. On the celestial sphere are marked the coordinates of the line - almucantarama (concentric circles with centers on the OZ ray) and the great circles of the sphere perpendicular to them - azimuth lines. With a stereographic projection on the equatorial plane *), this grid just turns into a grid similar to a spider’s web; This “web”, according to the second property, consists of circles and circles.

*) Astronomers usually use just this projection. It differs from the above-described readiness with a factor of 1/2.

To find the coordinates of a certain luminary, say Sirius, the astrolabe was hung on the thumb, and the ide was turned so that the observer's eye, Sirius and both answers appeared on the same straight line. Then the celestial arrow shows the angular altitude of the luminary on a graduated scale. To find out the altitude, they found on which almucantar the luminary is located (Fig. 7). After measuring the height, the spider turned along the web until the tip of the leg with the inscription “Sirius” was on the found almucantara. At the same time, the tip pointed to a certain azimuth line on the web: this azimuth was the azimuth of Sirius. Further, since hour divisions are marked on the azimuth lines, it was possible to find out the time (the moment of measurement). Moreover, the user of other spider legs could, without additional measurements, read the coordinates of all other stars marked on the web.
