Since $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$ are integers, the diameter $D(t)$ rotates, although unevenly, but periodically: $D(t + 2\pi) = D(t)$ (like $P_1(t + 2\pi) = P_1(t), P_2(t + 2\pi) = P_2(t)$). But also $P_3(t + 2\pi) = P_3(t)$, so we can limit ourselves to considering the movement during time $2\pi$. Thus, the diameter $D(t)$ has an average angular velocity of rotation equal to the average angular velocity of its rotation during time $2\pi$; let's denote it by $\omega$.

The question is: how is the speed $\omega$ related to $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$?

Therefore, if $\omega_3 > \omega_1 + \omega_2$, then the point $P_3(t)$, rotating on average faster than the diameter $D(t)$, will at some moments overtake its ends $D_1(t)$ and $D_2(t)$. These moments of overtaking are moments of flattening. Similarly, there are moments of flattening in the case $\omega_3 < \omega_1 + \omega_2$.

Unfortunately, we do not know how to prove statement 2 purely geometrically. Therefore, the conclusion that we drew from it should be considered by the reader as legal, but not proven. This conclusion shows that if we are looking for motion without flattening, then it is worth considering only the case $\omega_3 = \omega_1 + \omega_2$.

In our case, the average angular velocity $\omega$ of rotation of diameter $D(t)$ is equal to 2 and coincides with the angular velocity of rotation of the satellite $P_3$. Let us launch satellite $P_3$ so that at $t = 0$ the phase shift between $P_3(0)$ and $D_1(0)$ is equal to $\frac{\pi}{2}$ (Fig. 3). If the diameter $D(t)$ rotated uniformly, then this shift was unchanged. If the rotation $D(t)$ is uneven, but differs little from uniformly, then it is natural to expect that the phase shift between $P_3(t)$ and $D(t)$ will periodically fluctuate around the value $\frac{\pi}{2}$ and therefore will not become equal to zero.

And now, without formally relying on previous reasoning, we give an example of three satellites $P_1, P_2$ and $P_3$, rotating around the planet uniformly with angular velocities $\omega_1 = 1, \omega_2 = 1, \omega_3 = 2$ and without flattening.

Consider a sphere $S$ of uniform radius. Let the planet be at the center $O$ of this sphere, and the satellites $P_i$ rotate along its great circles $\gamma_i$. Let $A \in \gamma_1 \cap \gamma_2 \cap \gamma_3$. Let us place at time $t = 0$ satellite $P_1$ at point $A$, satellite $P_2$ at point $B$ of orbit $\gamma_2$ such that $\widehat{AOB} = \frac{\pi}{2}$, satellite $P_3$ at point $C$ of orbit $\gamma_3$ such that $\widehat{AOC} = \frac{\pi}{2}$ (Fig. 4).
