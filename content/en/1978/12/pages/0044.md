Static elements of deformable bodies

When solving problems on the statics of solid bodies, it is usually assumed that the bodies are absolutely solid (not deformable). In fact, all bodies existing in nature are deformed under the influence of forces applied to them - they change their size and sometimes their shape. It turns out that non-tortic positions of the stats of absolutely rigid bodies cannot be applied to deformable bodies.

For example, in the case of an absolutely rigid body, any force that does not change the equilibrium state of the body can be re-axialized along the line of its action. In the statics of a deformable body, this is unacceptable. Indeed, if the force $\vec{F}$ is applied at point $A$ (Fig. 1, ​​a), the firmament grows along the entire length $l$, and when it moves to point $B$ - only along the length $l_1$ (Fig. 1, b). Thus, although the balance of the entire rod is maintained, the elongation and direction of the state of the rod during re-ossification of the dream $\vec{F}$ along the line of its action change.

Another example. figure 2 shows a rod fixed at one end. Two forces, $\vec{F}$ and $-\vec{F}$, are applied to the other end, equal in absolute magnitude, opposite in direction and lying in the same plane (they are usually called a pair of forces). In the statics of an absolutely rigid body, a pair of forces that does not change the state of equilibrium of the body can be re-axed along the line of its action. In the statics of a deformable body, this is unacceptable. Indeed, if the force $\vec{F}$ is applied at point $A$ (Fig. 1, a), the solid grows along the entire length $l_1$.

In addition to tensile and torsional deformations, other types of deformations are also encountered in practice. Let us limit ourselves to considering axial tensile (or rolling) deformation.
