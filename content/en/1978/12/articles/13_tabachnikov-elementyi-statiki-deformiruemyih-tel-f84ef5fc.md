Static elements of deformable bodies

When solving problems on the statics of solid bodies, it is usually assumed that the bodies are absolutely solid (not deformable). In fact, all bodies existing in nature are deformed under the influence of forces applied to them - they change their size and sometimes their shape. It turns out that non-tortic positions of the stats of absolutely rigid bodies cannot be applied to deformable bodies.

For example, in the case of an absolutely rigid body, any force that does not change the equilibrium state of the body can be re-axialized along the line of its action. In the statics of a deformable body, this is unacceptable. Indeed, if the force $\vec{F}$ is applied at point $A$ (Fig. 1, ​​a), the firmament grows along the entire length $l$, and when it moves to point $B$ - only along the length $l_1$ (Fig. 1, b). Thus, although the balance of the entire rod is maintained, the elongation and direction of the state of the rod during re-ossification of the dream $\vec{F}$ along the line of its action change.

Another example. figure 2 shows a rod fixed at one end. Two forces, $\vec{F}$ and $-\vec{F}$, are applied to the other end, equal in absolute magnitude, opposite in direction and lying in the same plane (they are usually called a pair of forces). In the statics of an absolutely rigid body, a pair of forces that does not change the state of equilibrium of the body can be re-axed along the line of its action. In the statics of a deformable body, this is unacceptable. Indeed, if the force $\vec{F}$ is applied at point $A$ (Fig. 1, a), the solid grows along the entire length $l_1$.

In addition to tensile and torsional deformations, other types of deformations are also encountered in practice. Let us limit ourselves to considering axial tensile (or rolling) deformation.

Problem 3. A load of mass $m = 2$ t supporting two identical round steel rods, hingedly connected at node $A$ and making an angle $\alpha = 60^\circ$ with the vertical (Fig. 5). Determine the cross-sectional diameter of each rod if we allow stress for steel $\sigma_{\text{доп}} = 160\text{MH/m}^2$.

Consider node $A$. Three forces act on it: the weight of the load $\vec{P}$ ($|\vec{P}| = m|\vec{g}|$) and the reaction forces of the rods $\vec{R}_1$ and $\vec{R}_2$. Let us write down the conditions of the node $A$ in projections on the coordinate axes $X$ and $Y$:
$$-|\vec{R}_1| \sin \alpha + |\vec{R}_2| \sin \alpha = 0,$$
$$|\vec{R}_1| \cos \alpha + |\vec{R}_2| \cos \alpha - m|\vec{g}| = 0.$$
From here
$$|\vec{R}_1| = |\vec{R}_2| = |\vec{R}| = \frac{m|\vec{g}|}{2\cos \alpha}.$$

In conclusion, let us consider the case when statics problems cannot be solved at all if the bodies included in the systems are considered to be absolutely solid. See figure 6 for examples of three such systems. In the case of a rod fixed at both ends (Fig. 6, a), to determine two unknown reaction forces $\vec{R}_A$ and $\vec{R}_B$, only one condition can be written (in projections onto the $X$ axis).

In a system consisting of three rods (Fig. 6, b), to determine the three reaction forces ($\vec{R}_1, \vec{R}_2$ and $\vec{R}_3$), only two equilibrium conditions can be written (in projections on the axes $X$ and $Y$). For the system shown in figure 6, b, to determine three unknown forces ($\vec{R}_1, \vec{R}_2$ and $\vec{R}_3$), only two equilibrium conditions can also be written (the projections of all forces on the vertical axis being equal to zero and the moments of all forces being equal to zero relative to, for example, the point $A$).

Such systems are usually called statically indeterminate systems. How can we find all the unknown reaction forces in these cases? It turns out that it is possible to construct additional equations based on the condition of compatibility of deformations.

Let us show how such problems are solved.

Problem 4. In the urine $C$, located on the axis of a cylindrical rod fixed at both ends (Fig. 6, a), to determine two unknown reaction forces $\vec{R}_A$ and $\vec{R}_B$.

Let us write the equilibrium equation of the rod in projections onto the $X$ axis:

$$R_A + R_B + F = 0.$$

To determine two unknowns ($R_A$ and $R_B$), one equation is not enough. Let's create another equation - the equation of compatibility of deformations (displacements).

To do this, we mentally free the rod from its fastening, for example, in the lower section $A$, and replace the action of fastening with an external force $\vec{R}_A$. Let us denote by $\Delta l_1$ the elongation of the rod (displacement of the section $A$)

under the action of force $\vec{F}$, and through $\Delta l_2$ - elongation of the rod (again, movement of the section $A$) under the action of force $\vec{R}_A$. In reality, the rod does not lengthen (section $A$ does not move). Consequently, the equation for the compatibility of deformations (displacements) can be written in the form: $\Delta l_1 + \Delta l_2 = 0$.

We find the values ​​of $\Delta l_1$ and $\Delta l_2$ from Hooke’s law (for more details, see Problem 2), taking into account that $N_1 = -F = |\vec{F}|$ and $N_2 = -R_A$:

$$\Delta l_1 = \frac{|\vec{F}|b}{ES} \quad \text{и} \quad \Delta l_2 = -\frac{R_A(a+b)}{ES}.$$

Considering that $a+b=l$, we get:

$$\frac{|\vec{F}|b}{ES} - \frac{R_A l}{ES} = 0,$$

where

$$R_A = |\vec{F}| \frac{b}{l}.$$

From the equilibrium condition of the rod we find $R_B$:

$$R_B = |\vec{F}| - R_A = |\vec{F}| \frac{a}{l}$$

The projections $R_A$ and $R_B$ are positive; This means that the actual directions of the reaction forces coincide with those accepted at figure 6, $a$.
