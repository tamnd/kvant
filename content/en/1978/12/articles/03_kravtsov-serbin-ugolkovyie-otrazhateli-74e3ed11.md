Corner reflectors are a device that is used to reflect radar or optical beams in the opposite direction to the original one. Until recently, optical corner reflectors were used only as modest road warning devices - on bicycles, automatic vehicles, trains, and road signs. They were called reflectors *). Currently, corner reflectors are widely used: they are used in navigational radar, meteorology, and space research.

How does a corner reflector “work”?

Corner reflectors. This can be a triangular prism, the two side faces of which are mutually perpendicular (Fig. 1, a). These faces (ОAA'О' and OBB'O') are coated on the inside with a thin layer of materials that reflect light well. The corner reflector can be a tetrahedral paramid (Fig. 1, b), three mutually perpendicular faces of which (AOB, BOC and AOC) are silver-plated. Radar corner reflectors are three mutually perpendicular flat plates (Fig. 1, ​​c), made of materials that reflect radio waves well.

As you can see, a corner reflector can be schematically represented as a system of mutually perpendicular flat mirrors. To better understand the principle of operation of a reflector, consider how the path of light rays of a system of two such mirrors ($3_1$ and $3_2$ to figure 2) changes.

Ray S, incident on mirrors $3_1$ at an angle $\phi_1$, is reflected from it (at point A), hits mirrors $3_2$ at an angle $\phi_2$ and is reflected from it (at point B). It is easy to see that as a result of reflections, the beam “turns” by an angle $2\phi_1 + 2\phi_2 = 180^\circ$, that is, the reflected beam is parallel to the incident beam and is directed in the opposite direction.

The role of two such mirrors is performed in the reflector-prism (Fig. 1, a) faces OAA'O' and OBB'O'. A ray of light incident on the “hypotenuse” face BAA'B', after reflection in the prism, comes out in the opposite direction. Note that filling the ray on the hypotenuse face has no effect.

However, a reflector consisting of two mutually perpendicular mirrors “reverses” only those rays that lie in a plane perpendicular to the edge of the dihedral angle formed by the mirrors. A reflector consisting of three mutually perpendicular mirrors does not have this drawback. Let us show that any ray that hits such a reflector and experiences sequential reflection from all three mirrors will change its direction to the exact opposite.

To do this, firstly, by distance, how the unit vector that determines the direction of the ray changes when the ray is reflected from a flat mirror. At figure 4 is a mirror, $S$ is a ray incident on the mirror at an angle $\varphi$, $s$ is a unit vector defining the direction of the incident ray, $n$ is a unit vector perpendicular to the mirror plane, $s'$ is a unit vector in the direction of the ray $S$. Let us represent it as the sum of three components $s_x, s_y$ and $s_z$, parallel to the axes $X$, $Y$ and $Z$, respectively. After the first reflection—reflection from the mirror $3_1$—the ray travels in the direction determined by the single vector $s'$. We have already talked about the widespread use of corner reflectors. The “oldest” of them, cadafts, can be imagined as a set of pyramidal reflectors, copied in such a way that the edges of the dihedral angles formed by the reflective edges lie in the same plane and are rotated relative to each other at all possible angles. Each such prism reflects “back” rays perpendicular to the edge of the dihedral angle. That's why kadafot and each one is glowing.

In advanced practice, corner reflectors of the type shown in figure 1, in the radar used. For example, buoys and buoys equipped with such reflectors become more visible to radars. Using reflectors attached to weather balloons, wind speed and direction at higher altitudes are determined by radar.

Optical corner reflectors are unique in their application in space research. A reflector placed on an artificial satellite or a spacecraft makes it possible to determine the distances to these objects with very high accuracy. At the same time, as a laser beam.

In 1969, the corner reflector was placed on the Moon by the crew of the American Apollo II spacecraft. A year later, a second corner reflector appeared on the Moon - installed on a Soviet automatic self-propelled vehicle.
