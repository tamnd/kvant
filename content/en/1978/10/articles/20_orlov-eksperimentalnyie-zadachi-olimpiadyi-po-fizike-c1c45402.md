Experimental problems of the Olympiad in Physics

On April 16, in the laboratories of the Faculty of Physics of the Turkmen State University, an experimental round was held, the final stage of the XII All-Union Olympiad for Schoolchildren in Physics. Each participant was asked to solve two experimental problems. Solutions and analyzes of the implementation of these tasks by Olympiad students are given below.

8th grade

Task 1. Determination of the liquid density ratio.

Equipment: lever-ruler, two weights, two vessels with various liquids, a rod with a coupling and a foot. (An aluminium cube weighing 100 g and a steel weight weighing 100 g were used as weights; colored water and a coli solution were used as liquid.)

Solution. The method for comparing liquid densities is based on the dependence of the absolute values ​​$F_{A1}$ and $F_{A2}$, Archimedean forces on the densities $\rho_1$ and $\rho_2$ of the liquid in which a load of volume $V$ is immersed:

$$F_{A1} = \rho_1 V g, \quad F_{A2} = \rho_2 V g.$$

It follows that

$$\frac{\rho_1}{\rho_2} = \frac{F_{A1}}{F_{A2}}.$$

By balancing the weights on the lever, we establish that the masses of the weights are the same.

Having lowered the alimine cube into the first liquid (Fig. 1, a), we balance the weights on the lever:

$$(mg - F_{A1}) l = mgl_1.$$ (1)

Having replaced the vessel with liquid without changing arm $l$, we again balance the loads (Fig. 1, b):

$$(mg - F_{A2}) l = mgl_2.$$ (2)

From conditions (1) and (2) we find

$$\frac{F_{A1}}{F_{A2}} = \frac{l - l_1}{l - l_2} \Rightarrow \frac{\rho_1}{\rho_2} = \frac{l - l_1}{l - l_2}.$$

The relative measurement error $\rho_1/\rho_2$ is equal to

$$\varepsilon_{\rho_1/\rho_2} = \varepsilon_{l - l_1} + \varepsilon_{l - l_2} = \frac{h_l + h_{l_1}}{l - l_1} \times 100\% + \frac{h_l + h_{l_2}}{l - l_2} \times 100\%,$$ (3)

where $h_l, h_{l_1}$ and $h_{l_2}$ are the absolute errors in measuring the length of the arms.

What does the value of $\varepsilon_{\rho_1/\rho_2}$ depend on? The values ​​of $h_l, h_{l_1}$ and $h_{l_2}$ are determined by the accuracy of the measuring tool (ruler). From expression (3) it is clear that the smaller the difference in lengths $l$ and $l_1, l$ and $l_2$, the greater the relative measurement error. It follows that in order to increase the accuracy of measurements, it is the aluminium cube that should be lowered into vessels with liquids, and not a steel weight, since due to the large volume of the cube, a large Archimedean force will act on it, which, in turn, will lead to a greater difference in the lengths $l, l_1$ and $l_2$. For the same purpose, the shoulder length $l$ should be chosen as long as possible. It did not make sense to produce several wholesale ones with different lever arms, lower both weights into the liquid alternately, and then reduce the result, since this would completely reduce the accuracy of the measurement. Some students, when transferring a load from one liquid to another, measure a shoulder $l$ force $mg + F_A$. This led to a more complex calculation formula and additional measurement errors, increasing the likelihood of calculation errors.

Task 2. Experimentally check the fulfillment of the law of conservation of momentum when the movement of a chain of balls collides with a stationary one installed on a horizontal section of the tray. Explain your results.

Equipment: a tent with a coupling and a foot, an arched tray, a set of two plastic and two steel balls with a given mass, leaves of white and carbon paper, a scale ruler.

Solution and e. The simplest technique for performing the experiment is as follows. Using sheets of white and carbon paper, the place where the ball falls when it rolls down a free tray is recorded and the flight range S of the ball is measured (Fig. 2, a). The initial speed of the ball at the start of free flight is equal to $v=S/t$, where $t$ is the time the ball falls.

Having placed the second ball on the horizontal section of the tray, we measure the flight ranges $S_1$ and $S_2$ of both balls after they collide (Fig. 2, b). The speeds of the balls after the collision are $v_1=S_1/t$ and $v_2=S_2/t$. Since the time of falling of the balls in all experiments is the same, to check the law of conservation of momentum it is enough to check the compliance of the measurements

$$m_1S = m_1S_1 + m_2S_2,$$

where $m_1$ and $m_2$ are the masses of the steel balls.

In order to draw a conclusion about the feasibility or impossibility of the law of conservation of momentum in this experiment, the following are the errors in distance measurements ($S, S_1, S_2$) with the relative deviation of the left and right sides of the expression (*).

Relative error of flight range measurement $\varepsilon_s = (h_s/S) 100\%$ ($h_s$ - absolute error). Therefore, to reduce the value of $\varepsilon_s$, it is necessary to select the experimental conditions so that the value of $S$ is as large as possible. For this purpose, the tray should be extended to the entire height of the frame, and the ball should be released from the top point of the tray.

For plastic balls removed from the tray, the deviation of the left and right expressions (*) turns out to be significantly greater than the measurement errors - the momentum of the balls increased after impact! The reason for the non-fulfillment of the law of conservation of momentum in this case is that the ball rotates when rolling off the tray. When plastic balls of equal mass collide, the moving ball stops at the moment of impact, completely transferring its momentum to the second ball. However, due to the energy of rotational motion accumulated during rolling, the first ball again begins to move along the horizontal section of the tray. The consequence of this right, part (*) turns out to be greater than would be required by the law of conservation of momentum.

For steel balls, the law of conservation of momentum is satisfied within the limits of measurement errors, since the main balls slide off the tray.

Task 2. Determination of the optical design of the “black box” and possible parameters of the optical elements contained in it.

Equipment: box with six holes, scale ruler, 4 pins, lifting table, paper.

Solution. The “black box” contains a convex-convex mirror with radii of curvature $R_1 = R_2 = 21$ cm (Fig. 6).

You can easily verify that there is a mirror in the box by looking into holes 1, 2, 3, first with holes 4, 5, 6 open, and then with them closed, without detecting any changes.

The standard solution is to construct the path of incident and reflected rays from the mirror. To do this, using pins, the directions in which two other holes were visible through one hole were offset. The intersection of the corresponding directions (blue lines on figure 7) gives the position of the three mirror points on one side and the three mirror points on the other. The bissertrices of the angles between the incident and reflected rays give the position of the perpendiculars to the surface of the mirror, the intersection of the perpendiculars is the center of curvature. Measuring the distance from the center of curvature to the surface of the mirror gives the desired value for the radius of the mirror.

The described method of performing the work gives greater accuracy, the smaller the radius of curvature of the mirror surface. In this case, the radius of curvature was quite large, and therefore it was necessary to accurately construct the path of the rays.

Only 13 tenth-graders out of 49 successfully completed this work. The main mistake, which did not allow us to obtain the optical design of the “black box,” was a carelessly made drawing with the construction of the ray path.
