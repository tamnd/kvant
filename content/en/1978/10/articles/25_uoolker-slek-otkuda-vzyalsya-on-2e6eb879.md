Where did “he” come from?

Even casual witnesses of a professional conversation between physicists can notice that the conversation is replete with words ending in “he”: photon, phonon, electron, magnon, etc.

What is the origin of these words? Who was their inventor? Who introduced them into use?

The term "electron" (to denote a carrier of electric charge) appeared in 1891. This word comes from the Greek plechtrov, which means “inyatar”.

In 1920, the word “proton” appeared, which originated from Lrstof, who introduced “first”. The transition from "σ" to "v" at the end of the word was a product of the desire to harmonize the new term with the word "electron".

In 1921, the name “neutron” arose: a combination of the word “neutral” with an ending in the style of electron and proton.

In the 1930s, there was a slight departure from tradition in the naming of particles—the terms “mesotron” and “positron” appeared.

The first name of physics was later abandoned, but was retained. To designate these words, the sound-corresponding “throne” was separated from “electron”; the Greek mtsodor (“middle”) and the Latin positivus (“positive”) were used as roots.

The term "mesotron" was soon replaced by the term "meson", and "it" is now the traditional suffix serving the entire spectrum of particles in physics.

Let us trace the history of the names of some particles that appear in physics.

Electron

The name of the Irish physicist Georg Johnston Stoney is not often mentioned in modern works on physics. However, back in 1874, he postulated that there must be “certain amounts of electricity,” the magnitude of which he determined from experiments on electric creep. In 1891, Stoney proposed a name for these particles: "The author called attention to the existence of certain quantities of electricity in 1874... These charges, which are conveniently called electrons, cannot be separated from the atom, but appear when the atoms are chemically combined."

Stoney had no way of showing that electrons actually existed; rather, he demonstrated that they must exist. The existence of the electron as a particle was proven by Thomson in 1897.

Neutron and proton

The neutron was discovered in 1932 by J. Chadwick.

Chadwick points out that the name of his discovery of the particle belongs to Rutherford, and refers to a lecture Rutherford gave in 1920 at the Royal Society in London. In this lecture, Rutherford presents a certain neutral particle consisting of a bonded hydrogen nucleus and an electron. However, he does not use the name "neutron". None of Rutherford's works from 1920-1921 contain this word. We think that the name "neutron" was once suggested by Rutherford in the laboratory, and the word stuck, but it first appeared in the literature in 1921.

Rutherford also suggested the word "proton". This happened at a meeting of the British Association in 1920. The report of this meeting reads: "It is shown that the elements may be considered states of hydrogen nuclei, or 'protons,' as Sir Ernest Rutherford called them."

Photon

This word is used to describe the corpuscular properties of the electromagnetic field. Most physicists believe that Einstein was the author of this concept. Many books on modern physics refer to Einstein's landmark 1905 paper on the photoelectric effect, in which he wrote: "According to the assumption made here, the energy of a beam of light emerging from a certain point is not distributed continuously throughout all-returning space, but is the sum of a finite number of weekly energy quanta localized in space, absorbed or generated only as a whole."

However, Einstein did not use the term "photon" in his paper. It was introduced into city physics until later, in 1926.

But who exactly introduced this term?

The December 18, 1926 issue of Nature magazine contains an article by Lewis, “The Conservation of Photons,” in which the word is introduced for the first time: “I take the liberty of introducing for this hypothetical new atoma, which is not light, but plays an essential part in any process of radiation, the name “photon.”

Phonon

The quantum of energy of sound waves in a crystal, the idea of ​​which was introduced by the Soviet physicist I.E. Tump, received its name by direct analogy with the photon.

Who gave it this name?

It was proposed by the Soviet physicist Ya. I. Frenkel in the book “Wave Mechanics” (1932): “Just as in the case of light and electrons, we can compare sound waves with a certain kind of particles, which we will call “phonons,” and replace the study of the tell vibrations that form these waves with the study of the corresponding phonons.”

From the editor

The ending “he” in the names of elementary particles has already become a tradition. After "mesons", "hyperons" and other particles were discovered recently, this tradition was transferred to the name not of the particles themselves, but of special states of particles interacting with the study or other particles. This is how “exponential”, “polaron”, “magnon”, “plazon”, “vacasnon”, etc. appeared.

We will not touch on these “parts” here, otherwise we would have to talk about all of modern physics, which is impossible.
