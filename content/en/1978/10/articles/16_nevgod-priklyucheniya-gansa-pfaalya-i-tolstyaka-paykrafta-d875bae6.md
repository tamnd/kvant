⟦folio 58⟧

Edgar Poe has a relatively unimportant fantasy story, “The Extraordinary Adventure of a Certain Hans Pfaal.”
The hero of this story made an amazing discovery - he obtained an extraordinary gas, the density of which is 37.4 times less than the density of hydrogen. A balloon filled with such gas has the wrong lifting force. With his help, Hans Pfaal even managed to get to the Moon.

We will not prove that a gas lighter than hydrogen does not exist in nature - this is what was promised. (Can you explain why this is impossible?) But the problem is not without interest: if such a gas did exist, how many times would it increase the lifting force of a balloon (compared to a balloon filled with hydrogen)?

Despite the simplicity of the task, many people do not immediately find the correct answer. This is where the “logical” choice comes in: since gas is 37.4 times lighter than hydrogen, its lifting force is the same amount of times greater. Perhaps Edgar Allan Poe expanded on this last conclusion when he wrote his story. However, it is equally believed that he himself became the victim of an erroneous reasoning, outwardly solid.

However, the simplest calculation shows that the gain in lift was so insignificant that it could not be taken into account at all. Let's check it out. To do this, let's find the lifting force of a balloon filled with hydrogen and a balloon filled with Hans Pfaal's gas.

Let the volume of air in the ball be 1 m³. The density of air is 0.00129 g/cm³, hydrogen is 0.00009 g/cm³, and Hans Pfaal gas is 0.0000024 g/cm³. Let us recall that the lifting force of the air of a ball is the difference between the buoyant force, equal to the gravitational force of the air displaced by the ball, and the gravitational force of the gas inside the ball (we will assume that the shell of the ball is weightless). Then the lifting force of a balloon filled with hydrogen is approximately 12 N,

Thus, the gain in lifting force is only about 0.9 N! The result is real, that, obviously, Hans Pfaal (or Edgar Allan Poe) did not bother to invent a miraculous ultra-light gas, breaking, moreover, the laws of nature. (To be fair, we note that at the time of Edgar Poe, the periodic table had not yet been compiled.) The whole problem is the small gravity of hydrogen. Even if a gas were possible, even thousands of times lighter than hydrogen, it would still not significantly increase the lifting power of a balloon. The limit of such an increase is those 0.9 N, which constitute the gravitational force of hydrogen itself.

Let us now recall the popular science fiction story by G. Wallace “The Truth About Pycraft.” The funny fat man Pycraft, passionately wanting to get rid of excess weight, drank a tannic Indian potion - and completely lost weight, in the most literal sense! For days on end, he flew under the ceiling of his own office, without going outside, so as not to fly off into the heights, like a balloon. This continued until Pycraft advised him to order a special costom with lead gaskets. In such a suit, in heavy lead shoes and with a briefcase full of lead in his hands, he finally gained the airiness to walk the streets again, like other people.

This begs the question: how much lead was needed for Pycraft to be able to walk safely on Earth? Let's do some calculations. Suppose Pycraft alone weighed 1000 N (his mass was 100 kg), then the volume of his body can be considered equal to 0.1 m³. Deprived of weight, Pycraft seemed to turn into a kind of balloon of the same volume. Its “lifting” force was only about 1.3 N!

And here we see how the picture of the misadventures of the weightless Pycraft, painted by the writer’s wild imagination, fades! Even in his everyday clothes, Pycraft did not have to float under the ceiling of his office, but could, although not very steadily, sit in a cross at his desk and even carefully walk around the room (avoiding, however, sudden movements). And he wouldn’t need a lead suit and lead boots at all - in general clothes, and even taking a heavy briefcase in his hands, he could walk along the streets (of course, being careful of the strong wind!), without fear of flying into the skies.

As we have seen, the “volatility” of Pycraft and the problems it caused are greatly exaggerated by the author. Make sure, of course, that Wells doesn't notice this when he wrote his story. Most likely, he deliberately ignored the data obtained during the calculation in order to present the comic misadventures of poor Pycraft in brighter and more expressive colors. At the same time, the author apparently was confident that readers, captivated by the original fiction, would never notice the exaggerations he made.
