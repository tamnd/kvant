Where did “he” come from?

Even casual witnesses of a professional conversation between physicists can notice that the conversation is replete with words ending in “he”: photon, phonon, electron, magnon, etc.

What is the origin of these words? Who was their inventor? Who introduced them into use?

The term "electron" (to denote a carrier of electric charge) appeared in 1891. This word comes from the Greek plechtrov, which means “inyatar”.

In 1920, the word “proton” appeared, which originated from Lrstof, who introduced “first”. The transition from "σ" to "v" at the end of the word was a product of the desire to harmonize the new term with the word "electron".

In 1921, the name “neutron” arose: a combination of the word “neutral” with an ending in the style of electron and proton.

In the 1930s, there was a slight departure from tradition in the naming of particles—the terms “mesotron” and “positron” appeared.

The term "mesotron" was soon replaced by the term "meson", and "it" is now the traditional suffix serving the entire spectrum of particles in physics.

Let us trace the history of the names of some particles that appear in physics.

Electron

The name of the Irish physicist Georg Johnston Stoney is not often mentioned in modern works on physics. However, back in 1874, he postulated that there must be “certain amounts of electricity,” the magnitude of which he determined from experiments on electric creep. In 1891, Stoney proposed a name for these particles: "The author called attention to the existence of certain quantities of electricity in 1874... These charges, which are conveniently called electrons, cannot be separated from the atom, but appear when the atoms are chemically combined."

Stoney had no way of showing that electrons actually existed; rather, he demonstrated that they must exist. The existence of the electron as a particle was proven by Thomson in 1897.
