The tire comes into contact with the road at the so-called flat contact. The forces acting from the road, applied to the tire in the contact area, represent the load running along the tire.

A pneumatic tire (these are the tires used for automatic) is a shell “tensioned” by internal air pressure. The shape of the tire, as everyone knows, is close to toridal.

Let us mentally select the small element $A$ of the tire shell (Fig. 3). This area is subject to tension forces from its neighbors directed along the parallel and along the meridian (more precisely, along tangents to them). Let the values ​​of these forces per unit length (along the parallel) and per unit width (along the meridian) of the element $A$ be equal to $|\vec{N}_x|$ and $|\vec{N}_y|$, respectively. The analysis depends mostly on these forces and the speed of the tire to give the critical speed value. The formula for $V_{kp}$ in this case turns out to be similar to formula (3) for a heavy tape -

$$|\vec{V}_{kp}| = \sqrt{\frac{|N_x|}{m'}}$$

- with the difference that here $m'$ is the mass per unit surface of the shell. From formula (4) it is clear that the critical speed is greater, the stronger

To increase the strength of the tire, when making the shell, a mesh of very strong threads (cord) is used as “reinforcement”. Sometimes the threads are made of steel (for automatic truck tires, which must withstand heavy loads). To appreciate the role of the mesh reinforcement, it is enough to say that if it were not there, the tire would “burst” at pressures 20-30 times less than those that a modern tire with mesh can withstand. (For automatic passenger cars this pressure is 1.5-2 atm, for trucks - 4-6 atm.)

The direction of the cord threads is characterized by the angle $\alpha$. The value of the critical speed depends on this angle:

$$|\vec{V}_{kp}| = K \text{ ctg } \alpha.$$

Here $K$ is a coefficient determined by the air pressure $p$ in the tire, the radius $r$ of the tire cross-section and the value $m'$:

$$K = \sqrt{\frac{pr}{m'}}.$$

Thus,

$$|\vec{V}_{kp}| = \sqrt{\frac{pr}{m'}} \text{ ctg } \alpha.$$

This is the so-called Turner formula. You can derive it yourself using your high school physics course value.
