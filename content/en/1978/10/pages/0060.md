⟦folio 58⟧

Edgar Poe has a relatively unimportant fantasy story, “The Extraordinary Adventure of a Certain Hans Pfaal.”
The hero of this story made an amazing discovery - he obtained an extraordinary gas, the density of which is 37.4 times less than the density of hydrogen. A balloon filled with such gas has the wrong lifting force. With his help, Hans Pfaal even managed to get to the Moon.

We will not prove that a gas lighter than hydrogen does not exist in nature - this is what was promised. (Can you explain why this is impossible?) But the problem is not without interest: if such a gas did exist, how many times would it increase the lifting force of a balloon (compared to a balloon filled with hydrogen)?

Despite the simplicity of the task, many people do not immediately find the correct answer. This is where the “logical” choice comes in: since gas is 37.4 times lighter than hydrogen, its lifting force is the same amount of times greater. Perhaps Edgar Allan Poe expanded on this last conclusion when he wrote his story. However, it is equally believed that he himself became the victim of an erroneous reasoning, outwardly solid.

However, the simplest calculation shows that the gain in lift was so insignificant that it could not be taken into account at all. Let's check it out. To do this, let's find the lifting force of a balloon filled with hydrogen and a balloon filled with Hans Pfaal's gas.

Let the volume of air in the ball be 1 m³. The density of air is 0.00129 g/cm³, hydrogen is 0.00009 g/cm³, and Hans Pfaal gas is 0.0000024 g/cm³. Let us recall that the lifting force of the air of a ball is the difference between the buoyant force, equal to the gravitational force of the air displaced by the ball, and the gravitational force of the gas inside the ball (we will assume that the shell of the ball is weightless).
