(M., “Higher School”, 1975, 170 pp.).

The first of these books was written specifically for students in grades 9-10. It talks about the initial concepts of mathematical logic, its symbolic language and its expressive capabilities. The author, relying on well-chosen examples, very simply and clearly covers such issues as necessary and sufficient conditions, mutually inverse and mutually opposite statements. Considerable attention is paid to the so-called generality statements, explaining how such statements are proven using mathematical induction and how they are refuted by constructors.

The reader will undoubtedly be interested in the applications of symbolic logic discussed in the book, in particular the solution of “logical” problems traditional for popular mathematics.

Here is one such task:

Brown, Jones and Sim are charged with complicity in bank fraud. The kidnappers fled in an automatic vehicle that was waiting for them. During the investigation, Brown testified that the presentation was on a blue Buick, Jones said that it was a black Chrysler, and Simu argued that it was a Ford Musmane and by no means blue. It became known that, wanting to confuse the investigation, each of them correctly indicated either the make of the car or its name. What kind of cement was it and what brand?

It turns out that the answer to this question can be “calculated.” How exactly to do this is explained in detail in the book.

An important advantage of Kutasov’s book is the problem it contains. Some of them have been disassembled, others are intended for independent solution and are provided with detailed instructions and answers.

Whereas Kutasov’s book deals almost exclusively with the structures of mathematical statements and is a perfectly written book

Edelman goes much further and requires server services (but not representative knowledge) from the reader. Here, too, and in more detail, the language in which mathematical statements are formulated is considered, but in addition to this, the most important concepts of logical consequence, inference and proof are studied. The main attention is paid to the analysis and formal description of the means by which one of these statements is allowed to be seen by others.

Let us consider, for example, the following simple reasoning: Every rhombus is a parallelem, the figure ABCD is a rhombus; This means that the figure ABCD is pa-

rallylopardm. From Edelman's book the reader learns that this reasoning is obtained as a special case of some certain schemes. For example, we consider the problem of controlling an elevator motor when descending from a floor to the first. The activation of the motor depends on a number of conditions (whether the shaft doors on both floors and the cabin doors are closed, whether the passenger is in the cabin, whether the descent button in the cabin is pressed, whether the call button is pressed on the first floor). The book compiles a formula that takes into account all these conditions, and builds a corresponding switching circuit.

A patient and thoughtful student could, in principle, begin his acquaintance with mathematical logic vaguely from Edelman's book. However, it is easier to study Kutasov's more elementary and easy-to-read book. For those who are interested in the subject, Edelman's book can be recommended as relatively accessible and at the same time quite thorough in “server” mathematical logic.

Of course, the books recommended here cannot be considered completely flawless. In our opinion, for example, in Kutasov’s book it is not always convenient to think through the notation, and in Edelman’s book it would be desirable to increase the number of exercises (especially in the important third chapter). Presumably, the authors will improve their manuals in future articles. In general, however, each of the books appears to be a good introduction to the subject, based on proper pedagogical experience.
