vizne $-\|\vec{f}\| \sim \frac{1}{R}$. If $\vec{V} = 0$ (the tape is motionless), $\|\vec{f}\| = \frac{|\vec{N}|}{R}$ – the pressure force is determined by the tension force and the curvature of the tube at a given point.

With the appearance of speed $\vec{V}$ and with its increase, the value $\|\vec{f}\|$ begins to decrease and at a speed equal to

$$|\vec{V}| = \sqrt{\frac{|\vec{N}|}{m}},$$

vanishes immediately along the entire length of the tube. Let's call this speed critical ($V_{\kappa p}$).

What does $-\|\vec{f}\| = 0$ mean? This means that at $\|\vec{V}\| = V_{\kappa p}$ the tape does not interact with the tube. If the tube is changed at this speed value, then the curvature caused by it remains - the tape will still be bent in section $AB$.

In relation to the observable, moving to the right with a speed equal in magnitude to $V_{\kappa p}$, this bezite curvature along the tape to the left with a speed of $V_{\kappa p}$. This phenomenon is called a wave traveling along a tape, and the speed $V_{\kappa p}$ is the speed of wave propagation.

Let us now imagine that $|\vec{V}| < V_{\kappa p}$ and we removed the tube, but added to the tape in section $AB$ pressure forces $f$, distributed along the length of the tape in the same way as from the tube. It is clear that in this case the tape will receive exactly the same curvature as it had with the tube. At each point, the curvature of the tape $\frac{1}{R}$ will be related to the applied pressure forces by the same formula (2). If the pressure forces $f$ are kept constant and the belt speed is increased, then, as follows from formula (2), the degree of belt curvature, characterized by the value $\frac{1}{R}$, will increase. (In this case, the nature of the distribution of the value $\frac{1}{R}$ along the length of the section $AB$ will be determined by the law of distribution of pressure forces $f$ along the length of this section.)

With a constant law of distribution of pressure forces in section $AB$ and with a belt speed close to critical, it is thus possible to obtain an angularly large curvature of the belt in section $AB$.

This choice can be framed differently. If a load “runs” along the belt, that is, pressure, in some way distributed in section $AB$, and the magnitude of this load does not change, and the speed of its propagation relative to the belt increases, then the degree of curvature of the tape in section $AB$ returns with increasing speed, and therefore the deformation of the tape from a given load returns. As the speed approaches $V_{\kappa p}$, the tape curvature will return to unlimited. From this point of view, it turns out that when the load speed is close to critical, the tension tape ceases to resist the load.

The resulting choice does not depend on the nature of the load distribution in section $AB$, just as the expression for $V_{\kappa p}$ does not depend on the shape of the curved tube.

Let's move on to automation

The concept of the critical speed of a “running” load can be applied, with the help of more complex calculations, to other cases, such as, for example, a load running along a rail from a wheel of a railway car. The rail also bends under this load, but the task in this case is more complicated, since the rail, unlike the tape, has so-called “flexural rigidity” and lies on an “elastic base” - the ground. However, in this case there are critical speeds. Calculations show that this speed is very high (about 1000 km/h) and can be of practical importance only when creating trains moving at very high, not yet feasible, speeds.
