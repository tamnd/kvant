Where is the friction force directed?

If you claim that the friction force is directed against the movement, then everything in this statement is correct. For example, a friction force $\vec{F}_{\text{tr}}$ acts on a sled sliding down a mountain, directed in the direction opposite to the speed of the sled (Fig. 1). But very often the force of friction brings unexpected consequences. Here are some examples.

A person walks on an equal footing, the person’s speed is directed to the right. Where is the friction force directed? The correct answer is also to the right, that is, in the direction of the person’s movement, and not in the opposite direction (Fig. 2). However, let's look at this case in more detail. When a person, taking a step, pushes his foot off the ground, the sole of the shoe “tends” to move backward, in the direction opposite to the person’s movement. And the friction force acting from the earth on the sole is directed against the movement of the sole, that is, to the right, as shown in figure 2. The movement of the sole relative to the ground plays the main role—the friction force acts against it.

If the friction is sufficient for a person to click strongly without the opposite, or the friction is small, the opposite begins: the sole moves backward, the head remains in place, the balance is disturbed, and the person falls. Try to walk faster on smooth ice!

However, the process of walking does not consist only of pushes. The push is the first stage, besides it there are other characteristic movements: the “lagging” hora is carried forward and stands on the ground. In this case, the friction force is directed against the person’s movement.

Thus, when walking, the direction of the friction force changes all the time.

The same thing happens with more skis: when the skier is pushed by sticks and skis (Fig. 3, a), the direction of the friction force is one, and with further movement by inertia (Fig. 3, b) - another. Obviously, for running on flats, it is advantageous that during free gliding the friction force should be minimal, and when pushing there should be no slipping, that is, the friction force should be as great as possible.

This can be achieved, for example, by gluing fur onto the lie in such a way that the pile is directed from the toe of the lie to its end. Thus, they are really crawling in the North.

Let us now see how the friction force is directed when a person walks in the mountains. If the descent is quite round, then the person “brakes” with his feet, trying to prevent sliding (Fig. 4, a): the friction force is directed against the person’s speed.

During a gentle descent and when climbing a mountain (Fig. 4, b), there is no need to brake; everything will happen in the same way as in the case of walking on a level surface.
