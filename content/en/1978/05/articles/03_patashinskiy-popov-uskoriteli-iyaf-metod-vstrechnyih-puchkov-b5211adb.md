BINP accelerators - colliding beam method

In 1967, Academician G.I. Budekru and his closest collaborators were awarded the Lenin Prize for the development of the colliding beam method. This recognition summed up the first stage of work, which marked the beginning of the use of this very promising method in high-energy physics. The advantages of the colliding beam method, the history of its creation at the Institute of Nuclear Physics of the Siberian Branch of the USSR Academy of Sciences, the founder and director of which was the outstanding Soviet physicist and organizer of science, Academician G. I. Budekr, are described in this article.

I already spoke about why accelerators are needed and about the design of general accelerators «Kvant» (see No. 4, 1977). The main way to study the microworld at present is the occurrence of elementary particles. The deeper we want to penetrate into the microworld, the greater the collision energy must be. In an experiment, an elementary particle collides with another elementary particle. The result of the collision is recorded - the speed of the particles that deviated or were born during the collision, their type. The depth at which we thus penetrate into the microworld is determined by the energies of the accelerated particles. But on the path to the development of accelerator technology, two significant difficulties arise. The first is that in order to impart ever greater energies to accelerated particles, enormous accelerators are needed, which are very expensive. The second difficulty is of a fundamental nature.

In order to understand what it involves, let us turn to the well-known process of collision of balls.

Let us consider a “head-on” collision of two balls of equal masses. When they collide, the balls begin to deform, and part of the initial kinetic energy of the system turns into potential deformation energy of the balls. Let's imagine that we are studying to read our balls, we are interested in under what conditions they break. It is clear that these conditions are determined precisely by the magnitude of the potential energy of deformation of the balls. What part of the composition

Does this energy differ from the initial kinetic energy of the system?

Let us first consider the case when one of the balls initially ends up dead. Let a ball collide with a ball at rest whose kinetic energy is $E = \frac{mv^2}{2}$. Immediately after the collision, the balls begin to deform. It is obvious that the deformation of the balls increases until the relative speed of the balls becomes equal to zero. At this moment, the balls move as one unit (a ball with mass $2m$) with a certain speed $\vec{u}$, and the deformation energy of the balls is maximum. From the law of conservation of momentum $-m|\vec{v}| = 2m|\vec{u}|$ it follows that $|\vec{u}| = |\vec{v}|/2$. According to the law of conservation of energy $E = \frac{2mu^2}{2} + E_0$, where $E_0$ is the maximum deformation energy of the balls. Hence,

$$E_0 = E - \frac{2m(v/2)^2}{2} = \frac{1}{2} E.$$

Thus, the maximum deformation energy of the balls in the case when one of them initially dies is equal to half the initial kinetic energy of the system.

It’s a different matter when two balls move towards each other with speeds $|\vec{v}|$ equal in absolute value. In this case, the deformation energy $E'_0$ during conservation is maximum at the moment when the velocities of the balls are zero. From the law of conservation of energy it follows that

$$E'_0 = E'$$

where $E' = 2\frac{mv^2}{2}$ is the initial energy of the system. So, when balls moving towards each other collide, all the initial kinetic energy of the system is converted into deformation energy.

The case we considered of the collision of two balls, one of which is initially destroyed, is a model of a situation occurring in a common accelerator - an accelerated frequency hits a moving target on it. The deformation energy of the balls is an analogue of the energy that, when accelerated particles collide with target atoms, determines the processes occurring in the atoms, the possibility of the birth of new particles, and their energy. It is this “useful” energy that interests the researcher.

Now it becomes clear to us the fundamental difficulty that arose before the creator of the accelerator. When an accelerated particle collides with the atoms of a stationary target, part of its energy is spent on communicating the speed of the “particle + atom” system (or rather, “particle + particles born in the collision”) as a whole. And this part of the energy “wasted” without a user is of interest to the energy of the particles born in the process of collision, and the energy of movement of the system as a whole is a kind of “energy cost” in the process. In the case when the particle speed is small compared to the speed of light, these “costs” amount to half of the initial energy of the accelerated particles. As a result, the share of “useful” energy greatly decreases with increasing particle energy. So, with an energy of accelerated particles of the order of 10 GeV (1 GeV = 10^9 EV), this fraction is less than a percent - about 100 MeV (1 MeV = 10^6 EV). In order to obtain a “useful” energy of 2 GeV during the collision, it would be necessary to accelerate electrons to an energy of 4 TeV (1 TeV = 10^12 EV). An electron accelerator at such energies would have a length comparable to the circumference of the Earth at the equator!

What if, together with a stationary target, we use a counter beam of accelerated particles? The model of the processes occurring in such an accelerator is the case we considered of the collision of two balls moving towards each other. And then “useful” energy of 2 GeV, almost inaccessible to a general electron accelerator, can be obtained by colliding particle beams accelerated to an energy of 1 GeV per particle.

To prevent the beam from being scattered by gas particles in the storage chamber during hours of rotation in a circular orbit, a high vacuum is required - no more than 10^7 gas particles per 1 cm³. It is necessary that the admission of each new portion of particles does not “spoil” the already accumulated beam. It is necessary to establish (“suppress”) the instability of the beam—when a part deviates from the direction of beam motion, forces arise that eject particles onto the walls of the ring. These and many other problems require sophisticated designers and engineering developments.

In 1958, at the suggestion of I.V. Kurchatov, the laboratory of new acceleration methods moved to Siberia. Here, near Novosibirsk, the Academgorodok of the Siberian Branch of the USSR Academy of Sciences set its sights. The laboratory turns into the Institute of Nuclear Physics (INP) of the Siberian Branch of the USSR Academy of Sciences. G.I. Budker became the director of the institute.

The institute emerged with explosive speed. Its director is young, a great collaborator - former students and graduate students. Budker suggested a directed rhythm of work, and the team accepted this rhythm. “The average level in science is unstable. If we don’t move forward to the limit of our capabilities, we will fall behind and slide to a low level,” Budker said. “If we don’t do everything to be first, we will become a flat institution.” The task assigned to the institute was necessarily difficult. The assembly of the accelerator and storage wheels of the first installation on counter-propagating electron beams - VEP-1 - begins. The elements of the new accelerator must operate in modes that are much more complex than the elements of the large accelerator. Each node is a new problem. Many of G.I. Budker’s discoveries in physics are known, but when they talk about the “Budker method”, this is about the work of the institute. The Budker method is a Japanese method of new, more advanced solutions to problems. And there are plenty of people capable of finding such solutions. “Every talented person must work creatively and independently,” the director asserted and entrusted his students with increasingly complex areas of work. Many elements of the VEP-1 installation were essentially opened into a technical accelerator using colliding beams.

In 1963, electron beams collided in the VEP-1 storage ring. There are e⁻ e⁻-collisions on colliding beams! In addition to confirming the possibility of implementing an accelerator using colliding beams, the experiment at VEP-1 provided new physical information - the validity of Coulomb’s law was proven at distances of the order of 10⁻¹⁴ cm. (Recall that the “classical” electron radius is 2.7 × 10⁻¹³ cm.)

In parallel with the experiments, an installation for colliding beams of electrons and positrons, VEPP-2, was installed at VEP-1. In 1965, at this installation, for the first time in the world, an experiment on the collision of electron and positron beams was carried out.

Over the past 10 years, the counter-beam method has gained worldwide recognition. About 10 storage pits have been built in different countries and new ones are being built. The energies and currents in the beams grow. Experiments on colliding beams lead to a stream of new discoveries in the field of high-energy physics and elementary particle physics.

The history of the creation and development of this method in our country is inextricably linked with the Institute of Nuclear Physics of the Siberian Branch of the USSR Academy of Sciences, with the name of its first director, Academician G.I. Budker. The institute and the hike itself are an accelerator, built according to the Budker method. BINP is also a kind of experiment. Now this experiment is being continued by Budker’s students. Forward - the most difficult problems, new problems, new results. The work continues.
