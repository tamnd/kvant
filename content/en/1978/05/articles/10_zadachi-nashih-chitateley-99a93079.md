5. The following method is proposed for calculating the surface area of ​​a sphere. Divide the sphere with the “equator” into two hemispheres and one of them at a distance. Let us divide the equator into $n$ concussive parts by points $A_1, A_2, \dots, A_n$ and connect them with the “stripes” $P$ “meridans”. Now let us imagine that the regular $n$-gon $A_1 \dots A_n$ rises above the equatorial plane, remaining parallel to it and descending to the point $P$ so that its vertices always lie on the arcs $A_iP$. At the same time, its contour sweeps out some dome-shaped surface $\Pi_n$ (Fig. 5). It is clear that the surface $\Pi_n$ for large $n$ is estimated to be close to a hemisphere; Moreover, it is not difficult to prove (try) that its area at $n \to \infty$ tends to the area of ​​a hemisphere. If the doblets $A_iA_{i+1}P$ are straightened, we get $n$ triangles with identical bases, the sum of their lengths is equal to the perimeter of the $p_n n$-gon $A_1 \dots A_n$, and heights $h_n$. Since, obviously, $\lim_{n \to \infty} p_n = 2\pi R$, where $R$ is the radius of the sphere, and $\lim_{n \to \infty} h_n = \frac{1}{2}\pi R$, then the limit of the surface areas $\Pi_n$ is $\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{2} p_n h_n = \frac{1}{2}\pi^2 R^2$. Therefore, the area of ​​the sphere is $\pi^2 R^2$. Where is the error?

Prove that there are two points of different polyhedra placed by the same number, the distance between which is at least $\sqrt{2 \cdot \frac{1}{64}}$ m. Is it possible to strengthen this estimate?

2. Prove that the equation $x^2 - 2xy = 1978$ has no integer solutions.

3. An equal-volume trapezoid was cut out of cardboard, in which the angle between the diagonals is equal to φ. To which peaks is its center of gravity close - to the peaks smaller than the base or greater than it?

4. The king is on the bottom leftmost black square of the chessboard. It can only move diagonally. What is the maximum number of fields he can bypass if he can hit no more than once on each field? Consider the case when the size of the chess field is 1) $9 \times 9$; 2) $(2n-1) \times (2n-1)$; 3) $8 \times 8$; 4) $2n \times 2n$. F. Bartheen
