Where is the friction force directed?

If you claim that the friction force is directed against the movement, then everything in this statement is correct. For example, a friction force $\vec{F}_{\text{tr}}$ acts on a sled sliding down a mountain, directed in the direction opposite to the speed of the sled (Fig. 1). But very often the force of friction brings unexpected consequences. Here are some examples.

A person walks on an equal footing, the person’s speed is directed to the right. Where is the friction force directed? The correct answer is also to the right, that is, in the direction of the person’s movement, and not in the opposite direction (Fig. 2). However, let's look at this case in more detail. When a person, taking a step, pushes his foot off the ground, the sole of the shoe “tends” to move backward, in the direction opposite to the person’s movement. And the friction force acting from the earth on the sole is directed against the movement of the sole, that is, to the right, as shown in figure 2. The movement of the sole relative to the ground plays the main role—the friction force acts against it.

If the friction is sufficient for a person to click strongly without the opposite, or the friction is small, the opposite begins: the sole moves backward, the head remains in place, the balance is disturbed, and the person falls. Try to walk faster on smooth ice!
