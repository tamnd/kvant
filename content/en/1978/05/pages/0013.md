To prevent the beam from being scattered by gas particles in the storage chamber during hours of rotation in a circular orbit, a high vacuum is required - no more than 10^7 gas particles per 1 cm³. It is necessary that the admission of each new portion of particles does not “spoil” the already accumulated beam. It is necessary to establish (“suppress”) the instability of the beam—when a part deviates from the direction of beam motion, forces arise that eject particles onto the walls of the ring. These and many other problems require sophisticated designers and engineering developments.

In 1958, at the suggestion of I.V. Kurchatov, the laboratory of new acceleration methods moved to Siberia. Here, near Novosibirsk, the Academgorodok of the Siberian Branch of the USSR Academy of Sciences set its sights. The laboratory turns into the Institute of Nuclear Physics (INP) of the Siberian Branch of the USSR Academy of Sciences. G.I. Budker became the director of the institute.

The institute emerged with explosive speed. Its director is young, a great collaborator - former students and graduate students. Budker suggested a directed rhythm of work, and the team accepted this rhythm. “The average level in science is unstable. If we don’t move forward to the limit of our capabilities, we will fall behind and slide to a low level,” Budker said. “If we don’t do everything to be first, we will become a flat institution.” The task assigned to the institute was necessarily difficult. The assembly of the accelerator and storage wheels of the first installation on counter-propagating electron beams - VEP-1 - begins. The elements of the new accelerator must operate in modes that are much more complex than the elements of the large accelerator. Each node is a new problem. Many of G.I. Budker’s discoveries in physics are known, but when they talk about the “Budker method”, this is about the work of the institute. The Budker method is a Japanese method of new, more advanced solutions to problems. And there are plenty of people capable of finding such solutions. “Every talented person must work creatively and independently,” the director asserted and entrusted his students with increasingly complex areas of work. Many elements of the VEP-1 installation were essentially opened into a technical accelerator using colliding beams.

In 1963, electron beams collided in the VEP-1 storage ring. There are e⁻ e⁻-collisions on colliding beams! In addition to confirming the possibility of implementing an accelerator using colliding beams, the experiment at VEP-1 provided new physical information - the validity of Coulomb’s law was proven at distances of the order of 10⁻¹⁴ cm. (Recall that the “classical” electron radius is 2.7 × 10⁻¹³ cm.)

In parallel with the experiments, an installation for colliding beams of electrons and positrons, VEPP-2, was installed at VEP-1. In 1965, at this installation, for the first time in the world, an experiment on the collision of electron and positron beams was carried out.
