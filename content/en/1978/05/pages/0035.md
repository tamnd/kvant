Let us find the limit of the areas $S(n, k_n)$ of surfaces $\Pi(n, k_n)$. Let $s(n, k)$ be the area of ​​one face of the surface $\Pi(n, k)$, then (see Fig. 4) $s(n, k) = |AH| \cdot |CH|$. If the radius of the base of the cylinder is $R$, then $|AH| = |OA| \sin \frac{\pi}{n} = R \sin \frac{\pi}{n}$; further, $|CH| = \sqrt{|HD|^2 + |CD|^2}$, $|HD| = R \left(1 - \cos \frac{\pi}{n}\right), |CD| = \frac{H}{k}$.

Thus,

$$s(n, k) = R \sin \frac{\pi}{n} \sqrt{\frac{H^2}{k^2} + R^2 \left(1 - \cos \frac{\pi}{n}\right)^2} = R H \sin \frac{\pi}{n} \sqrt{\frac{1}{k^2} + 4 \frac{R^2}{H^2} \left(\sin \frac{\pi}{2n}\right)^2}.$$

$$S(n, k) = 2\pi R H \frac{\sin \frac{\pi}{n}}{\pi n} \times \sqrt{1 + \frac{R^2 \pi^4}{4H^2} \frac{k^2}{n^4} \left(\frac{\sin \frac{\pi}{2n}}{2n}\right)^4}.$$

Using the fact that $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x} = 1$ one can easily find $S = \lim_{n \to \infty} S(n, k_n)$ for various sequences $k_n$. In particular, at $k_n \equiv 1$ (and in general, at $k_n = \text{const}$) $S = 2\pi RH$ (correct answer!). The same answer is obtained with $k_n = n$. But then there will be disappointment: at $k_n = n^2$ the limit will be different, and at $k_n = n^3$ the sequence $S(n, k_n)$ is completely unbounded (see also Problem 4).

**Fig. 4. One grain of “Schwartz boot”.**

The reason is that if $k_n$ grows too quickly, the edges of surfaces $\Pi(n, k_n)$ will be located at a large angle to the surface of the cylinder, $\Pi(n, k_n)$ “gathers into an accordion”; it is clear that due to such folds the surface area increases greatly.

The described construction was proposed in 1890 by the famous German mathematician G. A. Schwartz; it is called the “Schwartz boot.” From this construction it is clear that the concept of “convergence” of the sequence $\Pi_n$ to $\Pi$ in our definition of surface area should be refined - so that not only the distances between the points of surfaces $\Pi_n$ and $\Pi$ are taken into account, but also the angles between the faces of surfaces $\Pi_n$ and surface $\Pi$. With this clarification, the definition becomes complex, cumbersome and, of course, inappropriate in a school course.
