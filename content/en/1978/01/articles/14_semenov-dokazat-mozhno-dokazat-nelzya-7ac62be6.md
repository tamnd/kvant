CAN YOU PROVE IT?
- IT IS IMPOSSIBLE TO PROVE!

- Dad, ah, dad! Does a squirrel eat pine nuts?
- Yes, daughter.
- Is cedar a kind of grass?
- What are you talking about, Tanyusha! Cedar is a tree!
- If my squirrel were not a puppet, but a real one, I would take it into the forest, and if this squirrel jumped onto some tree, then it was a cedar.
(From the conversation of little Tanyusha)

It is difficult to say at what age we begin to engage in evidence. Maybe from the time when we start to make mistakes and try to tell, when we turn our dolls into heroes in fairy tales, mixing children’s fantasy “with adult reality.” An example is the conversation just quoted. You probably noticed that little Tanya’s conversation contains (perhaps the first in her life) evidence. Look, she believes that:
1) a squirrel eats pine nuts;
2) cedar is a tree;
3) A squirrel climbed up this tree.

On this basis, Tanya makes the choice that this tree is cedar. True, Tanya’s conclusion is wrong. For this choice to be correct, one more condition is needed:

4) if the tree is not a cedar, then the squirrel will not climb that tree (otherwise: of all the trees, the squirrel climbs milk onto the cedar).

Let's look, however, at Tanino's proof from the other side. She had three listed conditions at her disposal. Is it possible, based on them, without using any other conditions, to prove the resulting choice? - Of course not! How about refuting? - No, either! It may turn out that the tree the squirrel climbed is a cedar, and then the conclusion is correct, but the squirrel might just as well have power on the shore, and then the conclusion is incorrect. A mathematician would say that Tanya's conclusion is independent of her three conditions.

And yet... even at an older age, we also sometimes make similar mistakes. Do you have any doubts? Then listen to the story that

happened to our weak sixth grader Stepa Moshkin and his friends when he was studying geometry.

1. Styopa and his friends are looking for proof

—The third property of distances is suspicious! Why does it give without proof? Each of them can be proven using the first two properties of distances! - Styopa said thoughtfully.

The next day, all Styopa’s friends were doing this, captivated by his idea. Their efforts were not directed: several pieces of evidence were soon found.

First of all, let us remind the reader of the properties of distances. There are three of them, as you remember.

1. The distance from point A to point B is positive if they are different, and equal to zero if they are the same:

$$|AB| > 0, \text{ если } A \neq B, \text{ и } |AB| = 0, \text{ если } A = B.$$

2. The distance from point A to point B is equal to the distance from point B to point A:

$$|AB| = |BA|.$$

3. For any three points A, B, C, the distance from A to C is not more (less than or equal to) the sum of the distances from A to B and from B to C:

$$|AC| \leq |AB| + |BC|.$$

And now get acquainted with one of the proofs of Styopa and his friends.

It is necessary to prove that for any three points A, B, C the following inequality holds:

$$|AC| \leq |AB| + |BC|.$$

Let's say it's not a poppy. Let

$$|AC| > |AB| + |BC|.$$

(*) Since A, B, C are any three points, we can assume, for example, that point B coincides with C, point A is different from B: B = C, A ≠ B. Since in this case |BC| = 0 (no property 1), then from (*) we obtain that |AC| > |AB|. On the other hand, since points B and C coincide, |AC| = |AB|. We came to a contradiction: |AC| > |AB| and |AC| = |AB|. Therefore, the assumption that |AC| > |AB| + |BC|, incorrect. Naturally, we remain that |AC| ≤ |AB| + |BC|, that is, property 3 is satisfied!

Styopa Moshkin and his friends rejoiced. The proof of the third property of distances has been found! Moreover, this proof uses only the first property! Hence a new idea: is it not possible to prove the second property of distances using the first? “Mathematical Society” Styopa Moshkin set to work with satisfaction. It is unknown when and how it would all end if it were not for Pyotr Ivanovich.

2. There is no proof!

At the next geometry lesson, Styopa and his friends introduced Pyotr Ivanovich to the evidence they had found. He listened to them carefully and said.

- You know, every one of your evidence contains an error. But this is not the most important thing. I could give such an answer without introducing your evidence. You cannot prove the third property using the first two properties of distances!

- How! - Styopa exclaimed. - It was impossible! And if before-

- No scientist, friends, will do this, because he knows in advance about the impossibility of such documentation. And a tenth-grader will not make such attempts if he knows mathematics well enough.

Euclid's axim of parallelism has been documented for 2 thousand years by many famous and talented mathematicians of the world. Further, Nikolai Ivanovich Lobachevsky first tried to do this. And only in the past it was established that it is impossible to prove this axiom on the basis of other axioms of Euclid. Now compare a few days of your labor with the labor of 2 thousand years! Do you see how modest your services are compared to the efforts of many mathematicians who devoted their entire lives to proving the parallelism axiom?

3. Is it impossible to prove? - Why?

- But how, how can you find out that proof is impossible? - all the students in the class spoke at once, even those who were not looking for a document of the third property of distance.

“First of all,” began Pyotr Ivanovich, “do not consider that distance is a mandatory something obtained with the help of a scale ruler.” After all, the axioms of distances say nothing about this! And why not assume that the distance can be changed, say, in degrees, with the help of transport? Do the axioms of distance prohibit this? Yes, and you will first change the distance in minutes - from home to school 10 minutes. So, now we will build a model in which properties 1 and 2 of distances will be fulfilled, but property 3 will not be fulfilled.

Imagine such a fantastic situation. The city (plane) is inhabited by extremely lazy creatures. They don’t go to work, to the movies, to visit each other, they just call each other on the phone. Moreover, you can get through from point A to point B and from B to A in 3 minutes. You can reach any other point from A and B in 1 minute. From any point other than A and B, you can also reach any other point in 1 minute. And from any point you can “get through” to it in zero time.

For such lazy people, the distance from one point to another is the time it takes to call from the first point to the second.

Check out the running distance in this fantastic city. Do you see? The first and second executions, but the third - not. For example, $|AB| > |AC| + |CB|$, since $|AB| = 3$, $|AC| + |CB| = 1+1=2$.

“Well, now we can draw a conclusion,” said the teacher. — What does it mean to “prove the third property of distances using the first two properties”?

“This means proving that if properties 1 and 2 are satisfied, then property 3 will also be satisfied,” Styopa answered immediately for everyone.

— And in considering the model, properties 1 and 2 are satisfied, but property 3 is not satisfied. This means that it is impossible to prove property 3 using properties 1 and 2! Property 3 is independent of properties 1 and 2.

4. New model

You are probably eager to find out whether Styopa and his friends have proven the second property of distances using the first one? And does such proof exist?

After receiving the “lesson,” Styopa Moshkin’s “mathematical society” began to act more cautiously. Its members realized that doing mathematics involves not only proving proofs, but also finding out: do they exist? First, the guys decided to try to build a model in which the first property of distances would be fulfilled, but the second would not. Luck would have revealed that there is no proof of the second property based on the first. And they were lucky. This is the “telephone” model they built: $|AB| = 2, |BA| = 1, |AC| = |CA| = |BC| = |CB| = 1$. Obviously, the first property of distances is satisfied for all points in such a model. However, for the point

$A$ and $B$ the second property is not satisfied: $|AB| \neq |BA|$. Consequently, the second property of distances is independent of the first property.

5. Epilogue

This is how another story ended that happened with sixth grader Stepa Moshkin. Do you think he calmed down after it? Wrong! Styopa has prepared tasks for you!

2. Find out whether the third property of distances is satisfied in the model built by Stepa and his friends.
3. Take two different points $A$ and $B$. We will assume that $|AB| = 1, |BA| = 3$. Consider the distance between any other non-coinciding points equal to 1, between coincidences - 0. Using this model, prove the second and third properties of distances independently of the first.
4. Prove by constructing an appropriate model that the second property of distances is independent of the third property.
5. Draw a triangle $ABC$ such that $\hat{C} = 100^\circ, \hat{A} = 30^\circ, \hat{B} = 50^\circ$. For the distance between two vertices of a triangle, the technique for making the angle at the third vertex is: $|AB| = 100, |AC| = 50, |BC| = 30$. The distance between any other distinct points will be considered equal to 1, the distance between coincident points will be considered equal to 0. Use this model to independently prove the third property using the first two properties of distances.
