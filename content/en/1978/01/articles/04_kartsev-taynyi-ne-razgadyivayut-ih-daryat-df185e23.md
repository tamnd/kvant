They enthusiastically welcomed this discovery. Arago, having collected many facts indicating the connection between lightning and magnetism, felt that he was on the verge of a new discovery. Joy and annoyance - these were perhaps the feelings that he experienced after seeing a task that had not been given to him for a long time. Solution found by Ørsted.

Historians of science will probably remain ignorant and bewildered for a long time regarding the circumstances of the discovery, which has now become partly a classic example of a happy accident. The exact date of this discovery is not known: some researchers attribute it to 1819, some to 1820. Some even doubt Oersted's authorship. However, let's not get ahead of ourselves.

On February 15, 1820, Ørsted, already an emeritus professor of chemistry at the University of Copenhagen, gave a lecture to his students. The lecture was accompanied by demonstrations. On the laboratory table there was a current source, a wire that closed its terminals, and a compass. While Oersted closed the circuit, the compass needle trembled and turned. When the circuit was opened, the arrow returned. This was the first experimental confirmation of the connection between electricity and magnetism, something that many scientists had been looking for for so long.

It would seem that everything is clear. Oersted demonstrated to the students yet another confirmation of the long-held idea of ​​the universal connection of phenomena. But why do doubts arise? Why did the circumstances of this event later become the subject of so much controversy? The fact is that the students who attended the lecture later told something completely different. According to them, Oersted wanted to demonstrate at the lecture only the interesting property of electricity to heat a wire, and the compass ended up on the table as a complete coincidence. And it was precisely by chance that they explained that the compass lay next to this wire, and it was quite by chance, in their opinion, that one of the keen-sighted students drew attention to the turning arrow, and the surprise and delight of the professor, according to them, was genuine. Oersted himself, in his later works, wrote: “Everyone present in the audience witnessed that I announced in advance the result of the experiment. The discovery, therefore, was not an accident, as Professor Gilbert would have liked to conclude from the expressions that I used when first announcing the discovery.” Is it really that important - was Oersted's discovery accidental or not? And what does this mean, “accidental discovery”? Have you heard, for example, that the chemist Oersted gave lectures on electricity? Of course not. Electricity was discovered relatively recently *), the knowledge base on electricity at that time was not great, and practicing it did not require any special preparation. So physicists, chemists, and mechanics could study it. The equipment was also simple; it could be made in any workshop. Therefore, in general, there was nothing accidental in Oersted’s lecture, and indeed in its basis. Oersted, giving a generally incorrect theoretical interpretation of the experiment, raised a deep thought about the external characteristics of electromagnetic phenomena. “Vortex” did not find a solution for a long time; most scientists were convinced that the forces acting between a current-carrying conductor and a magnetic needle are general forces of attraction and repulsion, similar to the Newtonian forces of universal gravitation or the Coulomb forces of interaction between electric charges. Oersted's experiment proves not only the connection between the electricist and magnetism. What was revealed to Oersted was a new secret that did not fit within the framework of known laws.

Oersted's memoirs were published on July 21, 1820. It is no coincidence that we date this precisely. Further events developed at a fairly continuous pace for the then leisurely science. Within a few days, the memoir appeared in Geneva, where Arago was visiting at that time. The very first acquaintance with Oersted's experience proved to him that the solution to the problem that he and many others had been working on had been found. The impression from the experiments was so great that one of those present at the demonstration stood up and excitedly uttered the phrase that later became famous: “Gentlemen, a revolution will take place!”

Arago returns to Paris shocked. At the very first meeting of the Academy, which he attended immediately upon his return, on September 4, 1820, he made an oral report about Oersted’s experiments. The notes made in the academic journal by Lenin's hand as a protocol officer indicate that the academicians asked Arago at the next meeting, September 22, to show everyone present Oersted's experience, as they say, “in full size.”

Academician Amperar listened to Arago's message with special attention. He perhaps felt at that moment that he had come, in the face of the whole world, to accept the baton of discovery from Oersted’s hands. He had been waiting for this hour for a long time - about twenty years - like Arago and like Oersted. And then the hour struck - September 4, 1820

Amperar realized that he had to act. Just weeks later, he informed the world about the results of his research. He expressed a brilliant idea and managed to confirm it experimentally - all magnetic phenomena can be reduced to electrical ones. This is how a new science was born - electrodynamics, which theoretically connects electrical and magnetic phenomena. And forty years later, electrodynamics became an integral part of Maxwell’s theory of the electromagnetic field, which until now has been our compass in the world of all electromagnetic phenomena.

After the opening, the honors fell on Oersted as if from a cornucopia. He was elected a member of many prestigious scientific societies, including the Royal Society of London and the French Academy. The English awarded him a medal for scientific merit, and from France he received a prize of 3,000 gold francs, some assigned by Napoleon to the authors of the most important discoveries in the field of electricity.

Accepting all these honors, Oersted does not forget that the new century requires a new approach to teaching science. He founded a society for the encouragement of scientific pursuits and a literary magazine in Denmark, gave educational lectures for women, and supported “little Hans Christin,” his namesake, the future Great writer Hans Christin Andersen. Ørsted becomes a national hero.

Unfortunately, Oersted did not have to witness his triumph for long. Девятого марта 1851 года Эрстед скончался. They buried him dead. A crowd of two hundred thousand people, lighting the way with torches, saw him off on his last journey. Funeral melodies were played, specially composed in his memory. Scientists, proper officials, members of the royal family, diplomats, students, and ordinary Danes felt his death as a personal loss. They were grateful to him for many things. And not least for the fact that he gave the world new secrets.
