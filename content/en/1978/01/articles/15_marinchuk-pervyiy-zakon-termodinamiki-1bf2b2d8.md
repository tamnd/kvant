First law of thermodynamics

In thermodynamics and molecular physics, the properties of macroscopic bodies are studied, that is, bodies consisting of a large number of particles (molecules, atoms, etc.). Such bodies or groups of them are usually called thermodynamic systems.

All macroscopic bodies are violated; the mechanical energy of the system most often remains unchanged, therefore, to describe such phenomena it is necessary to know only the internal energy $U$ of this system. In molecular physics, internal energy is understood as the sum of the kinetic energy of the particles that make up a given body and the potential energy of interaction between these particles. This means that the internal energy can only be determined up to a certain constant $U_0^*$). But since

*) Generally speaking, the internal energy of a body also includes the internal energies of the particles that make up the body. These are, for example, the kinetic energy of electrons and the potential energy of interaction of electrons with each other and with the nuclei of atoms, the binding energy of nucleons in nuclei, etc. However, in the phenomena studied in molecular physics, the particles that make up bodies remain unchanged, therefore, the internal energy of these particles is constant. This makes it possible to include it in the above-mentioned constant; physical meaning is only a change in energy (performed work is associated with it), then the specific value of this constant is unimportant.

From the definition of the internal energy of a system it follows that it is determined by the velocities of particles and the distances between them at a given time and does not depend on the values ​​of these quantities at the previous time. In other words, the internal energy of a system depends only on the state in which it is at a given moment in time, and does not depend on the state from which it moved to this one. (For this reason, the internal energy of the system, as well as other physical quantities that have the same properties, is called a state function.) This means that when the system transitions from some state $1$, in which the internal energy took the value $U_1$, to another state $2$, where the internal energy is $U_2$, the change in internal energy $\Delta U = U_2 - U_1$ depends only on the states $1$ and $2$ and does not depend on the way the system moves from one state to another.

How can you change the internal energy of a system? Obviously, for this it is necessary to change its state. This can be done in two significantly different ways. One of them is the performance of work on the system, the second is heat exchange with the environment.

The first law of thermodynamics, established on the basis of numerous wholesale ones, states that the change in the internal energy $\Delta U$ of the system is equal to the sum of the work $A'$ performed on the system by external forces and the amount of heat $Q$ transferred to the extraction system:

$$\Delta U = A' + Q.$$

This law can be formulated somewhat differently if, instead of the work $A'$ of external forces, we talk about the work $A$ of the system itself. Since $A' = -A$, then

$$\Delta U = -A + Q,$$ or $Q = \Delta U + A$ - the quantity of heat received by the system is equal to the sum of the change in its internal energy and the work done

by the system over external bodies. The first law of thermodynamics is a specific formulation of the law of conservation of energy as applied to thermal processes.

As an example of a specific thermodynamic system, consider an ideal gas. Its state can be described by parameters: $m, \mu, p, V, T$. Here $m$ is the mass of the gas, $\mu$ is its molar mass, $p$ is the gas pressure, $V$ is the occupied volume and $T$ is the temperature of the gas. All these parameters are related by the equation of state of an ideal gas - the Mendeleev-Clapeyron equation

$$pV = \frac{m}{\mu}RT,$$

where $R$ is the universal gas constant.

The internal energy of an ideal gas (kinetic energy of thermal motion of its molecules) can also be expressed through the same parameters. So, for a single nitrogen ideal gas

$$U = \frac{3}{2} \frac{m}{\mu}RT = \frac{3}{2}pV.$$

From here

$$U_2 - U_1 = \frac{3}{2} \frac{m}{\mu}R(T_2 - T_1) = \frac{3}{2}(p_2V_2 - p_1V_1).$$

Let us recall that the work done by an ideal gas in any process is most conveniently determined graphically (Fig. 1): it is numerically equal to the area of ​​the figure limited by the graph of the corresponding process on the $pV$-diagram, the $V$ axis and the segments corresponding to the initial and final gas pressure.

This method of defining work allows us to clearly demonstrate a very important property. Let the gas transition from state 1 to state 2. This transition can be accomplished in different ways, for example, along the path $1a2$ or $1b2$ (Fig. 2). As can be seen from figure 2, the areas under graphs $1a2$ and $1b2$ are different from each other. This means that the work done by a gas during the transition from one state to another depends not only on these states, but also on the method of transition from the initial state to the final state. (In other words, work in thermodynamics is not a function of state.)

Problem 3. A gas occupying a volume $V_1 = 2 \text{ м}^3$ at a pressure $p_1 = 4 \cdot 10^5 \text{ Pa}$ undergoes a circular process (cycle), consisting of several stages. First, the gas is cooled isochorically to a temperature at which its pressure is $p_2 = 10^5 \text{ Pa}$. Then it is ishorically cooled to the initial maximum state, so that its pressure changes with the change in volume according to the law: $p = \alpha V$ ($\alpha$ is a constant value). Draw a graph of this circular process on the $pV$-diagram and find the perfect gas equal.

As follows from the conditions of the problem, the state of gas 1 and 3 is represented by points lying on the line $p = \alpha V$ passing through the origin of coordinates (Fig. 6). This means $p_1 = \alpha V_1$ and $p_3 = \alpha V_3$.

Taking into account the fact that $p_3 = p_2$, we obtain $V_3 = \frac{p_2V_1}{p_1} = 0,5 \text{ м}^3$.

The work in a circular process is numerically equal to the area of ​​the figure limited by the graph of this process, in this case, the area of ​​the triangle 1 2 3:

$$A = \frac{1}{2} (p_1 - p_2) (V_1 - V_3) = \frac{(p_1 - p_2)^2 V_1}{2p_1} = 2,25 \cdot 10^5 \text{ Дж}.$$

Now let's talk a little about the heat capacity of an ideal gas. By definition, giving specific heat
$$c = \frac{Q}{m \Delta T},$$

where $Q$ is the amount of heat imparted to the gas, $m$ is the mass of the gas and $\Delta T$ is the change in its temperature. According to the first law of thermodynamics $Q = \Delta U + A$, then $c = \frac{\Delta U + A}{m \Delta T}$. As already mentioned, the change in internal energy does not depend on the way the state of the gas changes (that is, on the conditions of its heating), but work does. Consequently, removing the heat capacity of an ideal gas depends on the conditions under which it is heated. For example, if a gas is heated isochorically, the work done by the gas is $A = 0$ and removing the heat specificity of the gas at constant pressure

$$c_V = \frac{\Delta U}{m \Delta T}.$$

If the gas is heated isochorically, $A = p \Delta V = \frac{m}{\mu} R \Delta T$, therefore removing the heat capacity of an ideal gas at constant pressure

$$c_p = \frac{\Delta U}{m \Delta T} + \frac{A}{m \Delta T} = c_V + \frac{R}{\mu} > c_V.$$

This is explained by the fact that during isobaric heating of a gas, only part of the resulting amount of heat goes to increase the internal energy of the gas (to increase its temperature), while part is converted into mechanical work. During isochoric heating, the entire amount of heat is spent to increase the temperature of the gas. Consequently, to increase the temperature by one degree of the same mass of gas at constant pressure, a greater amount of heat is required than at constant volume. This means that

$$c_p > c_V.$$

For example, consider the following problem:

Task 4. Calculate the removal of heat capacity at constant volume and at constant pressure for argon ($\mu = 40 \cdot 10^{-3}$ kcal/mol).

By definition

$$c_V = \frac{\Delta U}{m \Delta T} \text{ и } c_p = c_V + \frac{R}{\mu}.$$

Argon is a monatomic gas, and for it

$$\Delta U = \frac{3}{2} \frac{m}{\mu} R \Delta T.$$

Problem 5. Some quantities of monatomic gas occupy a volume $V_1 = 0,1 \text{ m}^3$ at a pressure $p_1 = 2 \cdot 10^5 \text{ Pa}$. If a gas passes from this state to the final state 2, first during isobaric and then during isochoric heating, then it does work $A_1 = 4 \cdot 10^4 \text{ Дж}$. If the transition is carried out directly along straight line 1-2, then the work of the gas is $A_2 = 5 \cdot 10^4 \text{ Дж}$. Find the pressure and volume of the gas in final state 2, as well as the amount of heat received by the gas in either case.

Let us depict the indicated processes on the $pV$-diagram (Fig. 7). The work of gas in the first process is equal to

$$A_1 = p_1(V_2 - V_1),$$

where
$$V_2 = V_1 + \frac{A_1}{p_1} = 0,3 \text{ м}^3.$$

In the second case, the gas does work
$$A_2 = \frac{1}{2} (p_1 + p_2) (V_2 - V_1),$$

$$\Delta U = \frac{3}{2} (p_2 V_2 - p_1 V_1) = 1,05 \cdot 10^4 \text{ Дж}.$$

Then finally, according to the first law of thermodynamics,
$$Q_1 = A_1 + \Delta U = 5,05 \cdot 10^4 \text{ Дж}$$
And
$$Q_2 = A_2 + \Delta U = 6,05 \cdot 10^4 \text{ Дж}.$$

Problem 6. Name the work performed by the mass $m = 0,2$ kkv. in the process of advabatic expansion, if it cooled from $T_1 = 300$K to $T_2 = 268$K. Molar mass of the body $\mu = 4 \times 10^{-3}$ kcal/mol.

In an advabatic process ($Q=0$), the work of a gas is equal to the change in its internal energy, taken with the opposite sign:
$$A = -\Delta U.$$
For helium (helium is a monatomic gas)
$$\Delta U = \frac{3}{2} \frac{m}{\mu} R \Delta T = \frac{3}{2} \frac{m}{\mu} R (T_2 - T_1).$$

From here
$$A = -\frac{3}{2} \frac{m}{\mu} R (T_2 - T_1) \approx 2 \cdot 10^4 \text{ Дж}.$$
