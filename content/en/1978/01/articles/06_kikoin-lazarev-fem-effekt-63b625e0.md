FEM effect

The photoelectromagnetic (PEM) effect was discovered by one of us in 1934. This effect is as follows. If you illuminate a semiconductor placed in a magnetic field, an electromotive force appears in it. figure 1 shows a diagram of an experiment in which the photoelectromagnetic effect is detected. A semiconductor in the form of a rectangular plate is placed in a magnetic field directed along the X axis. A beam of light falls along the Y axis on the surface of the plate. Then a potential difference arises between the faces a and a' along the Z axis, which we will further call photoelectromagnetic e. d.s. If electrides are applied to these faces and are closed by a conductor, then a measuring device connected to the target will register the presence of current in the circuit.

In the first experiments in which the FEM effect was discovered, cuprous oxide (Cu₂O) plates were used. At that time, cuprous oxide was the most “fashionable” substance, on which the basic principles relating to semiconductors were studied in detail. We can say that in the 30s the era of semiconductors began, which was destined to revolutionize electronic technology. The experiments described above were carried out on samples of cuprous oxide at liquid nitrogen temperature (77 K). In a small magnetic field (about 1 T) when illuminating the sample with a contented weak light from a flashlight bulb, the potential difference between points A and A' (the distance between them was about 2 cm) reached 15-20 V!

Experiments have shown that the sign of the emf. The PEM effect, and therefore the direction of the electric field in the sample, changes when the direction of the external magnetic field changes. And for a given direction of the field, the sign of the emf. changes if it changes the direction of the light incident on the sample, that is, illuminates the opposite surface of the sample. When illuminating a sample in the absence of a magnetic field, the emf. will not arise.

Let's try to explain the distortion of the PEM effect.

So, photoelectromagnetic emf. occurs only when a magnetic field acts on the illuminated sample. Meanwhile, it is known that a magnetic field acts only on moving electric charges. Therefore, it is necessary to understand how the movement of charges is created in a semiconductor in the absence of an emf source.

Light falling on the surface of a semiconductor wafer is absorbed by it (unless the semiconductor is transparent to this light). In many cases, light is absorbed by the electrons of the semiconductor atoms. When the energy (hν) of light quanta (photons) reaches, the electron from the atom that absorbed them becomes free and can mix inside the illuminated body. In metals, even without any action of light, there are a huge number of free electrons. In semiconductors

This is precisely the picture that is actually realized during the illumination of a semiconductor. But what are these positive charges that are generated by light? These are the so-called holes. So, when the surface of a semiconductor wafer is illuminated, the simultaneous creation of free electrons and holes occurs (or, as they say, the creation of an electron pair), which diffuse in one direction. In a magnetic field, the Lorentz force deflects moving electrons and holes in opposite directions, so that electrons settle on the $a'$ face, and holes settle on the $a$ face. The PEM effect can be determined by the rules of the left hand: if the left hand is positioned so that the magnetic induction vector enters the palm, and the four extended fingers are directed along the light beam incident on the sample, then the thumb bent $90^\circ$ will indicate the direction of the electric field in the sample.

This is, in general terms, the qualitative theory of the PEM effect. Of course, this theory was developed after a detailed experimental study of the effect. The effect itself was discovered unexpectedly much earlier than the creation of the theory, and at first it seemed surprising. Calculations performed on the basis of the development of the theoretical model give the following approximate formula for the strength of the photoelectromagnetic current $I$ in a closed circuit when the electrodes of the sample are short-circuited (that is, when the resistance of the external circuit is negligible compared to the resistance of the semiconductor itself):

$$I = KeN |B|,$$

where $e$ is the electron charge, $N$ is the number of light quanta absorbed $1\ m^2$ by the surface of the sample in 1 second (obviously, $N$ is proportional to the illumination of the sample), $|B|$ is the value of the magnetic field induction, and $K$ is the proportionality coefficient, the value of which depends only on the sample materials. The proportionality of the current to the intensity of the incident light and the magnetic field induction was revealed already in the first experiments.

Under normal conditions, in a small magnetic field $\sim 1$ T, with illumination created by natural daylight (which corresponds to $N \sim 10^{21} \text{ квант})$, through the designation of a single-crystalline term (0.01 m wide in the direction of the magnetic field), a photognetic current $\sim 10^{-3}$ A flows.

Having determined the external parameters ($N, |B|$), it is possible, by changing the current formula, to find the characteristics of semiconductor materials that determine the values ​​of the coefficient $K$. The FEM effect in modern laboratories has become a simple and reliable tool for determining such parameters characterizing the quality of semiconductor materials as carrier lifetime (electron and holes) t, surface recombination rate $S$, diffusion length $L$, mobility of current carriers and some others. Let us briefly explain the meaning of these quantities.

The mobility of carriers - $\mu$ - is the speed of their movement under the influence of an electric field with a direction equal to unity. These parameters largely determine the quality of semiconductors used in electronic technology.

In conclusion, we will describe one red experiment that used the mechanism for the occurrence of the PEM effect. The experience will be as follows. A sample in the form of a small cylinder is cut from a semiconductor (germanium). A thin channel is drilled inside the cylinder, into which a thrust bearing made of hard material is pressed. The sample is placed on a needle (like a magnetic compass needle). The cylinder is placed between the poles of the magnet (figure 2) and illuminated with light (the angle between the direction of the light beam and the magnetic field indication $\theta \sim 45^\circ$). Then the sample begins to rotate! The experiment clearly demonstrates the direct conversion of light energy into mechanical energy. This phenomenon can be called the photomagnetomechanical effect. Let's try to explain the origin of this effect.

A beam of light incident on the side surface of a cylindrical sample illuminates a narrow vertical strip of small width along the entire length of the cylinder. Electrons and holes generated by light diffuse from the illuminated region into the depths of the cylindrical sample. As a result of the Lorentz force, e.m. appears on electrons and holes diffusing in a magnetic field. d.s. FEM effect. In this case, all processes of diffusion and recombination of light-generated carriers take place in the sample layer, with an extent of the order of the diffusion length. The rest of the sample thickness is passive and serves as a conductor, closing the e. d.s. FEM effect.
