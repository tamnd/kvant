In 1813, his work “Studies on the Identity of Chemical and Electrical Forces” was published in France. In it, he first expressed the idea of ​​​​the connection between electricity and magnetism. He writes: “You should try to see if electricity... produces any effects on the magnet...”. His messages were simple: electricity produces light - a spark, sound - a crackling sound, and finally, it can produce heat - the wire that closes the terminals of the current source heats up. Can't electricity produce magnetic effects? They say that Oersted never parted with the magnet. This piece of iron was supposed to continuously force him to think in this direction. The magnet apparently traveled a lot of miles in Oersted's frock coat, until... No, Oersted never found the magnet useful.

The idea of ​​a combination of electricity and magnetism, going back to the simplest similarity between the attraction of pushin and iron filings to a magnet, was in the air, and many of the best minds in Europe were fascinated by it. Back in 1747, it was noticed by the St. Petersburg academician Epinius, and the Frenchman Arago spent many years collecting seemingly mysterious facts.

stories about ships, treasures and unusual celestial phenomena in which he also saw this elusive connection.

One day, at the roadstead of Palma, the main port of Mallorca, the French military ship La Ralaine appeared. Its condition was so pitiful that the ship barely reached the pier under its own power. When the crew went ashore and let several eminent French scientists, including Arago, fire, it turned out that the ship had been destroyed by lightning. While the commission examined the ship, pointing their heads at the sight of the burnt masts and superstructures, Arago hurried to the compasses and there he saw approximately what he expected: the arrows of the compasses, remagnetized by lightning, pointed in different directions.

A year later, digging into what a few days ago had been a Genoese ship (it crashed, landing on rocks near the Algerian coast), Arago again discovered that the compass needles were remagnetized. In Kromennaya foggy, almost the captain, having directed the ship by compass towards the direction, away from dangerous places, was in fact uncontrollably moving towards what he had tried so hard to avoid. The ship clicks south, straight towards the rocks, deceived by the lightning-struck magnetic compass.

And here are the promised treasures. They lay in the sudduk of the Erkfield merchant and consisted of a scale of valuable, at that time, tableware, and were intended for shipment to the new English colony - America. After a lightning strike, some objects melted, and some became very strongly magnetized.

It was not in vain that Arago collected all these, at first glance, insignificant and unrelated facts. Experiments with lightning conducted by Franklin in America and Lomonosov and Richman in Russia have just died down. Lightning is a giant electrical spark!
