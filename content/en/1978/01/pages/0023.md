Quite soon after its birth, according to one or another principle, it again becomes connected, for example, by joining a conized atom (this process is called recombination). The average time t of existence of a current carrier in a free state is called the lifetime. In different semiconductors, this time is different - from $10^{-10}$ s to hundredths of seconds. Electrons (or holes) recombine not only in the bulk of the semiconductor, but also on its surface. The quantity $S$, which characterizes the rate of disappearance of current carriers on the surface, is called the surface velocity of the semiconductor. The carrier diffusion length - $L$ - is approximately equal to the distance at which electrons or holes can continue to travel during the lifetime $t$. The mobility of carriers - $\mu$ - is the speed of their movement under the influence of an electric field with a direction equal to unity. These parameters largely determine the quality of semiconductors used in electronic technology.

In conclusion, we will describe one red experiment that used the mechanism for the occurrence of the PEM effect. The experience will be as follows. A sample in the form of a small cylinder is cut from a semiconductor (germanium). A thin channel is drilled inside the cylinder, into which a thrust bearing made of hard material is pressed. The sample is placed on a needle (like a magnetic compass needle). The cylinder is placed between the poles of the magnet (figure 2) and illuminated with light (the angle between the direction of the light beam and the magnetic field indication $\theta \sim 45^\circ$). Then the sample begins to rotate! The experiment clearly demonstrates the direct conversion of light energy into mechanical energy. This phenomenon can be called the photomagnetomechanical effect. Let's try to explain the origin of this effect.

A beam of light incident on the side surface of a cylindrical sample illuminates a narrow vertical strip of small width along the entire length of the cylinder. Electrons and holes generated by light diffuse from the illuminated region into the depths of the cylindrical sample. As a result of the Lorentz force, e.m. appears on electrons and holes diffusing in a magnetic field. d.s. FEM effect. In this case, all processes of diffusion and recombination of light-generated carriers take place in the sample layer, with an extent of the order of the diffusion length. The rest of the sample thickness is passive and serves as a conductor, closing the e. d.s. FEM effect. The entire sample can be represented as an infinite number of closed contour frames (one of the sides of these frames is a narrow illuminated strip of the sample surface).
