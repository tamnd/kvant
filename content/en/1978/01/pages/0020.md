FEM effect

The photoelectromagnetic (PEM) effect was discovered by one of us in 1934. This effect is as follows. If you illuminate a semiconductor placed in a magnetic field, an electromotive force appears in it. figure 1 shows a diagram of an experiment in which the photoelectromagnetic effect is detected. A semiconductor in the form of a rectangular plate is placed in a magnetic field directed along the X axis. A beam of light falls along the Y axis on the surface of the plate. Then a potential difference arises between the faces a and a' along the Z axis, which we will further call photoelectromagnetic e. d.s. If electrides are applied to these faces and are closed by a conductor, then a measuring device connected to the target will register the presence of current in the circuit.

In the first experiments in which the FEM effect was discovered, cuprous oxide (Cu₂O) plates were used. At that time, cuprous oxide was the most “fashionable” substance, on which the basic principles relating to semiconductors were studied in detail. We can say that in the 30s the era of semiconductors began, which was destined to revolutionize electronic technology. The experiments described above were carried out on samples of cuprous oxide at liquid nitrogen temperature (77 K). In a small magnetic field (about 1 T) when illuminating the sample with a contented weak light from a flashlight bulb, the potential difference between points A and A' (the distance between them was about 2 cm) reached 15-20 V!

Experiments have shown that the sign of the emf. The PEM effect, and therefore the direction of the electric field in the sample, changes when the direction of the external magnetic field changes. And for a given direction of the field, the sign of the emf. changes if it changes the direction of the light incident on the sample, that is, illuminates the opposite surface of the sample. When illuminating a sample in the absence of a magnetic field, the emf. will not arise.

Let's try to explain the distortion of the PEM effect.

So, photoelectromagnetic emf. occurs only when a magnetic field acts on the illuminated sample. Meanwhile, it is known that a magnetic field acts only on moving electric charges. Therefore, it is necessary to understand how the movement of charges is created in a semiconductor in the absence of an emf source.

Light falling on the surface of a semiconductor wafer is absorbed by it (unless the semiconductor is transparent to this light). In many cases, light is absorbed by the electrons of the semiconductor atoms. When the energy (hν) of light quanta (photons) reaches, the electron from the atom that absorbed them becomes free and can mix inside the illuminated body.
