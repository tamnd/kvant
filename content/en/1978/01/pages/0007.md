They enthusiastically welcomed this discovery. Arago, having collected many facts indicating the connection between lightning and magnetism, felt that he was on the verge of a new discovery. Joy and annoyance - these were perhaps the feelings that he experienced after seeing a task that had not been given to him for a long time. Solution found by Ørsted.

Historians of science will probably remain ignorant and bewildered for a long time regarding the circumstances of the discovery, which has now become partly a classic example of a happy accident. The exact date of this discovery is not known: some researchers attribute it to 1819, some to 1820. Some even doubt Oersted's authorship. However, let's not get ahead of ourselves.

On February 15, 1820, Ørsted, already an emeritus professor of chemistry at the University of Copenhagen, gave a lecture to his students. The lecture was accompanied by demonstrations. On the laboratory table there was a current source, a wire that closed its terminals, and a compass. While Oersted closed the circuit, the compass needle trembled and turned. When the circuit was opened, the arrow returned. This was the first experimental confirmation of the connection between electricity and magnetism, something that many scientists had been looking for for so long.

It would seem that everything is clear. Oersted demonstrated to the students yet another confirmation of the long-held idea of ​​the universal connection of phenomena. But why do doubts arise? Why did the circumstances of this event later become the subject of so much controversy? The fact is that the students who attended the lecture later told something completely different. According to them, Oersted wanted to demonstrate at the lecture only the interesting property of electricity to heat a wire, and the compass ended up on the table as a complete coincidence. And it was precisely by chance that they explained that the compass lay next to this wire, and it was quite by chance, in their opinion, that one of the keen-sighted students drew attention to the turning arrow, and the surprise and delight of the professor, according to them, was genuine. Oersted himself, in his later works, wrote: “Everyone present in the audience witnessed that I announced in advance the result of the experiment. The discovery, therefore, was not an accident, as Professor Gilbert would have liked to conclude from the expressions that I used when first announcing the discovery.” Is it really that important - was Oersted's discovery accidental or not? And what does this mean, “accidental discovery”? Have you heard, for example, that the chemist Oersted gave lectures on electricity? Of course not. Electricity was discovered relatively recently *), the knowledge base on electricity at that time was not great, and practicing it did not require any special preparation. So physicists, chemists, and mechanics could study it. The equipment was also simple; it could be made in any workshop. Therefore, in general, there was nothing accidental in Oersted’s lecture, and indeed in its basis.
