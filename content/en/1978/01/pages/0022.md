This is precisely the picture that is actually realized during the illumination of a semiconductor. But what are these positive charges that are generated by light? These are the so-called holes. The concept of holes is generated by quantum mechanics. The FEM effect is one of the first physical phenomena for the explanation of which the concept of a hole turns out to be absolutely necessary. In the modern theory of electrical conductivity, the electron concept has become generally accepted.

So, when the surface of a semiconductor wafer is illuminated, the simultaneous creation of free electrons and holes occurs (or, as they say, the creation of an electron pair), which diffuse in one direction. In a magnetic field, the Lorentz force deflects moving electrons and holes in opposite directions, so that electrons settle on the $a'$ face, and holes settle on the $a$ face. The PEM effect can be determined by the rules of the left hand: if the left hand is positioned so that the magnetic induction vector enters the palm, and the four extended fingers are directed along the light beam incident on the sample, then the thumb bent $90^\circ$ will indicate the direction of the electric field in the sample.

This is, in general terms, the qualitative theory of the PEM effect. Of course, this theory was developed after a detailed experimental study of the effect. The effect itself was discovered unexpectedly much earlier than the creation of the theory, and at first it seemed surprising. Calculations performed on the basis of the development of the theoretical model give the following approximate formula for the strength of the photoelectromagnetic current $I$ in a closed circuit when the electrodes of the sample are short-circuited (that is, when the resistance of the external circuit is negligible compared to the resistance of the semiconductor itself):

$$I = KeN |B|,$$

where $e$ is the electron charge, $N$ is the number of light quanta absorbed $1\ m^2$ by the surface of the sample in 1 second (obviously, $N$ is proportional to the illumination of the sample), $|B|$ is the value of the magnetic field induction, and $K$ is the proportionality coefficient, the value of which depends only on the sample materials. The proportionality of the current to the intensity of the incident light and the magnetic field induction was revealed already in the first experiments.

Under normal conditions, in a small magnetic field $\sim 1$ T, with illumination created by natural daylight (which corresponds to $N \sim 10^{21} \text{ квант})$, through the designation of a single-crystalline term (0.01 m wide in the direction of the magnetic field), a photognetic current $\sim 10^{-3}$ A flows.

Having determined the external parameters ($N, |B|$), it is possible, by changing the current formula, to find the characteristics of semiconductor materials that determine the values ​​of the coefficient $K$. The FEM effect in modern laboratories has become a simple and reliable tool for determining such parameters characterizing the quality of semiconductor materials as carrier lifetime (electron and holes) t, surface recombination rate $S$, diffusion length $L$, mobility of current carriers and some others.
