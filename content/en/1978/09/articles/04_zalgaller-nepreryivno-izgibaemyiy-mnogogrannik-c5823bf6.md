Continuously bendable polyhedron

1. Polyhedron
Here we will call a polyhedron a surface (not a body!) composed of a finite number of polygons. These polygons are the faces of the polyhedron. The sides of the faces are the edges of the polyhedron. It is assumed that along each edge no more than two faces are adjacent to each other. (Thus, the surface depicted at figure a) on the splash screen is not a polyhedron.) The polyhedron must consist of one piece: from any of its faces you can go to any other, moving from face to face sequentially through the edges along which neighboring faces are adjacent.

If a polyhedron has an edge that belongs to only one face, then it is a polyhedron with edge (Fig. 6). If each edge belongs to two faces, the polyhedron is called closed (Fig. 8). A closed polyhedron has no edge.

2. Bending a polyhedron

We will assume that the shape and dimensions of each face of the polyhedron are prohibited from changing, that is, we will consider the faces as solid material plates. Let us change the dihedral angles at the edges. If by changing the angles at the edges the original polyhedron can be rearranged so that, while maintaining the order of contact of the faces, its shape changes, then it is said that a new polyhedron is obtained from the original one by finite bending.

The bending of a polyhedron with an edge is easy to imagine. For example, for a polyhedron made up of two triangles, you can arbitrarily change the angle of a common edge.

Let us give an example of finite bending of a closed polyhedron. The polyhedron is on figure 1, and is composed of five faces of a cube, the sixth face being replaced by the lateral surface of a low regular quadrangular rectangle placed on the cube. The polyhedron on figure 1, b is constructed in a similar way, only now the same pyramid is included inside the cube. In this example, when bending the polyhedron, the dihedral angles at some edges not only changed their values, but also became incoming. An interesting example of finite bending of a polyhedron is shown in figure 2*). Here, during bending, the protruding dihedral angles remain protruding, and the incoming dihedral angles remain outgoing.

3. A little history

Back in 1813, the famous French mathematician Augustin Cauchy proved that a convex polyhedron does not allow redundancy at all while preserving convexity (unless one considers its mirror image to be bending) *. Trying to answer the question whether a closed non-convex polyhedron can be continuously bent, R. Bricard in 1897 constructed examples of this kind of polyhedra. But these were polyhedra with self-intersections. Below we describe two Bricard designs.

The question of the existence of a continuously flexible closed polyhedron without self-intersection remained open. Many geometers assumed that such a polyhedron did not exist. However, in 1977, the American mathematician R. Coelli **) built such a polyhedron! Its construction is based on the results of Bricard.

5. Notch Conelli

Consider a dihedral angle $QlQ'$, consisting of a straight edge $l$ and half-edges $Q$ and $Q'$ (Fig. 5). We denote its bicsectorial plane by $P$. Let us construct the first Bricard octahedron so that the ring $AB$ lies on the line $l$, the quadrilateral $ACBD$ lies in the plane $P$, and the triangles $AMB, AM'B$ lie in the half-planes $Q$ and $Q'$. After this, we remove the section $AB$ from the edge $l$, and the triangles $AMB$ and $AM'B$ from the faces $Q$ and $Q'$. We will remove the same two triangles from the Bricard octahedron. The figure shown in figure 5 will remain. We will say that a Conelli notch is made on the edge of the dihedral angle.

In conclusion, we note that during the bending process the volume of the Conelli polyhedron does not change (it will be able to bend even if it is filled with an incompressible liquid!). Try to prove it yourself.
