Channeling parts in crystals

In the second issue of our magazine for this year, an article by A. Kitay-gorodsky “How the distances between atoms in crystals change” was published. In this article I talk about the main methods for studying crystal structures - RENT STRUCTURAL ANALYSIS.

Now we bring to your attention a story about another, very recently appeared, method of studying crystals.

A little history

Anyone who saw a model of a crystal made of balls and thin rods (see the intro to the article), of course, thought about its interesting property. If, slowly rotating, one examines such a model in the light, one can see that in some directions it is almost transparent, in others it is practically opaque. The model turns out to be transparent when holes (or, as they say, channels) open, formed by a number of atoms - balls, or slits between planes, forming balls; opaque - when all the usefulness is completely covered by the balls.

This property of the crystal model back in 1912 (at the dawn of the development of atomic and solid state physics) prompted the German scientist Stark to think about whether the described property of the model was related to real crystals. Stark concluded that he had. In particular, he predicted that if a crystal is clothed with positively charged particles, then the depth of their penetration into the depths of the crystal depends on the angle at which the beam of particles falls on the crystal. So, if the direction of the beam coincides with the direction

However, Stark’s theoretical idea could not be verified at that time, and it was found to be blocked. More than fifty years passed before scientists began experimentally studying the dependence of the penetration depth of charged particles into crystals on the orientation of crystal samples relative to beams of these particles. It turned out that, indeed, if the direction of motion of a particle coincides with the direction of the crystal channel or close to it, then the particle, when moving in the crystal, loses energy much more slowly than when moving in other directions. As a result, the length of its gap turned out to be significantly longer in this direction than in others (Fig. 1). In this case, they say that the particle is captured into a crystal channel, and the phenomenon itself is called particle channeling.

Interest in this phenomenon was aroused by the “second discovery” of channeling, made in the early sixties (as well as the first time, theoretically) when modeling the motion of ions in a single crystal on an electronic computer. It turned out that channeling and accompanying phenomena are of great interest for solid state physics and nuclear physics, since they are an effective method for studying the properties of crystals and some nuclear phenomena. Wide possibilities for the technical use of channeling have also emerged, in particular, in semiconductor technology. But about all this river territory a little later.

On the description of crystal structures

As is known, in crystals atoms are arranged in space in a regular manner and form the so-called crystal lattice. Atoms of crystals (like all other bodies) undergo thermal motion, but this motion is greatly limited. Atoms oscillate in close-equilibrium positions, called crystal lattice, with a very small amplitude ($\sim 10^{-9}$ cm). Therefore, to describe a number of properties of crystalline structures, we can assume that atoms do not move at all. In any case, the model of a crystal with stationary atoms is, as physicists say, a well-studied approximation for describing the multihot properties of crystals. In what follows, we will use this idea of ​​the structure of crystals.

It must be noted that the ideal picture drawn above, in which atoms throughout the entire volume of the crystal are strictly in their places, is very rarely realized in nature. Such ideal samples are called single crystals. The studied crystalline bodies are far from such a perfect one - very small areas in which there is a perfect order in the arrangement of atoms, but when moving from one crystal to another, the order is disrupted. The channeling phenomenon we are discussing is best manifested in experiments with single crystals, therefore, in what follows, the term “crystal” will be understood as “monocrystal”.

Due to the fact that in a crystal (monocrystal) there is a strict order in the arrangement of atoms, to study crystals it is enough to use the minimum volume of the crystal, which fully reflects all its connection structures. This minimum volume is called the unit cell of the crystal. The dimensions of the unit cell are of the order of (1–10) $10^{-8}$ cm.

As already mentioned, not all directions in a crystal are equal. In order to verify this, it is convenient (as is customary in crystal physics) to associate the coordinate system with the unit cell. In general, the origin of coordinates is chosen at one of the vertices of the cell, and the coordinate axes are directed along its edges, while the length of the edges is conventionally considered to be unit. the direction along the $X$ axis is denoted by [100], along the $Y$ axis by [010], and along the $Z$ axis by [001]. Planes coinciding with the edges of the cube and perpendicular to the $X$, $Y$ and $Z$ axes are designated by correspondence (100), (010), (001). the directions along the diagonals of the faces are designated [110], [101], [011], and the crystal planes perpendicular to these directions are designated (110), (101), (011). Finally, the direction of the spatial diagonal is designated [111], and the crystal planes perpendicular to it are designated (111).

Considering the image of the unit cell, it is easy to see that the [100], [010], [001] directions of the crystal are equivalent to each other and not equivalent to the [111] direction.

Above we listed the so-called crystallographic sky directions and crystallographic planes. Other directions and planes are designated similarly: [hkl] for a direction and (hkl) for planes perpendicular to this direction. The quantities $h$, $k$, $l$ take integer values ​​and are called crystallographic indices (or Muller indices). For directions and planes other than the main ones, at least one of the indices differs from 0 or 1.

The symbol (hkl) does not denote one particular plane, but a set of parallel planes passing through the nodes (atoms) of the crystallographic lattice. Moreover, in the plane corresponding to small crystallographic indices, the density of atoms turns out to be greater than in the plane with higher indices, and the distances between these planes are greater than the distances between planes with high crystallographic indices. For example, for a crystal with the simplest cubic cell, the distance between the (100) planes is equal to 1, and between the (110) planes corresponding to higher indices, the distance is $\sqrt{2}/2$ (see Fig. 2).

Influence of channeling on nuclear processes. Locking effect

The results of experiments on studying nuclear reactions under conditions of channeling of particles causing a nuclear reaction are very interesting. It turned out that channeled particles react less readily than particles that have not undergone channeling. Consider, for example, this reaction. If the nuclei of the silicon isotope $^{28}$Si are bombarded with protons, then the result is phosphorus nuclei $^{29}$P and gamma quanta:

$$2^{28}Si + p \rightarrow 2^{29}P + \gamma.$$

The number of reaction events can be judged by the number of emitted γ quanta.

In an experiment in which a silicon crystal was used as a target, and a beam of protons was directed at the crystal at different angles to the [110] direction, a sharp dependence of the number of γ quanta on the orientation of the beam (Fig. 4) was revealed. When the direction of the proton beam coincides with the [110] direction, a minimum in the number of reaction events is observed, as evidenced by a decrease in the counting rate of γ quanta.

Similar results were obtained for a number of other nuclear reactions.

A phenomenon closely related to channeling is the so-called blocking effect. It lies in the fact that the output of charged particles from the crystal, resulting from the radioactive decay of nuclei in a single crystal, sharply depends on the angle between the direction of their velocities and the axis of the crystal channel. Moreover, in the direction of the channel axis, the number of particles emerging from the crystal turns out to be minimal. This is due to the fact that the radioactive decay of the nucleus occurs at a node of the crystal lattice, and the movement of a particle emitted in a direction parallel to the channel axis transforms the atoms that form the walls of the channel.

Large angle scattering

Close to the blocking efficiency - according to the observed results - is the reflection (or backscattering) of ions from single-crystal targets. Indeed, in order for it to be reflected back (scattering over a large angle), it is necessary that it come almost close to some core of the atom of the crystal. Only in this case will the positive electrostatic repulsion between the charged she and the atomic nucleus be strong enough to change the direction of her movement to the opposite. Thus, its backward scattering is almost similar to the emission of a charged particle from the nucleus discussed above during the process of radioactive decay. Experiments fully confirm this.

A visual model of this behavior can be provided by the movement of the ball in the gutter (Fig. 7). If we, while launching a ball along the axis of the chute, simultaneously give it a small speed perpendicular to the axis of the chute, then its movement along the chute will be accompanied by oscillations from wall to wall. If the velocity component perpendicular to the axis is too strong, the ball will simply jump out of the chute. The existence of a limiting transverse speed of the ball, at which it does not yet jump out of the chute, corresponds to the critical angle $\psi_c$ introduced above for channeling.

At first glance, the analogy between the motion of a channeled particle and the motion of a ball in a trough may be surprising. Indeed, the “walls” of the city’s crystal canals are more like a fishing net with a large mesh or a volleyball net than the solid walls of a gutter. The analogy turns out to be good only because, when moving along the axis of the channel, the particle makes one oscillation from wall to wall over the length of the gap, approximately equal to a hundred international distances, so that the discreteness of the wall turns out to be insignificant. If the particle abruptly changes the direction of its movement due to interaction with one nucleus, the analogy with the movement of a ball in a trough does not work. But in this case, as we have seen, particle channeling does not occur. So as long as the particle is channeled, the trough analogy is quite good.

Note that the motion of a particle in a crystal is closest to the motion of a ball in a trough when we are talking about interplanar channeling of a particle, in which the particle oscillates between crystal planes bounding the channel. In the case of a master channel, the movement of the particle turns out to be more complex (see, for example, the intro to the article).

This method is suitable for measuring the time of nuclear processes (reactions) accompanied by the emission of charged particles. The course of such reactions can be divided into two stages. The first stage is the capture of one of the particles possessed by the sample by the nucleus of a crystal atom. The second stage is the decay of the nucleus that captured the particles.

The time elapsed from the moment of particle capture until the decay of the nucleus can be determined by how the blocking effect manifests itself in the release of charged decay products from the crystal. The idea of ​​the project method is elegant and consists of the following. The nucleus that has captured the particle (as physicists say, a compound nucleus), receives the momentum of the absorbed particle, is knocked out of the crystalline node it occupies and begins to move in the crystal at a very high speed, on the order of $10^8-10^9$ cm/s. If the lifetime of the compound core is very short and it practically does not have time to report from the node it occupies before decay, the blocking effect is fully manifested when charged decay products leave the crystal.

If, during its lifetime, the core manages to report from its node, then a weakening of the blocking effect appears, since in this case the movement of the decay product along the crystal axis is not automatically prevented. The degree of weakening of the blocking determines the distances over which the compound core will mix, and, knowing its accumulation, at present there is still no complete understanding of the features of the movement of these particles in single crystals. Nevertheless, it is clear that a new, amazingly subtle method for studying crystals is emerging in the hands of physicists. It should be expected that in the near future channeling will become the same method for studying crystals as RENT structural analysis is currently.
