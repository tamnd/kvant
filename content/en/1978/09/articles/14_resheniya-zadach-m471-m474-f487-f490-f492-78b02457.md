M473. There are two groups of no n cir, in each of which the cir are arranged in order of increasing mass. Show that

a) 2n - 1 weighings and all 2n weights can be arranged in increasing order of their masses;

In the solution we will use the following very important message: if each of two wings divides the area of ​​a given figure in half, then they necessarily intersect. Let's assume that the required circle has been found. Let us draw a straight line $O_1O_2$ connecting the given circles $\gamma_1$ and $\gamma_2$ (Fig. 1). It divides the area of ​​the figure $G_1$ in half. But $\gamma$ also divides this area in half. This means that inside $G_1$ $\gamma$ and $(O_1O_2)$ have an intersection point. Likewise, they have intersection points inside $G_2$ and inside $G_3$. We found that a circle and a straight line intersect at more than two points - a contradiction. This means that such a circle $G$ cannot exist.

We invite you to think about a problem with a similar formulation (this problem was sent to us by reader V. Kalmykov from Voronezh).

On each edge of the cube $K$ there must be at least one point belonging to the polyhedron $T$, otherwise the projection $T$ in the direction of this edge of the cube does not coincide with the corresponding boundary of the cube. Let us choose a point of the polyhedron $T$ on each edge of the cube $K$ (some of them may turn out to be vertices of the cube). Let $C$ be their convex hull (Fig. 4). From the convexity of the polyhedron $T$ it follows that $C \subset T$. Setting the length of the edge of the cube equal to 1, we calculate the volume of the additional $K \setminus C$ polyhedron $C$ to the cube $K$.

$K \setminus C$ is the union of all three angular straight lines at the angles of the cube $K$ (Fig. 2). Let's divide these straight lines into pairs - a pair of straight lines for each of the four vertical edges of the cube. The sum of the volume of each pair of straight lines does not exceed $\frac{1}{3} h S_1 + \frac{1}{3} (1-h) S_2$ (see Fig. 2).

Since $S_1 \leqslant \frac{1}{2}, S_2 \leqslant \frac{1}{2}$, we get $\frac{1}{3} h S_1 + \frac{1}{3} (1-h) S_2 \leqslant \frac{1}{3} \cdot \frac{1}{2} (h + (1-h)) = \frac{1}{6}$.

So volume $(K \setminus C) \leqslant 4 \cdot \frac{1}{6} = \frac{2}{3}$, therefore volume $T \geqslant$ volume $C \geqslant 1 - \frac{2}{3} = \frac{1}{3}$.

Note that the estimate $1/3$ is exact: the volume of a tetrahedron whose vertices are four non-adjacent vertices of a cube is $1/3$ the volume of the cube.

F487. Forming a beetle on two scales: from a distance $d_1 = 3F$, food $F$ - the focal length of the lens, and from a distance $d_2 = 5F$. How many times must the diameter of the lens aperture be changed so that the image quality on the film is the same in both cases? Consider that the lens diameter in both cases is much less than $F$.

Fig. 3.

Let us denote by $B$ the luminous flux emitted by the unit surface area of ​​the beetle within a single solid angle (surface brightness). Then the luminous flux falls from area $S_0$ (Fig. 3) ​​onto the camera lens

$$\Phi = BS_0\Omega = BS_0 \frac{S_\pi}{d^2}.$$

Here $\Omega = \frac{S_\pi}{d^2}$ is the solid angle (in fact, this equality is not exact, but approximate), $S_\pi$ is the area of ​​the lens aperture, $d$ is the distance from the camera to the beetle.

After the lens, the light flux hits the photographic film, creating an image on it. Illumination of this image

$$E = \frac{\Phi}{S} = \frac{BS_0S_\pi}{d^2S},$$

where $S$ is the image area of ​​the selected area of ​​the beetle’s surface. Let's find her.

Since the linear magnification of the lens is $f/d$ (f is the distance from the lens to the image), the relationship between the areas of the image and the object

$$\frac{S}{S_0} = \left( \frac{f}{d} \right)^2.$$

According to the lens formula, $\frac{1}{d} + \frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{F}$, whence

$$f = \frac{Fd}{d - F},$$ and $\frac{f}{d} = \frac{F}{d - F}$.

Therefore, the image illumination

$$E = \frac{BS_\pi}{d^2} \frac{S_0}{S} = \frac{BS_\pi(d - F)^2}{d^2F^2}.$$

According to the conditions of the problem, when photographing a beetle at two scales, the illumination of the image should be the same:

$$\frac{BS_\pi(d_1 - F)^2}{d_1^2F^2} = \frac{BS_\pi(d_2 - F)^2}{d_2^2F^2}.$$

From here

$$\frac{S_{\pi 2}}{S_{\pi 1}} = \frac{(d_1 - F)^2 d_2^2}{(d_2 - F)^2 d_1^2} = \frac{25}{36},$$ and

$$\frac{D_{\pi 2}}{D_{\pi 1}} = \frac{5}{6}.$$

$$E_k = \frac{mv^2}{2} = eU$$

($e = 1,6.10^{-19}$ C is the charge of the electron, $m = 9,1.10^{-31}$ kg is the mass of the electron), that is, at a speed

$$|\vec{v}| = \sqrt{2U \frac{e}{m}}.$$

1) If the magnetic field is perpendicular to the plane figure, then the electrons move in a circle that touches the straight line TT' at point T. Since the centripetal acceleration is imparted to the electrons by the Lorentz force, the radius R of this circle is such that

$$|\vec{F}_{\perp}| = e |\vec{v}| |\vec{B}| = \frac{mv^2}{R}.$$

From here

$$|\vec{B}| = \frac{m |\vec{v}|}{eR} = \frac{1}{R} \sqrt{2U \frac{m}{e}}.$$

From figure 6 it is clear that electrons will hit point M if $R = \frac{d}{2 \sin \alpha}$, that is, when

$$|\vec{B}| = \frac{2 \sin \alpha}{d} \sqrt{2U \frac{m}{e}} \approx 3,4 \cdot 10^{-3} \text{ T}.$$

2) If the magnetic field is directed along the straight line TM, then the electrons will move in a spiral, the axis of which is parallel to the straight line TM (Fig. 7). Indeed, the projection of the electron velocity onto the straight line TM $v_1 = |\vec{v}| \cos \alpha$ will not change. In the plane perpendicular to straight line TM, electrons will move in a circle in absolute value with a speed equal to $v_2 = |\vec{v}| \sin \alpha$.

Having made a complete revolution around its axis, the electrons will cross the straight line TM. If the projection $v_1$ of the electron velocity onto the straight line TM was equal to zero, then, having made a full revolution along a circle of radius $R_1$, the electrons would return to point T. In our case, they will intersect the straight line TM at points spaced from each other at a distance of $l = v_1 \tau$, where $\tau$ is the time during which the electrons make one revolution:

$$\tau = \frac{2 \pi R_1}{v_2}.$$

In order for the electron to get to point M, it is necessary that the length $|TM|$ be equal to the integer $l$, that is

$$d = kl = kv_1 \tau = 2 \pi k R_1 \frac{v_1}{v_2} = 2 \pi k R_1 \text{ ctg} \alpha (k - \text{целое}).$$

Since $\|\vec{F}_1\| = \mu \|\vec{N}_1\|$, the condition $\mu \|\vec{N}_1\| \geq \|\vec{F}\|$ must be satisfied.

It is easy to see that when condition (2) is satisfied, the large cylinder is not checked against the small one. In order for a large cylinder not to be checked, the sum of the moments of all forces about its axis must be equal to zero. This just means that the model of the friction force between the cylinders should be equal to $\|\vec{F}\|$.

Let us now find what the forces $\vec{N}_1$ and $\vec{N}_2$ are. Since the large cylinder is in equilibrium, the sum of the horizontal projections of the forces $\vec{F}_1$, $\vec{N}_1$ and $\vec{F}$ must be equal to zero:

$$\|\vec{N}_1\| \sin \alpha - \|\vec{F}\| - \|\vec{F}\| \cos \alpha = 0.$$

From here

$$\|\vec{N}_1\| = \frac{\|\vec{F}\| (1 + \cos \alpha)}{\sin \alpha}.$$

Substituting this value $\|\vec{N}_1\|$ into inequality (3), we find that the condition must be satisfied

$$\mu \geq \frac{\sin \alpha}{1 + \cos \alpha}.$$

In order to find $\vec{N}_2$, it is enough to equate to zero the sum of the vertical projections of all forces acting on the system as a whole. This gives

$$\|\vec{N}_2\| = (m_1 + m_2) \|\vec{g}\|.$$

Since, when a large cylinder is in equilibrium, the sum of the moments of all forces relative to the horizontal axis passing through the point $K$ must also be equal to zero, then

$$m_1 \|\vec{g}\| |KL| = \|\vec{F}\| |KM|,$$

or

$$m_1 \|\vec{g}\| R \sin \alpha = \|\vec{F}\| (R + R \cos \alpha).$$

From here

$$\|\vec{F}\| = m_1 \|\vec{g}\| \frac{\sin \alpha}{1 + \cos \alpha}.$$

Substituting this expression for $\|\vec{F}\|$ and the expression found above for $\|\vec{N}_2\|$ into inequality (1), we obtain

$$\mu \geq \frac{m_1}{m_1 + m_2} \frac{\sin \alpha}{1 + \cos \alpha}.$$

This means $\mu$ must be greater than the largest of the expressions on the right in inequalities (4) and (6). Obviously, for this to happen, condition (4) must be satisfied, that is,

$$\mu \geq \frac{\sin \alpha}{1 + \cos \alpha}.$$

Since $\cos \alpha = \frac{OP}{OO'} = \frac{R - r}{R + r} = \frac{D - d}{D + d}$ and $\sin \alpha = \frac{2 \sqrt{Dd}}{D + d}$, then

$$\mu \geq \frac{2 \sqrt{Dd}}{(1 + \frac{D - d}{D + d}) (D + d)} = \sqrt{\frac{d}{D}},$$

$$|\vec{F}| = \sqrt{\frac{d}{D}} m_1 \|\vec{g}\|.$$
