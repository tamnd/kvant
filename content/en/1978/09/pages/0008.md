As already mentioned, not all directions in a crystal are equal. In order to verify this, it is convenient (as is customary in crystal physics) to associate the coordinate system with the unit cell. In general, the origin of coordinates is chosen at one of the vertices of the cell, and the coordinate axes are directed along its edges, while the length of the edges is conventionally considered to be unit. the direction along the $X$ axis is denoted by [100], along the $Y$ axis by [010], and along the $Z$ axis by [001]. Planes coinciding with the edges of the cube and perpendicular to the $X$, $Y$ and $Z$ axes are designated by correspondence (100), (010), (001). the directions along the diagonals of the faces are designated [110], [101], [011], and the crystal planes perpendicular to these directions are designated (110), (101), (011). Finally, the direction of the spatial diagonal is designated [111], and the crystal planes perpendicular to it are designated (111).

Considering the image of the unit cell, it is easy to see that the [100], [010], [001] directions of the crystal are equivalent to each other and not equivalent to the [111] direction.

Above we listed the so-called crystallographic sky directions and crystallographic planes. Other directions and planes are designated similarly: [hkl] for a direction and (hkl) for planes perpendicular to this direction. The quantities $h$, $k$, $l$ take integer values ​​and are called crystallographic indices (or Muller indices). For directions and planes other than the main ones, at least one of the indices differs from 0 or 1.

The symbol (hkl) does not denote one particular plane, but a set of parallel planes passing through the nodes (atoms) of the crystallographic lattice. Moreover, in the plane corresponding to small crystallographic indices, the density of atoms turns out to be greater than in the plane with higher indices, and the distances between these planes are greater than the distances between planes with high crystallographic indices. For example, for a crystal with the simplest cubic cell, the distance between the (100) planes is equal to 1, and between the (110) planes corresponding to higher indices, the distance is $\sqrt{2}/2$ (see Fig. 2).
