However, Stark’s theoretical idea could not be verified at that time, and it was found to be blocked. More than fifty years passed before scientists began experimentally studying the dependence of the penetration depth of charged particles into crystals on the orientation of crystal samples relative to beams of these particles. It turned out that, indeed, if the direction of motion of a particle coincides with the direction of the crystal channel or close to it, then the particle, when moving in the crystal, loses energy much more slowly than when moving in other directions. As a result, the length of its gap turned out to be significantly longer in this direction than in others (Fig. 1). In this case, they say that the particle is captured into a crystal channel, and the phenomenon itself is called particle channeling.

Interest in this phenomenon was aroused by the “second discovery” of channeling, made in the early sixties (as well as the first time, theoretically) when modeling the motion of ions in a single crystal on an electronic computer. It turned out that channeling and accompanying phenomena are of great interest for solid state physics and nuclear physics, since they are an effective method for studying the properties of crystals and some nuclear phenomena. Wide possibilities for the technical use of channeling have also emerged, in particular, in semiconductor technology. But about all this river territory a little later.

On the description of crystal structures

As is known, in crystals atoms are arranged in space in a regular manner and form the so-called crystal lattice. Atoms of crystals (like all other bodies) undergo thermal motion, but this motion is greatly limited. Atoms oscillate in close-equilibrium positions, called crystal lattice, with a very small amplitude ($\sim 10^{-9}$ cm). Therefore, to describe a number of properties of crystalline structures, we can assume that atoms do not move at all. In any case, the model of a crystal with stationary atoms is, as physicists say, a well-studied approximation for describing the multihot properties of crystals. In what follows, we will use this idea of ​​the structure of crystals.

It must be noted that the ideal picture drawn above, in which atoms throughout the entire volume of the crystal are strictly in their places, is very rarely realized in nature. Such ideal samples are called single crystals. The studied crystalline bodies are far from such a perfect one - very small areas in which there is a perfect order in the arrangement of atoms, but when moving from one crystal to another, the order is disrupted. The channeling phenomenon we are discussing is best manifested in experiments with single crystals, therefore, in what follows, the term “crystal” will be understood as “monocrystal”.

Due to the fact that in a crystal (monocrystal) there is a strict order in the arrangement of atoms, to study crystals it is enough to use the minimum volume of the crystal, which fully reflects all its connection structures. This minimum volume is called the unit cell of the crystal. The dimensions of the unit cell are of the order of (1–10) $10^{-8}$ cm.
