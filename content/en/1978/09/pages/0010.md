Influence of channeling on nuclear processes. Locking effect

The results of experiments on studying nuclear reactions under conditions of channeling of particles causing a nuclear reaction are very interesting. It turned out that channeled particles react less readily than particles that have not undergone channeling. Consider, for example, this reaction. If the nuclei of the silicon isotope $^{28}$Si are bombarded with protons, then the result is phosphorus nuclei $^{29}$P and gamma quanta:

$$2^{28}Si + p \rightarrow 2^{29}P + \gamma.$$

The number of reaction events can be judged by the number of emitted γ quanta.

In an experiment in which a silicon crystal was used as a target, and a beam of protons was directed at the crystal at different angles to the [110] direction, a sharp dependence of the number of γ quanta on the orientation of the beam (Fig. 4) was revealed. When the direction of the proton beam coincides with the [110] direction, a minimum in the number of reaction events is observed, as evidenced by a decrease in the counting rate of γ quanta.

Similar results were obtained for a number of other nuclear reactions.

A phenomenon closely related to channeling is the so-called blocking effect. It lies in the fact that the output of charged particles from the crystal, resulting from the radioactive decay of nuclei in a single crystal, sharply depends on the angle between the direction of their velocities and the axis of the crystal channel. Moreover, in the direction of the channel axis, the number of particles emerging from the crystal turns out to be minimal. This is due to the fact that the radioactive decay of the nucleus occurs at a node of the crystal lattice, and the movement of a particle emitted in a direction parallel to the channel axis transforms the atoms that form the walls of the channel.

Large angle scattering

Close to the blocking efficiency - according to the observed results - is the reflection (or backscattering) of ions from single-crystal targets. Indeed, in order for it to be reflected back (scattering over a large angle), it is necessary that it come almost close to some core of the atom of the crystal. Only in this case will the positive electrostatic repulsion between the charged she and the atomic nucleus be strong enough to change the direction of her movement to the opposite. Thus, its backward scattering is almost similar to the emission of a charged particle from the nucleus discussed above during the process of radioactive decay. Experiments fully confirm this.
