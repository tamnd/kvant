In this case, it, having “pushed off” from one wall of the channel, will approach its opposite wall, simultaneously moving along the axis of the channel. Then he will “push off” from the opposite wall in the same way. In general, its movement along the crystal channel will be accompanied by its “oscillations” between the walls of the channel.

If the transverse component of the velocity is too strong, the Coulomb repulsion forces turn out to be insufficient to keep it in the crystal channel; it goes beyond the channel, that is, no channeling occurs.

A visual model of this behavior can be provided by the movement of the ball in the gutter (Fig. 7). If we, while launching a ball along the axis of the chute, simultaneously give it a small speed perpendicular to the axis of the chute, then its movement along the chute will be accompanied by oscillations from wall to wall. If the velocity component perpendicular to the axis is too strong, the ball will simply jump out of the chute. The existence of a limiting transverse speed of the ball, at which it does not yet jump out of the chute, corresponds to the critical angle $\psi_c$ introduced above for channeling.

At first glance, the analogy between the motion of a channeled particle and the motion of a ball in a trough may be surprising. Indeed, the “walls” of the city’s crystal canals are more like a fishing net with a large mesh or a volleyball net than the solid walls of a gutter. The analogy turns out to be good only because, when moving along the axis of the channel, the particle makes one oscillation from wall to wall over the length of the gap, approximately equal to a hundred international distances, so that the discreteness of the wall turns out to be insignificant. If the particle abruptly changes the direction of its movement due to interaction with one nucleus, the analogy with the movement of a ball in a trough does not work. But in this case, as we have seen, particle channeling does not occur. So as long as the particle is channeled, the trough analogy is quite good.

Note that the motion of a particle in a crystal is closest to the motion of a ball in a trough when we are talking about interplanar channeling of a particle, in which the particle oscillates between crystal planes bounding the channel. In the case of a master channel, the movement of the particle turns out to be more complex (see, for example, the intro to the article).

The above qualitative description of the behavior of a channeled particle in a crystal makes it possible to obtain the dependence of the critical angle $\psi_c$ on the particle energy. Let us denote by $v$ the speed of motion of the particle, and by $v_c$ the critical value of its transverse velocity. For a particle moving at a small angle to the channel axis, the transverse velocity

$$\vec{v} = \vec{v}_c - \frac{\vec{v}_c}{\|\vec{v}\|}.$$
