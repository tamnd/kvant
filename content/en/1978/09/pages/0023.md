The weakness of the reverse reaction is also confirmed by experience with a Segner wheel. Two vessels (Fig. 4) A and B are connected by a gutta pepper tube. At the bottom of vessel A there is a small segner wheel, through which the tube communicates with vessel B. A certain amount of water is poured into both vessels. If you place vessel B above vessel A, then water begins to pour from B to A, and the segner wheel begins to rotate; but if vessel B is placed significantly lower than vessel A, then water will flow from A to B, and the segner wheel will remain motionless."

You can check the operation of a segner wheel yourself using the device shown in figure 5. Carefully drill three holes with a diameter of 4-5 mm in the ping-pong ball so that their centers coincide with the ends of two mutually perpendicular diameters of the ball. Insert PVC tubes bent over the hot plate into two holes lying on the same line (they are often used to insulate wires). Insert a metal rod from a ballpoint pen into the third hole, in the upper part of which (located inside the ball) several holes are made. To keep the ping pong ball stable at the end of the rod, you need to make a small indentation in the inside of the ball with a blunt awl. Place a rubber bulb on the lower end of the rod.

When you press the pear, air comes out of the curved tubes of the Segner wheel, and the wheel begins to rotate. When air is drawn in, the Segner wheel remains motionless. By periodically pressing and releasing the pear, you can make the Segner wheel rotate at a fairly high speed.

Think about what other experiments in which inflowing and outflowing reactions are manifested could be performed.

Another demonstration of weightlessness

In the fourth issue of our magazine, A. Dozorov’s article “Demonstration of weightlessness” was published, which describes a simple device that makes it possible to visually verify the absence of weight in freely feeding bodies. After this publication, the editors received a letter in which reader Yu. Smirnov from Tula writes that “it is much easier and simpler to demonstrate weightlessness if you use a polystylene bag with a small hole filled with water.”

These are the simple observations Yu. Smirnov offers. When the bag is held weightlessly, a stream of water flows out of the hole. If, holding a bag filled with water by the edges, it rises faster, the shavings become stronger. If you already hold it by the edges, the shavings of water are weakened. If you let go of the bag, giving it the opportunity to fall freely, water does not flow out through the hole.

These simple experiments demonstrate not only weightlessness, but also the occurrence of overloads (“positive”) and “negative”).
