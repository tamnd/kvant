Knowledge of hydro laws

torus, is absolutely necessary for everyone who deals with the sea verse and atmosphere, especially for sailors and pilots. He backs up his statement with numerous facts, mostly sad ones. Thus, ignoring the laws inherent in Bernoulli’s control became the reason for the hundred-year-old battleship “Hawk” with the superliner “Olympic”, and neglect of Archimedes’ law and the “principle of irreplaceability” based on it, which was developed by Academician Krylov, caused one of the most terrible tragedies in the entire history of navigation - the death of the “Titanic”.

In nature and everyday practice, the vortex movement of liquids and gases plays an important role. Whirlwinds are a destructive death at sea and a safe whirlpool in a bathtub, a dangerous airplane wake and a whirlpool in a river. How vortex phenomena are formed, what impact they have on the movement of ships and aircraft, on the operation of engines and tourism, on the strength of structures - all this is described in the book.

An equally large place is devoted to another tricky phenomenon - cabitation, or, according to the author’s figurative definition, “cold boiling”. Cabitation is the emergence and rapid “collapse” of air bubbles, which leads to an instantaneous local increase in pressure up to 100,000 atmospheres. In this case, ship propellers, turbine and pump blades, and parts of nuclear reactors are covered with “ulcers” and ultimately destroyed. It turns out that cavitation affects not only parts moving in a liquid at high speeds, but also damage to stationary bodies immersed in stationary liquids! The author introduces in detail the causes of cabitation, measures of prevention and treatment of this dangerous disease.

Two chapters—“Liquids Resist” and “Not Only Birds Fly”—tell the history of the struggle for speed in navigation and aviation. Man learns from nature - this thesis runs like a red thread through narration. It turns out that the scaly cover of fish is not just a protective layer, but also an active participant in movement, a means of reducing the resistance of the aquatic environment. Even more surprising is that the gills perform the same function. When the gill covers are slightly lifted (actions are similar to the valves of a pump), the water from the fish's mouth rushes to the gills. There it separates dissolved oxygen and, flowing from the gills responsible in the form of a strong jet, “blows away” the boundary layer. The idea of ​​“blowing away” or “suctioning” the boundary layer of the shriko is now used in aviation and astronautics. Likewise, the idea of ​​slats and flaps, indispensable elements of a modern aircraft, is borrowed from birds. Recently, dolphins, capable of reaching speeds of up to 65 kilometers per hour, have become the object of additional blame among scientists, including hydrodynamicists. The dolphins have already shared some of their “secrets,” but they still want to find out the rest, and then the ships will learn to swim even faster and longer than they do now.

The “toys” that scientists love to “play with” are discussed in the chapter “Modeling”. “During model tests,” the author writes, “real phenomena appear before us on a modified scale, that is, with different geometric dimensions and the size of the operating awl. This allows scientists to “experience” a century in one hour or a second in a week, makes it possible, as far as necessary, to strengthen or weaken natural phenomena, to make them accessible to our senses through equipment.”

Unfortunately, having dwelt in detail on physical and geometric modeling, the author did not say a word about mathematical modeling, which in recent years, in connection with the development of computers, has become a powerful tool for solving scientific and engineering problems in hydrodynamics.

The book is abundantly illustrated with photographs of hydrodynamic processes. But gases and liquids, as a rule, are non-poisonous! How were these chimki obtained, on which there were no lines or shock waves? Modern experimental methods of hydrodynamics, scientific from the simplest Pitot method to laser devices, are described in the book “Can We See the Flow?”

To summarize the review of K. Kuzov’s small book, I would like to quote the words of the author, who formulated his goal as follows: “The main task of this book is to contribute to the acquisition of hydrodynamics, to break the barrier of fear of complex forms of specialized literature and to attract the attention of young people to an important branch of human knowledge, the importance of which in the practical life of people is constantly returning.”

Well, we can say with enlargement that K. Kuzov’s book is a complete response to this task.
