This method is suitable for measuring the time of nuclear processes (reactions) accompanied by the emission of charged particles. The course of such reactions can be divided into two stages. The first stage is the capture of one of the particles possessed by the sample by the nucleus of a crystal atom. The second stage is the decay of the nucleus that captured the particles.

The time elapsed from the moment of particle capture until the decay of the nucleus can be determined by how the blocking effect manifests itself in the release of charged decay products from the crystal. The idea of ​​the project method is elegant and consists of the following. The nucleus that has captured the particle (as physicists say, a compound nucleus), receives the momentum of the absorbed particle, is knocked out of the crystalline node it occupies and begins to move in the crystal at a very high speed, on the order of $10^8-10^9$ cm/s. If the lifetime of the compound core is very short and it practically does not have time to report from the node it occupies before decay, the blocking effect is fully manifested when charged decay products leave the crystal.

If, during its lifetime, the core manages to report from its node, then a weakening of the blocking effect appears, since in this case the movement of the decay product along the crystal axis is not automatically prevented. The degree of weakening of the blocking determines the distances over which the compound core will mix, and, knowing its accumulation, at present there is still no complete understanding of the features of the movement of these particles in single crystals. Nevertheless, it is clear that a new, amazingly subtle method for studying crystals is emerging in the hands of physicists.
