Bear! Rummaging through old «Kvant», I recently realized that points and lines can be called any objects for which the properties specified in the axioms are satisfied *). Then I realized that distance is any number **) that has properties 1-3 indicated in the textbook.

And in different geometries, distance can be determined in different ways: in school geometry - in one way, in Lobachevsky’s geometry - in another way, and in mine - in a third way. It is only important that three of its properties are satisfied. That's what Pyotr Ivanovich said.

And yesterday I found such a place in Karl Levitin’s book “Geometrical Spread”. The great physicist Einstein explains to his nine-year-old son why he became famous in science: “When a blind beetle crawls along the surface of a ball, it does not replace the fact that the path it has taken is curved, but I was lucky enough to notice this.” Come on, you and I will notice something and come up with something.

R.S. It seems that with distance I am developing a new “craziness”. Wait, I'll write.

Your eternal friend Styopa Moshkin.

*) Styopa is referring to the article “Point, straight line... - what is it?” («Kvant», 1975, No. 9 11-12).

**) Here Styopa does not express himself very competently. Distance is not a "number" but a numerical function defined on pairs of points.

Follow up

I’m sure “Stepino’s Distance” seemed very unusual to you. But what to do? Since all three properties (axioms) of distances are satisfied for it, it should be called a distance.

Is it possible to add some kind of axiom and call only that which satisfies the extended list of axioms distance? For example, on a plane for any number there is a pair of points, the distance between which is equal to this number. maybe include this property as a fourth distance property? But then what is considered such on a sphere, globe or sheet of paper will cease to be a distance (explain why).

What is the benefit of fantasies similar to Stepina’s? It is impossible to answer this question in advance and for every case. But for his case, Stepa himself must: one of the properties of the location of a point on a straight line cannot be derived from the properties of the distance.

Another conclusion from Moshkin’s words: when we introduce a certain concept, we start from one or more models (for example, when introducing the concept of distance, we start from a model in which the distance is changed using a scale bar on a plan or sheet of paper). But after we have introduced this concept, we must be prepared for the fact that it may have completely unexpected, unusual models.
