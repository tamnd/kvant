In what follows, we will assume that in a closed polyhedron all faces are absciltonically rigid, and along the edges the neighboring faces are hinged, like the plates of a door hinge. Do we admit at least one small closed polyhedron to have continuous excess?

4. First Bricard octahedron

Let us consider a four-bar mechanism $ABCD$ (Fig. 3) ​​on the plane $P$. The vertices $A, C, B, D$, in which the hinges are located, serve as the vertices of an equal trapezoid. The quadrilateral $ACBD$ is movable: the angle $\alpha$ can be changed within certain limits; in this case, links $AB$ and $CD$ slide freely over each other. As $\alpha$ changes, the vertices of the quadrilateral continue to form an equal trapezoid. Therefore, a circle can be described around the quadrilateral $ACBD$ at any moment.

Let us now fix the position of the edge $AB$ on the plane $P$. Then the radius $R(\alpha)$ of the circumcircle depends continuously on $\alpha$. The center of the circle $O(\alpha)$ also continuously depends on $\alpha$ (in the sense that a small change $\alpha$ leads to a small shift of the point $O(\alpha)$).

3. A little history

Back in 1813, the famous French mathematician Augustin Cauchy proved that a convex polyhedron does not allow redundancy at all while preserving convexity (unless one considers its mirror image to be bending) *. Trying to answer the question whether a closed non-convex polyhedron can be continuously bent, R. Bricard in 1897 constructed examples of this kind of polyhedra. But these were polyhedra with self-intersections. Below we describe two Bricard designs.

The question of the existence of a continuously flexible closed polyhedron without self-intersection remained open. Many geometers assumed that such a polyhedron did not exist. However, in 1977, the American mathematician R. Coelli **) built such a polyhedron! Its construction is based on the results of Bricard.
