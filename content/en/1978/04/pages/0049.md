Zinc and copper ions each carry a double electronic charge ($Zn^{++}, Cu^{++}$). Further, it should be borne in mind that in a solution of sulfuric acid, a significant part of the molecules turns into three ions - two positively charged hydrogen ions ($H^+$) and one on-group $SO_4^-$ with a double negative charge ($SO_4^{--}$):

$$H_2SO_4 \rightarrow H^+ + H^+ + SO_4^{--}$$

Let's see what happens when zinc and copper plates are immersed in such a solution. Let's start with zinc.

In the presence of the acid solution and the siliptic charges it contains, the attraction between zinc ions and electrons decreases, and the zinc ions are torn from the plate and transferred to the solution. The plant ends up with an excess of electrons, so it becomes negatively charged. But as $Zn^{++}$ ions accumulate in the solution, the remaining part of these ions, pressed down by the negative charge of the plate, returns to the growth. Eventually, equilibrium occurs: the number of ions leaving the zinc becomes equal to the number of ions returning to it. But the plant remains negatively charged. The solution near the plate receives a positive charge (due to the positive ions $Zn^{++}$ present in it).

In copper plants, different processes occur. Copper almost does not dissolve in sulfuric acid solution. When present in the zinc plant, zinc ions repel the positive hydrogen ions in the solution, assigning them to the copper plant. Here, these ions, falling on copper, take away electrons from it, becoming neutral hydrogen atoms. the copper, of course, becomes positively charged. Thus, in the Volta element, copper is the positive band, and zinc is the negative band. The potential difference between them is the electromotive force of this element. It is equal to 1.1 V.

If you connect the stripes (electrons) of an element with a wire, then an electric current will arise in the resulting electrical circuit, directed from copper to zinc. This means that the electrons in it move from zinc, where they are abundant, to copper, where there is a shortage of electrons.

What about the element itself? Here, due to the departure of electrons from the zinc, the $Zn^{++}$ ions will be held weaker, and they will begin to move into dissolve (zinc will dissolve), maintaining a negative charge of the plate. And due to the arrival of electrons to the copper plate, it will become more “attractive” to itself of positive hydrogen ions, to which these electrons will go. So the copper plate will also maintain a positive charge. Thus, the end result of these processes in the element will be the departure of electrons from copper and their appearance on zinc: electrons seem to move from copper to zinc.
