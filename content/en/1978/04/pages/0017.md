Before the collision, the balls move according to the laws $x = x_1(t) = a_1 + v_1t$ and $x = x_2(t) = a_2 + v_2t$ (here $a_1$ and $a_2$ are the initial coordinates of the balls); their motion graphs are straight (more precisely, segments or rays) on the plane $Otx$, with angular coefficients $v_1$ and $v_2$. The moment of collision of the balls corresponds to the point of intersection of the graphs. After the collision, the graphs $x = x_1(t)$ and $x = x_2(t)$ are also straight. After the collision, graph $x = x_1(t)$ became equal to $z_1 = v_2$, then the first graph after the collision is a continuation of the second graph $x = x_2(t)$ before the collision; similarly, the second graph after the collision is a continuation of the first.

§ 2. For balls of different masses: configuration space

When two balls of different masses collide, not an exchange of speed will occur, but a more complex redistribution of speeds. In the case under consideration (for balls of different masses), examining graphs on the $Otx$ plane no longer gives anything good. Moreover, it is not at all inconceivable that the number of events will necessarily be finite: imagine that a light ball is attacked from both sides (not simultaneously!) by two massive balls (see figure on the screensaver); won't the small ball oscillate endlessly between the big ones?
