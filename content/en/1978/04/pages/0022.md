On the contrary, using $n$-dimensional geometry, we can show that the total number of contents cannot be infinite; According to this scheme, this problem was solved in 1971 by its author, Professor of Moscow State University Ya. G. Sinai. We will present other solutions that do not require knowledge of multidimensional geometry; the idea for the solution was proposed by A. Avdeev, a student at the Physics and Mathematics School at Moscow State University (now a student at MIPT); later similar solutions were found by Kyiv University student A. $1^\circ$. We will reason by induction on the number of balls. In § 2 it was proved that for $n = 3$ the number of collisions is finite. Let us assume that for any initial conditions in a group of $k < n$ balls during the time from 0 to $T$ (where $T$ can be equal to $+\infty$) only a finite number of collisions will occur. Let us assume that for each initial condition $n$ the balls become infinite many times during the time from 0 to $T$. Let's resize the balls in the order they follow along the $Ox$ axis, but from right to left. At each moment of time, consider the total implus $k$ of the right balls:

$$P_k = m_1 v_1 + m_2 v_2 + \dots + m_k v_k (k = 1, 2, \dots, n)$$

With each paired collision, the value of $P_k$ can only increase: indeed, $P_k$ does not change if the balls to the left of the $k$ or to the right of the $(k+1)$ are played; if the $k$th ball faces the $(k+1)$th one, the $P_k$th one increases, (for the $(k+1)$th ball only gives the $k$th one to the right).

Let us now denote by $v_i^{(s)}$ the speed of the $i$-th ball after the $s$-th collision in order (in which the $i$-th ball may not participate), and by $P_k^{(s)}$ - the implus $P_k$ after the $s$-th collision. From the above it follows that for every $k$ the sequence $P_k^{(s)}$ ($s = 1, 2, \dots$) is non-decreasing.

On the other hand, during all collisions, the total kinetic energy of all balls is conserved:

$$\frac{1}{2} \left( m_1 v_1^2 + m_2 v_2^2 + \dots + m_n v_n^2 \right) = E = \text{const.}$$

It follows that for each $i$ at any time the speed is limited:

$$m_i v_i^2 \leq 2E \text{ и } |v_i| \leq \sqrt{2E/m} \leq \sqrt{2E/m},$$

where $m$ is the smallest of the masses $m_1, \dots, m_n$. This implies the boundedness of each of the sequences $P_k^{(s)}$:

$$|P_k^{(s)}| \leq m_1 |v_1^{(s)}| + \dots + m_k |v_k^{(s)}| \leq (m_1 + \dots + m_k) \sqrt{2E/m}.$$

So, for every $k$, the sequence $P_k^{(s)}$ is monotonic and bounded, therefore, according to Weierstrass's theorem, all these sequences have a limit at $s \to \infty$. But $P_1^{(s)} = m_1 v_1^{(s)}$, so there is a limit $v_1^{(s)} = w_1$. Next, $P_2^{(s)} = m_1 v_1^{(s)} + m_2 v_2^{(s)}$, so there is a limit $v_2^{(s)} = w_2$ (explain). Continuing this reasoning, we deduce the existence of speed limits for all balls:

$$\lim_{s \to \infty} v_k^{(s)} = w_k (k = 1, 2, \dots, n).$$
