Red physics

The fact that physics can be talked about in a lively and exciting way has been proven by many authors of popular science books, but this is perhaps the first time the reader will encounter a colorful presentation of this science. The bright, effective binding (which schematically depicts the reaction of the interaction of a proton and an antiproton) cannot but attract attention, makes you pick up the book, open it, leaf through it, and even then you cannot take your eyes off the rainbow-colored illustration. On 270 pages there are exactly 300 figure! Figure are untrained, original, sometimes unique, and, most importantly, each figure carries a great semantic load. They do not just appear as text, but like reproductions in an album, they form an integral, organizational part of the book.

This is the first feature of the book, written (it would be better to say masterfully done) by the GDR scientist Helmut Lindner and recently published in Russian translation *). The second feature is the unconventional construction. Typically, in a book devoted to “all physics,” its classical sections are first presented: mechanics

Lindner broke this tradition. Already in the first chapter, entitled “Motion and Forces,” he, having barely introduced the reader to the elements of mechanics, immediately takes him to the world of optical phenomena - interfaces and light diffraction: And the next chapter (“Relativity”) is entirely devoted to the theory of relativity. It describes Michelsan’s experiment, which gave birth to the hypothesis of the existence of a “world physicist”, introduces the Galileo and Lorentz transformations, formulates the main postulates of Einstein’s special theory of relativity, and breaks down the consequences of this theory (contraction of length, change in the time scale, the significant “twin paradox”, the connection between mass and energy, etc.). Then, briefly, but intelligibly and therefore completely accessible to the unprepared reader, the fundamentals of the theory of relativity are outlined, and the possibility and attempts to experimentally test this theory are discussed.

Chapter III is devoted to thermal phenomena, but its very title - “Heat and Elevation” - warns that the reader will encounter here not only a presentation of issues that are significant in the school course, but also something deeper. And in fact, the author, for example, focuses on the question of reverse and non-reverse physical processes. As one of the founders of modern physics, Max Planck, argued, “the difference between inverse and non-inverse processes lies much deeper than, for example, between mechanical and electrical processes; therefore, it is reversibility with greater reason than any other feature that should be chosen as the primary

principle in the classification of physical phenomena."

To distinguish between reversible and non-reversible processes and to assess the degree of reversibility, you need a certain amount of methodological probability. And from here follows a natural transition to a consideration of the probabilistic laws that prevail in the world of atoms and molecules, to a discussion of the problems of regularity and randomness, order and disorder, and finally, the principle of increasing entropy and the issue of thermal estimates.

This chapter is, perhaps, the most complete manifestation of the author’s pedagogical talent - he presents all complex issues in a completely understandable manner, which is supported by impressive illustrations: successive steps of the Carnot cycle and an automatic cutter, playing dice and “Maxwell’s demon” sorting molecules, and many others.

In Chapter IV “Charges and Fields,” G. Lindner focuses on the development of the concept of the electromagnetic field. Without a single formula, as they say, “at your fingertips”, the essence of Maxwell’s equation (which underlies electrodynamics) and the consequences arising from them, in particular, the existence of electromagnetic waves, are revealed. Next, we talk about the four states of matter - plasma and its technical use (MHD generator), the spectrum of electromagnetic oscillations, polarization and addition of polarized waves.
