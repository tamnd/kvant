Moebius strip in art

The Möbius strip is the most popular representative of one-sided surfaces *). What this is is explained not only in mathematical literature, but even in fiction. For example, in the novel by the Costa Rican writer Joaquin Gutierrez “In the morning, Federico?...” (“Foreign Literature”, 1976, No. 2) it says:

*) The Möbius strip will be discussed in detail in one of the upcoming issues; in addition, see «Kvant», 1974, no. 3 and 1975, no. 7.

“Today the teacher showed us a Mobius strip. This is great. Take a strip of paper—it’s better from a newspaper, it’s longer—and you’ll see that the strip has two sides; confirmation of this is that if an ant wants to crawl along one side, it can do this as much as it wants, but in order to get to the other, it must climb over the edge. That's why they say that the stripe has two sides. But the Mobius strip turns out like this. You need to turn over one end of the strip, as if you are going to close it, but we make only one turn and glue the ends together. Then the paper will only have one side, and anyone who wants to can check it if in doubt. Leading his finger as if chasing an ant along the entire tape, he is suddenly convinced that both sides have converged into one and there is no need to cross the edge.”
Both artists and sculptors have resorted to the Möbius strip more than once. Quite a lot of different figure depicting the Möbius strip were left by the famous Dutch artist M. Escher (1898-1971). One of them is featured on the cover of our magazine.
A whole series of variants of the Möbius strip was created by the sculptor Max Bill (b. 1908). For almost twenty years, he repeatedly turned to the Möbius strip, trying to express in sculpture the idea of ​​perpetual motion and a form unfolding in space. However, one of his works - the sculpture "Knot without End", located in the National Museum of Modern Art in Paris - is depicted in the picture.
