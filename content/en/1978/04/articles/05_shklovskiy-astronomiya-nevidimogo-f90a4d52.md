It’s simply a miracle that we can see the stars through the enormous thickness of the earth’s atmosphere! After all, above every square centimeter of the surface of our planet there is one kilogram of atmospheric matter - a fact known even to Ostap Bender...
This is a real brow. Why does it absorb only 15-20% of radiation falling from space? But—stop! A caveat is necessary: ​​we are talking about visible radiation.

The light visible to our eyes is a very narrow strip on the scale of electromagnetic radiation, limited to wavelengths of 8·10⁻⁷ m (red rays) and 4·10⁻⁷ m (filulet rays). In the direction of wavelengths, this strip is adjacent to a wide band of infrared waves, extending approximately to a wavelength of 10⁻⁴ m. Even further, there is a huge region of radio waves from submillimeter to multi-kilometer waves.
In the direction of short waves from the strip of visible light there are ultraviolet waves with a length of up to 10⁻⁸ m, even shorter RENTgen rays and, finally, the shortest gamma rays.

The amazing transparency of the thick armor of the earth's atmosphere relates primarily to visible rays. In addition, the earth's atmosphere is also suitable for radio waves with a length from one centimeter to 15-20 meters. There are also separate “transparency windows” in the infrared region of the spectrum. The earth's atmosphere completely absorbs all other types of electromagnetic radiation.

Observed for many years now, the red shift of modern cosmology, starting from our replacement physicist and mathematician A. A. Friedman, declares the real distribution of the Universe. Over time, the distance between galaxies increases more and more, therefore, the scale of the Universe grows. Observing a quasar with λ/λ₀=4, we seem to be looking into the distant past of the Universe, when its dimensions were 4 times smaller than the current ones, and the average density of matter was 64 times greater. Well, what else was equal?

So far, quasars with λ/λ₀ greater than 4.6 have not yet been discovered. It was very likely that there would either be none at all, or very few. This means that galaxies (and, consequently, their nuclei) were formed at a certain stage in the development of the Universe, when its scale was 4-5 times smaller than the current one. Even in an earlier era, there were no galaxies. What happened? It turns out there was a gas, and a rather simple chemical state - a mixture of hydrogen and helium. As this gas expanded, associated with the expansion of the Universe, it decreased in density and cooled. This means that when the “age” of the Universe was several million years, the temperature of the gas filling it reached several thousand degrees. At this temperature, this gas was partially reduced, that is, it was a plasma.

An important feature of the Universe in the early stages of its evolution was that it contained a huge amount of radiation. The spectral composition of this radiation was the same as that of a body heated to the appropriate temperature. The energy density of this radiation (energy per unit volume) was hundreds of millions of times greater than the thermal energy density of the gas (since the matter in the Universe then still had a relatively low density). Quanta of radiant energy are in equilibrium with the gas atoms that absorbed and emitted them. This means that the number of quanta absorbed by atoms is equal to the number of quanta emitted by them (at the same time). For this reason, the temperatures of matter and radiation were the same. But this could only last for so long.

Due to the expansion of the Universe, the density of gas decreased rapidly, and this is the reason why the exchange of energy between radiation and gas occurs more and more slowly. Finally, these processes became so slow that the radiation filling the Universe lost everything with the gas. This will happen just when the age of the Universe was several million years old, and its dimensions were about a thousand times smaller than perfect. The temperature filling the Universe quickly decreased in accordance with the fact that its temperature changed in proportion to its size. And now, when since the time of “unstitching” of radiation from matter, the Universe has increased its size by more than a thousand times, we expect the temperature of the radiation filling it to be on the order of several degrees.

The greatest achievement of radio astronomy is the discovery of this radiation that fills the entire Universe, better called “relict radiation”. It is shown how the environment of animal and plant species living on Earth comes across “living anachronisms” - representatives of the fauna and flora of a long-past state of the Universe, when there were no galaxies and stars in it, but only hot gas and radiation filling the entire world. This discovery was made 12 years ago. It turns out that on radio waves in the centimeter and millimeter ranges, everything does not receive equally in all directions. The spectral composition of this radiation corresponds to a body heated to a temperature of 2.7 degrees Kelvin. The maximum intensity occurs at a wavelength of about one millimeter. The significance of this discovery lies primarily in the fact that it must be possible to study the Universe when it was ten thousand times “younger” than it is now, and completely different from the present one. The Universe has a history; under the influence of internal causes it is continuously developing and, of course, becoming more complex. For now the Universe is immeasurably richer and more complex than it was in that separate era. For example, at that time the substance did not yet have the rich chemical state it has now. Elements other than hydrogen and gel formed in the interiors of stars much later. It should also not be forgotten that life in the Universe could arise only after the formation of heavy elements.

It is paradoxical that we now know about that era, separate from us, when “relict” radiation was formed, much larger than the conditions that conditioned the formation of our Solar system and, in particular, the Earth, although this important event will not happen very long ago: just “some” five billion years ago, when the size of the Universe was half the size of today. This is a good example of the fact that science develops unevenly, that the progress of our knowledge comes from “exploring” into the unknown.

One of the most important areas in radio astronomy can be called “astropedectory”. We are talking about the study of various spectral lines, the wavelengths of which are in the radio range. Most often, these lines are observed in certain regions of a very thin interstellar medium, the integrated study of which has become especially important in recent years. The most “famous” radio line has a wavelength of 21 cm and belongs to hydrogen. Since hydrogen is the most abundant element in the Universe, studying this line provides very valuable information that applies to our entire star system - the Galaxy. Studying this line makes it possible to reveal the temperature of the interstellar medium, its density and the nature of the movement of clouds of interstellar gas.

Along with atoms, interstellar gas contains molecules. Many of them emit radio lines that are successfully observed. What was unexpected was the discovery in interstellar space of complex, polyatomic molecules, for example, formic acid, ethyl and methyl alcohols and a number of others. The study of radio lines from molecules opens up the study of the isotopic state of matter in the Universe. It was in this way that interstellar deuterium was first discovered - heavy hydrogen, whose radio line has a wavelength of about 92 cm. It turns out that in the interstellar medium, for every hundred thousand atoms of cloud hydrogen there is one atom of heavy hydrogen.

This discovery has some rather unexpected consequences. The fact is that actions cannot form in the depths of stars. Already at a fairly “moderate” temperature of several million degrees, nuclear reactions occur, leading to the destruction of nuclei of activity. Therefore, just like ordinary hydrogen, it must have a “relict” origin. It turns out that the nuclear reactions in which the action was synthesized should have taken place when the temperature of the Universe was several billion degrees, and its age was only about 10 minutes! This is where the “eyes” of radio telecops can look...

A special place in space radio spectroscopy is occupied by the study of the lines of the hydroxyl molecule OH, the wavelengths of which (there are only four) are about 18 cm. The fact is that at this wave, quite unexpectedly, very small sources were discovered, usually located in gaseous nebulae. It turned out that these sources operate on the same principle as lasers—quantum generators of electromagnetic waves in the visible range of the spectrum. For radio waves, the sources are also called *masers*. In addition, very bright sources in the H₂O water vapor lines with a wavelength of 1.35 cm, having the same nature, were discovered in approximately the same places in the sky. Thus, it turned out that nature implements in natural conditions a device that is, in our time, a symbol of the scientific and technological revolution experienced by mankind! What is most curious is that these amazing sources are located in close proximity to the region of the Galaxy, where, according to all data, the still largely mysterious process of the formation of young stars from the interstellar gaseous medium originated. Thus, radio engineering, quite unexpectedly, turned out to be a powerful means of studying this long-standing classical problem.

When listing the outstanding achievements of radio astronomy over the past 10-15 years, one cannot remain silent about the discovery that excited humanity 10 years ago. We are talking about the discovery of *pulsars*. This completely unusual class of radio sources is characterized by a strict periodicity of radio signals. The shortest known period is close to 1/30 of a second, the longest is about 4 seconds. Research has shown that pulsars are very rapidly rotating *neutron stars*, and the period of rotation coincides with the observed periodicity of radio pulses. What is a neutron star? Imagine our Sun rolled down to a size of about 10 km. Since the radius of the Sun is close to 700,000 km, and its average density is 1.4 g/cm³, the average density of a 10-kilometer ball - a neutron star - will be close to 10¹⁵ g/cm³. This is a surprisingly large value, close to the density of the substance of an atomic nucleus.

Neutron stars, as the natural end of the evolution of certain stars, were theoretically predicted over 40 years ago. These stars (with one important exception) cannot be detected in optical rays. Only the methods of advanced radio astronomy have made it possible in our day to discover neutron stars - the dream of two generations of physicists and astronomers around the world. This is one of the greatest discoveries in astronomy and physics of the 20th century, rich in outstanding achievements of science.
