Gradually the term “energy” came into use. In 1807, the English physicist Thomas Young began using it in scientific articles. In 1829, French physicist Gaspard Coriolis first used the term “kinetic energy.” In 1851, the concept of “potential energy” appeared (English physicist William Rankine). And finally, in a lecture by William Thomson (Lord Kelvin) in 1881, it was said: “The word energy itself, although used in the modern sense by Dr. Thomas Young at the beginning of this century, is only now coming into use, after the theory has developed from a simple formula to a principle that pierces all nature and guides the investigator in the field of science.” In honor of Joule, at the Second International Congress of Electricians in 1889, a unit of energy was named after him. But, unfortunately, the custom of measuring thermal and chemical energy in the old energy units - calories - has survived to this day. Chemists and chemical technologists especially hold on to this unit. Some justification for them can be the fact that the specific heat capacity of water is exactly equal to unity (1 cal (g ◊ deg)). In addition to establishing the law of transformation and conservation of energy, Joule did a lot in thermodynamics and the study of gas processes. Together with W. Thomson, he discovered the cooling effect during slow (rather than fast) expansion of gas through a porous partition from high to low density. This effect, called Joule–Thomson, still underlies the process of producing liquefied gases and many technological processes. For example, if it is necessary to condense water vapor contained in natural gas before it enters the pipeline, in order to protect steel pipes from oxidation (rust). Joule was one of the founders of the kinetic theory of gases, far ahead of the work of Maxwell and Boltzmann. A staunch supporter of the atomic theory, he viewed heat as the energy of gas particles transferred from one body to another. He considered the gas pressure to be the result of impacts of gas particles on the walls of the vessel, and the speed of the particles to depend on the temperature of the gas. He also studied the magnetic properties of substances - he discovered the phenomenon of saturation during the magnetization of iron and the phenomenon of magnetostriction during magnetization reversal. Thanks to his precise experimental work, Joule was already elected to the Royal Society at the age of 32 and took a strong place in the history of science as one of the creators of the law of conservation and transformation of energy, this most important law of nature.
