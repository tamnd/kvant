KVAN T 2018/№7

It would be possible to observe this piece of land during the summer, systematically measure the light flux entering its different parts, and determine the soil moisture. Then average all the data, obtain “maps” of the distribution of light and humidity and compare them with the “map” of moss growth. This will be an observational study - the most cautious, i.e. minimally affecting the system under study, but at the same time labor-intensive and not always allowing for an unambiguous interpretation. You can carry out an experimental study: place illuminators near the bench that create uniform illumination of the entire space under it during the day, but do not prevent water from entering the ground through the cracks between the boards during rain. Or, conversely, evenly moisten the ground under the bench without changing the conditions of light and shadow, and observe how the figure formed by the moss changes. But even after carefully performing such research, you may not get a definite answer. Imagine, for example, that a certain range of soil moisture is fundamental to the growth of vegetation. Moreover, this humidity is determined not by the penetration of moisture under the bench through the cracks in it during rain, but by the penetration of sunlight through the same cracks, which dries out the soil (and in the shade it remains moister). And then the distribution of moisture under the bench will be determined by... figure light and shadow! Similar problems occur quite often in real research. They don't necessarily make the problem unsolvable, but they can make it very difficult to solve. And therefore, those experimental models that allow us to see a clear, unambiguous solution are always especially valuable. The photograph shown here on figure 2 and on page 4 of the cover of this issue provides an example of just such a successful model of a plant community exposed to a range of changing growing conditions. The photograph clearly shows how the density of vegetation gradually decreases as you move up the steps. If desired, you can even determine the optimal moisture level for growth. True, here too one can suspect the play of various factors - for example (along with moisture), wave impacts that wash away algae from the steps. Thus, the picture we observe is still somewhat more complex than it might seem at first glance, and is determined not by one, but at least two factors. However, each factor has its own “area of ​​responsibility”: one of them (humidity) determines the upper limit of distribution, and the second (unrest) determines the lower limit. Just like in real biogeography! Truly a good model. However, to paraphrase the well-known expression about a correct and beautiful theory, we can say that a good model is always beautiful!
