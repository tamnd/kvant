AND HISTORY OF SCIENCE

James Joule

(on the 200th anniversary of his birth)

L. BELOPUKHOV

THE GREAT ENGLISH SCIENTIST JAMES

Prescott Joule (1818–1889) was born in Salford, near Manchester, into the family of a brewer. Due to poor health, he was able to begin systematic schooling only at the age of 15, and at the same time he had to work at the family brewery (after the death of his father, he managed it together with his brother) until the age of 36, when the family enterprise was finally sold. One of his home teachers was John Dalton (1766–1844), a remarkable experimental physicist and theoretical chemist, one of the creators of atomistic concepts in chemistry. Dalton taught Joule mathematics and physics. This study was so successful that already at the age of 19, Joule applied for a patent for the original electric motor that he designed and built in his workshop.

A member of the editorial board of our journal, Lel Konstantinovich Belopukhov, recently celebrated his 90th birthday. For this anniversary, he prepared for publication a new book entitled “Physics and Physicists,” in which he outlines the history of the development of his beloved science through the biographies of its creators. The book was published by the University Book House publishing house in the series “Library of the Physics and Mathematics School” and contains 75 biographies of outstanding physicists. Their choice was determined solely by the author's sympathies and preferences, based on many years of teaching experience in higher education. We chose as an example the biography of James Joule, who turned 200 years old this year. The editors of our magazine congratulate Lelya Konstantinovich on his anniversary and wish him new books and articles in «Kvant», as well as curious listeners and readers.

James Joule (1818–1889)

Joule was interested in electricity and heat. After careful, precise experiments in 1841, he established the law of thermal action of electric current (the dependence of the amount of heat released in a conductor when an electric current passes through it, on the magnitude of the current and the resistance of the conductor). A year later, in 1842, the Russian physicist Emilius Lenz (1804–1865), dean of the Faculty of Physics and Mathematics of St. Petersburg University (who later became its rector), knowing nothing about Joule’s experiments, also experimentally established this law, which today bears a double name: the Joule–Lenz law.
