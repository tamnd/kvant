celestial mechanics

So, on our astronomical journey, we have finally gone beyond the Earth. And first of all, we came across the problems of describing the cosmic trajectories followed by celestial bodies. For the time being, the only source of information about them was observations with the naked eye, which often led to unfounded speculation or very complex theoretical concepts. Moreover, these materialistic constructions had to be coordinated with religious canons! Despite everything, the picture of the world around us has been replenished with new details over many centuries, and with the invention of optical instruments a significant breakthrough was made in the knowledge of space. Peering through the telescope's eyepiece, astronomers have greatly expanded our understanding of the Solar System. It turned out that the movements of its constituent planets, comets and asteroids obey laws discovered by scientists, which now make it possible not only to explain, but also to predict their future fate. Today, a striking confirmation of the universality of these laws is the discovery of more and more exoplanets that are part of incredibly distant similarities to the Solar System. The celestial mechanics created by human genius works there too! Let's try to verify its reliability using examples closer to us.

Summation of games

Let us define the concept of the sum of two or more independent games. Two players simultaneously play several games - "on several boards." On each turn, the player has the right to choose one of the games and make a move in it in accordance with the rules. (In this case, the other player is not obliged to immediately respond in the same game, but can make a move in another.) The states of the remaining games do not change. If no move can be made in any of the games (as in Nimes, when all the piles are empty), then the player who must move loses. If all games are simultaneously in states with a “–” sign, then the sum of games is also in a minus state, i.e. The second player can ensure victory for himself. Indeed, the first player makes a move in one of the games and necessarily transfers the game to the state indicated by the “+” sign. The second player should receive a minus state in response in the same game: according to the rules for placing the “+” and “–” signs, this can always be done. Ultimately, each game will end with the second player's turn. This means that the last winning move in the general game will be made by the second player. It is important to note, however, that the opposite is not true: if in one of the games the state is marked with a “+” sign, then the state of the sum of games is not necessarily also positive. An example is Nim itself, which can be thought of as the sum of "trivial nims" with one pile. For a non-empty pile, each of the games is in the “+” state (the first one wins in one move), while the sum can also be in the minus state. So, if new k piles of stones are added to the Nim game with m piles and the state of the Nim game on the added k piles corresponds to the sign “–”, then the state of the new game with m + k piles corresponds to the same sign as the state of the original game with m piles. Indeed, a new game is the sum of two games, in one of which the state is obviously “minus”. If the state of another game is also marked with a “–” sign, then the state of the sum of games is “minus”, if “+” is “plus”. Heaps can not only be added, but also deleted. If k piles are removed from m piles, and the state of the Nim game on these k piles corresponds to the “–” sign, then the state of the original Nim game with m piles corresponds to the same sign as the state of the new Nim game with m – k piles. This property allows, when assessing the state of the Nim game, to replace it with a game with fewer piles, but preserving the signs assigned to the states. Indeed, in the game Nim with m heaps, let us select arbitrary 2 heaps. Let there be a and b stones in them. Let's add to the existing m piles 3 more piles, consisting of a, b and c F a, b stones. The added 3 piles form a game with a minus state. In the resulting game with m + 3 heaps, two pairs of heaps with the same number of stones appeared: a pair of a and a and a pair of b and b. A pair of piles with the same number of stones forms a minus state in the game Nim with 2 piles. Such a pair can be removed without changing the sign of the game state. As a result, we replaced two piles with a and b stones with one pile containing c F a, b stones. The number of piles decreased by one, but the sign assigned to the state of the game did not change.
