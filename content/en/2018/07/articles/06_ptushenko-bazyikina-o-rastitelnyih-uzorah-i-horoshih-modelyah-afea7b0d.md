About plant patterns and good models

V. PTUSHENKO, A. BAZYKINA

ON PAGE 4 OF THE COVER OF THE EIGHTH issue «Kvant» for last year there was a photograph of a simple pattern formed by moss under a wooden bench. In the accompanying text it was mentioned that the reason for its appearance can be various factors: the availability of light, moisture and the effect of trampling. This “toy” situation is actually a good model for much larger and more complex problems of ecology and biogeography - problems of the distribution of different species of plants and animals across regions of the Earth. For some plants, their distribution area is limited by the amount of available moisture in the form of precipitation during the growing season. Others have adapted to dry conditions through deep root systems or a very short development period, but require specific temperatures throughout the year. Still others are “tied” to the distribution of their pollinators or seed dispersers (a classic example is coconut palms, which grow mainly in coastal areas and have fruits that float freely on the water and are carried far by ocean currents). In addition, competition with other species plays an important role: remember how plants from “warm regions” grow well at higher latitudes in open ground in botanical gardens, where the competition factor is excluded. However, in all these cases, the area of ​​distribution is determined by the play of a number of factors. And it is not always possible to single out the main one - the so-called limiting factor. Let's look again at the example we started with: the uneven growth of moss under the bench (Fig.1). How could one determine what is the limiting factor for moss growth here: light or moisture?

It would be possible to observe this piece of land during the summer, systematically measure the light flux entering its different parts, and determine the soil moisture. Then average all the data, obtain “maps” of the distribution of light and humidity and compare them with the “map” of moss growth. This will be an observational study - the most cautious, i.e. minimally affecting the system under study, but at the same time labor-intensive and not always allowing for an unambiguous interpretation. You can carry out an experimental study: place illuminators near the bench that create uniform illumination of the entire space under it during the day, but do not prevent water from entering the ground through the cracks between the boards during rain. Or, conversely, evenly moisten the ground under the bench without changing the conditions of light and shadow, and observe how the figure formed by the moss changes. But even after carefully performing such research, you may not get a definite answer. Imagine, for example, that a certain range of soil moisture is fundamental to the growth of vegetation. Moreover, this humidity is determined not by the penetration of moisture under the bench through the cracks in it during rain, but by the penetration of sunlight through the same cracks, which dries out the soil (and in the shade it remains moister). And then the distribution of moisture under the bench will be determined by... figure light and shadow! Similar problems occur quite often in real research. They don't necessarily make the problem unsolvable, but they can make it very difficult to solve. And therefore, those experimental models that allow us to see a clear, unambiguous solution are always especially valuable. The photograph shown here on figure 2 and on page 4 of the cover of this issue provides an example of just such a successful model of a plant community exposed to a range of changing growing conditions. The steps of the Venetian embankment, going down to the water, are overgrown with a dense green “carpet”. It is obvious that the illumination of all steps is almost
