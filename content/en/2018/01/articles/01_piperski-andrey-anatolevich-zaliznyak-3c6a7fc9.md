DECEMBER 2017, AT THE AGE OF 82, the great Russian linguist, academician of the Russian Academy of Sciences Andrei Anatolyevich Zaliznyak (1935–2017) passed away. He has many outstanding discoveries to his credit. He studied the ancient Novgorod birch bark letters and described the grammar of the ancient Novgorod dialect; he convincingly proved that “The Tale of Igor’s Campaign” was indeed written around 1200, and is not a forgery of the 18th century, as many previously believed; he created a dictionary of Russian inflections, which to this day forms the basis of computer programs working with the Russian language; he developed and refined the definition of case given by A.N. Kolmogorov - and much more. The rigor and evidence-based work of Andrei Anatolyevich was and remains an ideal for all his students - and almost all linguists in Moscow and beyond consider themselves to be such.

A distinctive feature of Zaliznyak’s scientific activity is his interest in mathematics and precise methods in linguistics. In the 1950s, he studied at the French department of the Faculty of Philology of Moscow University - it would seem that what could be more humanitarian and far from the exact sciences? However, he began to go to V.A. Uspensky for mathematics classes for philologists and was among the best students. “He absolutely shocked me. He asked some questions, extremely deep, but completely perpendicular to everyone else's questions and to what I could expect. There was some kind of twist in his questions, as if he was looking at everything from a different perspective. It immediately became clear that he was a genius,” recalls $Успенский.^{1}$ Soon Zaliznyak became one of the leaders of a seminar on mathematical linguistics at the Faculty of Mechanics and Mathematics of Moscow State University. I would like to talk in more detail about some of Zaliznyak’s achievements related to the formalization of linguistic knowledge.

Russian inflection In 1957–1958, Zaliznyak was on an internship in Paris. There he taught Russian to French students and discovered that the existing descriptions of Russian declension and conjugation were not enough for foreigners: they were too vague. Already in 1961, Zaliznyak published a “Concise Russian-French educational dictionary”, appending to it an absolutely formal 150-page essay on Russian inflection. In 1965, this essay grew into a Ph.D. thesis; the work turned out to be so innovative that it was immediately counted as a doctoral thesis. In 1967, Zaliznyak published the book “Russian nominal inflection,” and in 1977, “Grammar Dictionary of the Russian Language.”

In this dictionary, each word has unusual markings: oak m 1c, threshold m 3a, lantern m 2b. It’s clear that “m” denotes the masculine gender, but what’s the point of everything else? After all, we already know from school years that oak, threshold and lantern are the second declension. However, such knowledge is not enough to fully describe the Russian language. To make sure of this, we form the genitive case of the singular and the nominative case of the plural: no deb - there are oaks ', no poruga - there are porugi, no lantern ' - there are lanterns'. It can be seen that different letters appear at the endings of these words (in one - a and y, in the other - a and i, in the third - i and i), and with the stress everything is not simple: for the word oak it falls either on the ending or on the stem, in the word threshold it falls in both forms on the stem, and in the word lantern it falls on the ending. It is this information that litters carry. The numbers indicate what the stem ends with and what the endings will look like: 1 – stem with a hard consonant (except for the back-lingual k, g and x, hissing sh and zh and whistling c); 2 – base on a soft consonant (except h and th); 3 – base on k, g and x, etc. There are nine such types of nouns: 0 – indeclinable words (for example-

Now the purpose of such sorting seems far-fetched: you can take a digitized version of the dictionary and select all the words starting with -nut in a split second, but in 1977 there was no such possibility. However, with the spread of computers, a new use for the “Grammar Dictionary” was discovered: it is on it that programs that work with Russian morphology are based. Without a dictionary, modern search engines and voice assistants would function much worse. For example, by entering Pushkin's poems into the search, you get pages on which Pushkin's poems are written. These are not just words that differ by 1-2 letters (otherwise there would be pages with the words guns and elements, but this does not happen), but precisely the forms of words built according to Zaliznyak’s models. And a voice assistant without a dictionary would not construct the genitive case of the word lantern, and if it simply voiced the text, it would not have learned that it is necessary to read lantern ’, and not phonbrya. It is easy to understand how morphological synthesis works based on the Zaliznyak dictionary. If we build forms from the word lantern, we need to take its mark (2b), choose from the endings of type 2 the one that expresses the desired combination of case and number: for example -я for gender. units - and find out in the introductory part of the dictionary that the stem of words of type 2 is obtained by discarding the final b. In an effort to describe the language in an optimal way, Zaliznyak posed and solved mathematical problems. One of them is suggested by the example with which we began. Let us recall that the word lantern has the stress pattern b, i.e. The emphasis is always on the ending. Before you read further, think: is there some contradiction here that needs to be resolved? Let's ask ourselves a strange question: where does the emphasis fall in the shape of a lantern - on the base or on the ending? There are three possible answers to this question: 1) to the stem: it is clear that the vowel a is included in the stem, and the ending here is zero; 2) we don’t know where: the stem and ending do not compete for stress here, since the ending is zero; 3) to the ending, but let’s introduce an additional rule: if the stress falls on the zero ending (and it turns out that there is nowhere for it to sound), it is transferred to the stem. Let’s denote the stress on the base by +, the stress on the ending by –, and “we don’t know” by 0.
