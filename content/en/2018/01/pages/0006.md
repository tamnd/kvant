When calculating the upper one, it was assumed that the frequency of satellite launches, which had decreased by the first decade, would remain so for a long time. This will lead, as we see, to the rapid accumulation of space debris. The lower curve shows the forecast under a completely fantastic assumption: what will happen if we completely stop space launches. This will happen due to mutual collisions and the division of large fragments into smaller ones, but nevertheless dangerous. Astronomers are busy, as well as developing methods for cleaning near space. Here, astronomers also have ideas that arose from observing the movement of comets and asteroids. By the way – about them. Comets and asteroids are, in a sense, also space debris of natural origin. And if we don’t want it to suddenly fall on us, we need to keep an eye on it. Astronomers are trying to do this, discovering hundreds of new objects in the Solar System every day, mostly small asteroids. In total, about a million of them have already been recorded, and all of them are being monitored and their movements in the future are being calculated. But we cannot yet establish total control over all dangerous bodies. This requires special telescopes - ground-based and space-based - with a large field of view. Now they are in the design stage, and some are already in the construction stage. With their help, astronomers, although they will not be able to protect the Earth, will be able to warn of impending danger. Another important area of ​​astroecology is monitoring the stars and especially the closest of them, our Sun. We still have little idea of ​​how the activity of the Sun changes over large time intervals, and the future of humanity depends on the forecast of this activity. The capabilities of solar astronomy are growing rapidly. Until recently, we observed only one hemisphere of the Sun, currently visible from the Earth, and did not know what surprises the other hemisphere was preparing for us, which in two weeks would turn in our direction (the Sun rotates with a period of about a month). But now space telescopes monitor the Sun from all directions, including the opposite direction, and in all ranges of the electromagnetic spectrum. So the possibilities of short-term forecasting of its activity have increased sharply. But there are still many problems regarding the long-term forecast.
