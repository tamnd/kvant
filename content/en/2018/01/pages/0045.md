The comparison of star luminosities with their spectral types was first done at the beginning of the 20th century by Einar Hertzsprung and Henry Russell, which is why the spectral luminosity diagram is often called the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram. On this diagram (see figure), spectral classes or effective temperatures are plotted along the abscissa axis, and luminosities L or absolute magnitudes M are plotted along the ordinate axis. If there were no relationship between luminosities and temperatures, then all stars would be distributed evenly on such a diagram. But in a real diagram, several patterns are found, which are called sequences. Luminosity is a physical characteristic of a star, indicating how much energy it loses per unit time. The Sun's luminosity is about 4 $10^{26}$ J s, supergiants have greater luminosity. They can be blue-white, such as Rigel (E Orionis), with a surface temperature of 12,130 K, or red supergiants, such as Betelgeuse (D Orionis), with a surface temperature of 3,590 K. Most stars (about 90%) lie along a long, narrow stripe called the main sequence on the diagram. It stretches from the upper left corner (from blue supergiants) to the lower right corner (to red dwarfs). The main sequence stars include the Sun, whose luminosity is taken to be unity.
