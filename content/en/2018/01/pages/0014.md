Since the diagonals are not parallel, they intersect at one point. Conclusion. How did going into space help here? Why couldn't the same proof be carried out without leaving the plane? The fact is that if three lines that do not lie in the same plane intersect in pairs, then all three intersect at one point (fact 2). But if they lie in the same plane, then this is incorrect! Example: lines containing the sides of any triangle. Therefore, from the fact that the diagonals of a hexagon intersect in pairs, it would not follow that they intersect at one point. In this sense, there are fewer possibilities in space than in a plane. We went out into space and “raised” the diagonals $A_{1} A_{4}, A_{2} A_{5}$ and $A_{3} A_{6}$ to the segments $B_{1} B_{4}, B_{2} B_{5}, B_{3} B_{6}$. Having proved that these segments intersect in pairs, we thereby prove their intersection at one point.

Configurations of points and lines

Projective geometry studies facts and properties of figures that do not change under central projections, i.e. projections from a fixed point. Many projective problems are solved by entering space. There are reasons for this that we will not discuss here; it would take us too far into the foundations of projective geometry. Let us just say that very difficult planimetric problems about the configurations of points and lines sometimes turn into very simple “spatial” statements. Problem 5 (polars relative to angles).
