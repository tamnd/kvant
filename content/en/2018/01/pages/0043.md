Let us illustrate the application of Theorem 5 with examples. Problem 7 (E. Bakaev, based on problem M2460). The grasshopper can jump along a checkered strip 60 cells long by 8, 9 or 10 cells in any direction. Prove that a grasshopper cannot, starting from a certain cell, bypass this stripe by visiting each cell exactly once. Solution. As in Problem 5, consider the “grasshopper graph”: put the vertices at the centers of the cells, and connect with an edge each pair of cells between which the grasshopper can jump. And after their removal, 30 “black islands” remain: almost all black vertices are isolated, except for two “islands” of two black cells. By Theorem 5, no path in our graph can contain vertices from all 30 “islands”. Thus, at least one black vertex was not passed. Problem 8. From a board 10 x 10, cut out a middle square of 6 x 6. Prove that the remaining part of the board cannot be bypassed by a knight, visiting each square exactly once. Solution idea. Let's select 12 white cells, and in the other cells of our “edge” we will place numbers from 1 to 14, as shown in figure 8. Note that numbering has the following property: if you cut out white cells, then the knight will be able to move from any cell only to a cell with the same number. The cell numbers determine the “islands” from Theorem 5.

Exercise 8. Complete the solution of this problem by reducing it to Theorem 5.

Problem 8, just discussed, is a special case problem M118 from the “Problem Book «Kvant» («Kvant» No. 12 for 1971), in which other similar formulations of the problem are also interesting, for example: “Add the minimum number of edges in the graph so that a Hamiltonian path appears in it.”

Exercise 9. Given a graph. Let h be the minimum number of edges that can be added so that a Hamiltonian path appears in the graph. We call a set of paths covering if each vertex of the graph belongs to exactly one of these paths (paths of length 0 are allowed that contain exactly one vertex). Let p be the minimum number for which there is a covering set of p paths. Prove that h = p – 1.

In conclusion, let's look at a few examples where finding Hamiltonian (or simply long) paths in a graph can be the main step in a short solution to a problem. Problem 9 (XIX Tournament of Cities).
