It was not for nothing that Zaliznyak chose this order. Thanks to this, words with the same right side appear next to each other in the dictionary, i.e. those that change similarly: verbs ending in -nut, nouns ending in -a, etc. In our example you can see: 7 words of the traditional 1st declension are located in a row, then 7 words of the 2nd declension and then 2 more words of the 1st declension. Now the purpose of such sorting seems far-fetched: you can take a digitized version of the dictionary and select all the words starting with -nut in a split second, but in 1977 there was no such possibility. However, with the spread of computers, a new use for the “Grammar Dictionary” was discovered: it is on it that programs that work with Russian morphology are based. Without a dictionary, modern search engines and voice assistants would function much worse. For example, by entering Pushkin's poems into the search, you get pages on which Pushkin's poems are written. These are not just words that differ by 1-2 letters (otherwise there would be pages with the words guns and elements, but this does not happen), but precisely the forms of words built according to Zaliznyak’s models. And a voice assistant without a dictionary would not construct the genitive case of the word lantern, and if it simply voiced the text, it would not have learned that it is necessary to read lantern ’, and not phonbrya. It is easy to understand how morphological synthesis works based on the Zaliznyak dictionary. If we build forms from the word lantern, we need to take its mark (2b), choose from the endings of type 2 the one that expresses the desired combination of case and number: for example -я for gender. units - and find out in the introductory part of the dictionary that the stem of words of type 2 is obtained by discarding the final b. The result is: lantern- + -i = lantern. It’s a little more difficult with words that have several stems: for example, the word cucumber has the mark 5*b,

The opposite task - relying on the “Grammar Dictionary”, to understand from a string of characters whether it is a form of any Russian word, and if so, what forms it could be and from which words - is more difficult to solve from a computational point of view. It is required not only to store all possible sets of endings and markings for each word, but also to construct in advance all possible stems and write down in what form which of the stems is used. Then we can propose the following algorithm: we break the word into two parts in all possible ways and see whether the first part is the basis of any words. If yes, we check whether the second part of these words has an ending with such a basis, and if so, then we write down those words and forms in which this is possible. One can, of course, say: why all these complex notations, stress patterns, alternation of stems, etc.? Let's just write out all the forms with stress for each word, and we're done. But this is very uneconomical: each noun will have 12 forms, and some verbs will have more than 100 (counting four participles in all genders, numbers and cases). And the “Grammar Dictionary” is not such a thick book, although it has a large format: in the 4th edition, the description of Russian inflections takes up 118 pages, followed by 586 pages of the dictionary itself.
