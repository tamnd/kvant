Here are the words of Einstein himself in his wonderful popular book (written jointly with L. Infeld) “The Evolution of Physics” (1938): “Newton’s law of gravity connects the motion of a body here and now with the motion of another body at the same time at a distant distance... Our new laws are structural laws that describe the change in the gravitational field. They connect events that are happening now and here with events that will happen or have already happened at other times and places. Schematically, we can say that the transition from Newton’s law of gravitation to the general theory of relativity is to some extent analogous to the transition from the laws of electrostatics and magnetostatics to Maxwell’s theory.” The validity of Einstein's theory of gravity has been proven many times. It is enough at least for the fact that all accurate calculations of the trajectories of spacecraft in near-Earth and near-solar space are based not on the simple Newtonian law of gravity, but on the equations of the general theory of relativity. And navigation systems such as GPS, Glonass, etc., without using the general relativity equations, would give an error in determining the coordinates of an earthly object of the order of 10 kilometers, and not 5 meters, as has been achieved now. General relativity is absolutely necessary when we are talking about huge masses (stars, galaxies and the entire Universe). In addition, General Relativity predicts phenomena that are absent in Newtonian theory. It is these phenomena that gravitational waves belong to. Just as from Maxwell's equations for the electromagnetic field, the existence of electromagnetic waves became a necessary consequence, and from the analysis of Einstein's gravitational equations it followed that gravitational waves must exist. stars (pulsars), astrophysicists recorded a significant decrease in the total angular momentum of this system (Nobel Prize in Physics 1993). This “loss” could only be explained by the loss of mass-energy during the merger of stars, carried away by a gravitational wave. Another example is the presence of so-called gravitational lenses. Theorists have calculated that a gravitational wave, by changing the space-time metric, can distort the radiation of any cosmic object, just as a lens distorts the path of light rays. And such distortions were recorded by astronomers. But there was no direct, “terrestrial” experimental evidence of the existence of gravitational waves. The problem is the weakness of this wave signal reaching the Earth. The energy of the signal that reached Earth on September 14, 2015 was at least $10^{51}$ times less than the energy of this wave at its birth. If we draw an analogy with the sound from a shock wave, it is the same as detonating one millionth of a gram of explosive at a distance of ten meters. For the human ear, it will not be a “scream” or even a faint crackling sound - it will not react at all to such a weak sound. However, attempts by physicists to catch the signal of a gravitational wave began back in the 60s of the last century. The first to express the idea of ​​capturing a gravitational wave and try to implement it was the American physicist J. Weber in 1969. The idea was simple - with sensitive sensors to measure the deformations of massive bodies that should occur when a gravitational wave, distorting the space-time metric, passes through these bodies. The bodies were aluminum cylinders weighing one and a half tons with belts of dozens of piezoelectric strain sensors. The sensitivity of the sensors was unusually high - up to $10^{-} 15$ m (the size of atomic nuclei). Weber stated that he recorded the waves. But this turned out to be a mistake. Most likely, these were signals from numerous interferences - vibrations from extraneous sources, for example from transport.
