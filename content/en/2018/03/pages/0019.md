Under Barish's leadership, two identical observatories were built in 1994–1998 - in Hanford and in Livingston at a distance of 3000 km from each other. The interferometric system was unable to recognize events that would be characteristic of gravitational waves. Theorists under Thorne's leadership have done a great deal of work recalculating the amplitudes of possible deformations from gravitational waves. They turned out to be even smaller than previously thought, namely on the order of $10^{-} 20 - 10^{-} 21$ m. At first glance, recording deformations with such accuracy might seem impossible. After all, it’s the same as measuring the distance to the nearest stars with an error of one tenth of a millimeter. But the project leaders did not give up. This is exactly what happened on September 14, 2015, when gravitational waves were detected for the first time - within half an hour all participants in the collaboration received a message about this event. Careful signal processing and calculation of the energy of black holes began. Astrophysicists began to check all the data on radiation in this area of ​​​​the sky from where the wave came, and indeed they discovered powerful short-term electromagnetic radiation, which could only be explained by the pull of masses into two powerful converging black holes (after all, the black holes themselves cannot be seen). It is quite possible that without the “hint” of physicists from LIGO, astrophysicists would have missed these emissions - they were very short-lived. But now the ends meet, and the event recorded on September 14, 2015 is considered the first reliable evidence of the existence of not only gravitational waves, but also black holes! The identification work lasted several months, and on February 11, 2016, the discovery of gravitational waves was announced at a press conference at CERN in Geneva. On the same day, a special issue of the most authoritative physics journal, Physical Review Letters, was published, where this discovery was announced. Over the past two years, four more gravitational wave detections have occurred. The registration on August 17, 2017 was especially interesting. On this day, a gravitational wave from the merger of two neutron stars was recorded for the first time, and at the same time this event was observed by astronomers as the transformation of two stars into one. And here the Nobel Committee on Physics had nowhere to go. The discovery became a fact and was unanimously recognized as deserving of the 2017 Nobel Prize.
