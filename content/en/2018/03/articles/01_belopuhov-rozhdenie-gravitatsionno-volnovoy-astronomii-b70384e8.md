The Birth of Gravitational Wave Astronomy

L. BELOPUKHOV

In 2017, the Nobel Prize in Physics “for decisive contributions to the development of the LIGO (Laser Interferometer Gravitational-Wave Observatory) detector and the observation of gravitational waves” was received by American scientists Rainer Weiss, Barry Barish and Kip Thorne. One billion three hundred million years ago, at a great distance from the Earth, two solid black holes, in their circling around the Universe, came to such a distance that the force of their gravitational interaction began to actively attract them to each other. One of them had a mass 36 times greater than the mass of the Sun, the other - 29 times more. As they approached, they rotated around their center of mass so that they moved as if in a spiral. The speed of this rotation eventually became enormous - one revolution every few milliseconds. And finally, the black holes merged, emitting a short but terrible sound.

The news of this event came to Earth on September 14, 2015 at 9 hours 50 minutes 45 seconds UTC international time (different from Moscow time by three hours ago). It was recorded in the USA by two LIGO collaboration installations located at a distance of 3000 km from each other. The scientific explanation for the birth of gravitational waves is given by GTR - Einstein's general theory of relativity. In essence, this is a theory of gravity taking into account the special theory of relativity and with the refusal to be bound by the Euclidean geometry so familiar to us. The brief essence of GTR is that any mass distorts and “bends” the geometry around itself, turning it into non-Euclidean (Riemannian). In this case, space and time cannot in general be separated from each other and geometry must be considered as the geometry of a four-dimensional world,

the specific properties of which are determined by the gravitational forces of the masses located in this world. The mathematical side of this theory is the gravitational field equations. Here are the words of Einstein himself in his wonderful popular book (written jointly with L. Infeld) “The Evolution of Physics” (1938): “Newton’s law of gravity connects the motion of a body here and now with the motion of another body at the same time at a distant distance... Our new laws are structural laws that describe the change in the gravitational field. They connect events that are happening now and here with events that will happen or have already happened at other times and places. Schematically, we can say that the transition from Newton’s law of gravitation to the general theory of relativity is to some extent analogous to the transition from the laws of electrostatics and magnetostatics to Maxwell’s theory.” The validity of Einstein's theory of gravity has been proven many times. It is enough at least for the fact that all accurate calculations of the trajectories of spacecraft in near-Earth and near-solar space are based not on the simple Newtonian law of gravity, but on the equations of the general theory of relativity. And navigation systems such as GPS, Glonass, etc., without using the general relativity equations, would give an error in determining the coordinates of an earthly object of the order of 10 kilometers, and not 5 meters, as has been achieved now. General relativity is absolutely necessary when we are talking about huge masses (stars, galaxies and the entire Universe). In addition, General Relativity predicts phenomena that are absent in Newtonian theory. It is these phenomena that gravitational waves belong to. Just as from Maxwell's equations for the electromagnetic field, the existence of electromagnetic waves became a necessary consequence, and from the analysis of Einstein's gravitational equations it followed that gravitational waves must exist. Indirect evidence of the presence of gravitational waves appeared towards the end of the twentieth century. For example, when two neutrons merge

stars (pulsars), astrophysicists recorded a significant decrease in the total angular momentum of this system (Nobel Prize in Physics 1993). This “loss” could only be explained by the loss of mass-energy during the merger of stars, carried away by a gravitational wave. Another example is the presence of so-called gravitational lenses. Theorists have calculated that a gravitational wave, by changing the space-time metric, can distort the radiation of any cosmic object, just as a lens distorts the path of light rays. And such distortions were recorded by astronomers. But there was no direct, “terrestrial” experimental evidence of the existence of gravitational waves. The problem is the weakness of this wave signal reaching the Earth. The energy of the signal that reached Earth on September 14, 2015 was at least $10^{51}$ times less than the energy of this wave at its birth. If we draw an analogy with the sound from a shock wave, it is the same as detonating one millionth of a gram of explosive at a distance of ten meters. For the human ear, it will not be a “scream” or even a faint crackling sound - it will not react at all to such a weak sound. However, attempts by physicists to catch the signal of a gravitational wave began back in the 60s of the last century. The first to express the idea of ​​capturing a gravitational wave and try to implement it was the American physicist J. Weber in 1969. The idea was simple - with sensitive sensors to measure the deformations of massive bodies that should occur when a gravitational wave, distorting the space-time metric, passes through these bodies. The bodies were aluminum cylinders weighing one and a half tons with belts of dozens of piezoelectric strain sensors. The sensitivity of the sensors was unusually high - up to $10^{-} 15$ m (the size of atomic nuclei). Weber stated that he recorded the waves. But this turned out to be a mistake. Most likely, these were signals from numerous interferences - vibrations from extraneous sources, for example from transport.

According to Weber’s scheme, similar experiments began to be carried out in a number of countries, including Russia (V.B. Braginsky, Moscow State University). The result was negative everywhere, since astrophysicists’ calculations showed that deformations under the influence of a gravitational wave should be significantly less than $10^{-} 15$ m. And there were no sensors that could measure such small values. In the 60s, another idea appeared for measuring such small deformations - using interference. The idea was put forward by Soviet physicists from Moscow State University M.E. Herzenstein and V.I. Pustovoit in 1962. But at a seminar at the Physical Institute of the USSR Academy of Sciences, the idea was criticized, and the corresponding article was not recommended for publication. A few years later, the idea of ​​using the interferometric method arose again, this time in the USA. Rainer Weiss at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology in 1967 began to develop a project for a laser interferometer that could overcome “noise” from all extraneous sources (thermal, seismic, gravitational sources of interference, instability of the frequency of laser radiation, and even possible interference from geomagnetic storms and bursts of cosmic radiation). He calculated that the interferometer should have colossal dimensions - several kilometers. It was the further development of the Weiss project that led to the creation of LIGO, the laser interferometric gravitational-wave observatory, which was destined to make the discovery. In 1968, at the California Institute of Technology, theoretical astrophysicist Kip Thorne began investigating possible sources of gravitational waves. He identified three sources - the merger of two black holes, the merger of binary pulsars (neutron stars) and the final stage of a supernova explosion with the formation of a neutron star at its center. He made estimates of the energy of gravitational waves and modeled the nature of the signal itself arriving at Earth. This should be a piece of a non-standard sinusoid with increasing amplitude and frequency and an abrupt termination of the signal. Length

The strength of the signal, its amplitude and frequency depend on the type and energy of the wave source. It became clear what signal to look for. In 1970, physicist Ronald Drever moved to California from Scotland, working at the University of Glasgow. He built an interference detector according to his design at the same time as Weiss. They were not competitors, but colleagues and friends, and from the moment LIGO was created they worked together. Unfortunately, R. Drever, who died in March 2017, did not live six months before the Nobel Prize was awarded to his friend. The third Nobel laureate was experimental physicist Barry Barish, who worked as one of the leaders of the Enrico Fermi National Accelerator Laboratory Fermilab (near Chicago), and since 1993 a professor at the California Institute of Technology. Weiss and Drever's project progressed at a slow pace - there were too many scientific and engineering problems to solve. And although over 15 years of work, Weiss and Drever’s group expanded to 40 people, this was clearly not enough. What was missing was a physicist with engineering thinking and experience in organizational and administrative work. Barish was just such a person. He reworked the project from an engineering perspective and in a few years created a large international team - the LIGO collaboration, where he attracted research groups from different countries (Italy, Germany, Japan, Russia) with the necessary experience. His truly frantic energy, character that allows him to work well with people of very different personalities, and ability to secure financing for the project ensured the creation of the collaboration. Today, LIGO employs over 1,200 researchers from 110 scientific organizations in 18 countries. Russian participants - a group of V.B. Braginsky from Moscow State University and a group from the Nizhny Novgorod Institute of Applied Physics of the Russian Academy of Sciences. According to the project leaders, Vladimir Borisovich Braginsky made a huge contribution to the discovery of gravitational waves. Unfortunately, he passed away on March 29, 2016. It capped his more than ten years of efforts to bring the laser interferometry project to completion. Under Barish's leadership, two identical observatories were built in 1994–1998 - in Hanford and in Livingston at a distance of 3000 km from each other. By 2002, laser interferometers were installed there. But the very first measurements showed that the project needed serious reworking. The interferometric system was unable to recognize events that would be characteristic of gravitational waves. Theorists under Thorne's leadership have done a great deal of work recalculating the amplitudes of possible deformations from gravitational waves. They turned out to be even smaller than previously thought, namely on the order of $10^{-} 20 - 10^{-} 21$ m. At first glance, recording deformations with such accuracy might seem impossible. After all, it’s the same as measuring the distance to the nearest stars with an error of one tenth of a millimeter. But the project leaders did not give up. They turned out to be optimists who believed in the abilities of their team, and Barry Barish again managed to secure funding for the project. The project update has begun. The use of a more powerful laser, the creation by V.B. Braginsky’s group of an improved triple mirror suspension system, the development by mathematicians from Moscow State University of software for automatic registration of events, and much more required another ten years of hard work. In 2014, regular and then constant operation of the installations began, interrupted only by stops to recreate the necessary vacuum in the pipes. This is exactly what happened on September 14, 2015, when gravitational waves were detected for the first time - within half an hour all participants in the collaboration received a message about this event. Careful signal processing and calculation of the energy of black holes began. Astrophysicists began to check all the data on radiation in this area of ​​​​the sky from where the wave came, and indeed they discovered powerful short-term electromagnetic radiation, which could only be explained by the pull of masses into two powerful converging black holes (after all, the black holes themselves cannot be seen). It is quite possible that without the “hint” of physicists from LIGO, astrophysicists would have missed these emissions - they were very short-lived. But now the ends meet, and the event recorded on September 14, 2015 is considered the first reliable evidence of the existence of not only gravitational waves, but also black holes! The identification work lasted several months, and on February 11, 2016, the discovery of gravitational waves was announced at a press conference at CERN in Geneva. On the same day, a special issue of the most authoritative physics journal, Physical Review Letters, was published, where this discovery was announced. Over the past two years, four more gravitational wave detections have occurred. The registration on August 17, 2017 was especially interesting. On this day, a gravitational wave from the merger of two neutron stars was recorded for the first time, and at the same time this event was observed by astronomers as the transformation of two stars into one. And here the Nobel Committee on Physics had nowhere to go. The discovery became a fact and was unanimously recognized as deserving of the 2017 Nobel Prize.
