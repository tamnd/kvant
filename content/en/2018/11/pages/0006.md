A fresh look at the problem allowed him to achieve such success. In de Gray's work, the distance graph with chromatic number 5 had 1581 vertices. Now graphs with 553 vertices have already been found [6]. And there are brave souls hoping to find graphs with chromatic number 6! In this article we will talk about how di Gray's result is obtained.

About the benefits of a fresh look

Let's see how the simplest distance graphs with chromatic number 4 are constructed. This is the Moser spindle, invented by the brothers Leo and William

Mosers, and Count Golomba. The Moser spindle has two “diamond” subgraphs, consisting of two equilateral triangles with a common side. For any coloring of the vertices of such a subgraph in 3 colors, the points M, N have the same color (Fig. 5,a). This means that in the Moser spindle the points P, Q, R are the same color, but Q and R are connected by an edge (Fig.5,b). Can we get a contradiction in the same way when there are not three colors, but four? In other words, construct a graph such that it contains a pair of vertices whose colors coincide under any correct coloring. Then it would be proven that the plane is not painted in four colors. But how to construct such a graph is not clear.

⟦column⟧

Let us split the Golomba graph into two subgraphs (Fig.6,a). The first one is a hexagon containing six equilateral triangles. Up to permutations of colors, it is painted in three colors in a unique way (Fig.6, b) This means that the vertices of an equilateral triangle A c B c C c with side 3 are colored in this case in one color. Here and below we denote $'_{3}$ – a set consisting of the vertices of an equilateral triangle with side 3. In this case, the location of the triangle on the plane can be any. In the second subgraph, the vertices of such an equilateral triangle are connected to the vertices of an equilateral triangle with side 1 (Fig.6,c). When coloring in three colors, the points X, Y, Z are multi-colored. Then A cc, B cc, C cc cannot be the same color, since it will coincide with the color of one of the points X, Y, Z. Thus, in the Golomb graph, the coloring of two subgraphs leads to a contradiction: the first of them guarantees that the vertices $'_{3}$ will be of the same color, and the second excludes this possibility. Now we are interested in the distance graph, which cannot be colored in four colors. Maybe it would be easier to look not for the entire graph, but for subgraphs whose coloring leads to contradictions? In the first subgraph, a certain set of vertices, a “template”, will be monochromatic for any correct coloring, and in the second subgraph it is always multi-colored. Or this: if in the first subgraph there is at least one single-color pattern, and in the second subgraph it cannot be colored in one color, then this will certainly lead to a contradiction when coloring the plane. (On the one hand, a one-color pattern must be found somewhere, but on the other hand, it is forbidden - after all, a second graph can be attached to any of its locations on the plane.)
