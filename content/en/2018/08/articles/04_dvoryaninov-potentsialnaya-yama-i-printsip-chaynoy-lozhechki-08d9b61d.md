Each of us observes a potential hole almost every day. Imagine, for example, that there is a glass of tea with a teaspoon inside and a saucer on the table. After stirring the tea, you want to place the spoon on the saucer. To do this, you need to do work - raise the spoon higher than the glass, overcome the potential barrier. The position of the spoon both in the glass and on the saucer is stable. If tea drinking takes place, say, in the carriage of a moving train, then small shocks or shocks will leave the spoon in place both in the glass and on the saucer. In this simplest situation, the bottom of the glass and the saucer are two potential holes, and the spoon is a ball rolling from one hole to another. It turns out that such an abstract “spoon” is present in many structures around us, where instead of the potential energy of gravitational interaction with the Earth, we must consider the potential energy of a deformable spring. If, when the system deviates from the equilibrium position, a force appears that returns the system to its original state, then this equilibrium position is called stable. An example is a ball located in a hole (or hollow). To compress or stretch a spring, some work is required. This work results in an increase in the potential energy stored in the spring. A deformed spring tends to return to its original undeformed state, which corresponds to a minimum of potential energy. The undeformed state of the spring is stable because its stretching or compression causes the appearance of an elastic force, which tends to return the spring (and the system associated with it) to its original state. And now - the design task. figure 7 shows two tea jars. Their locks operate according to the principle outlined in our article. Where is the spring here? It turns out that the role of a spring is played by an elastic rubber gasket reinforced around the perimeter of the lid (at the same time, this gasket ensures the tightness of the can). When closing the jar, the lid is first pressed against it, and then slightly (about a millimeter) rises up. Both levers are so rigid that they practically do not deform. The gasket, being deformed and then freed from external influence, easily lifts the lid. If the gasket is removed, the system stops working.
