Gorbachev’s meeting with US President Donald Reagan at the end of 1986 in Reykjavik (Iceland), halfway between Moscow and Washington. Reagan was an extraordinary politician. A former famous film actor, he was an educated man and had good advisers. In one interview, when asked by a journalist about what guides his foreign and domestic policies, Reagan answered: “Niels Bohr’s principle of complementarity.” And he explained to journalists this principle, based on the quantum uncertainty relation. It is not known whether the journalists understood Reagan, but one of the results of the meeting between the leaders of the two countries was the creation, under the auspices of the IAEA (International Atomic Energy Agency), of a group of scientists developing the possibility of creating a new large scientific international community. French President Francois Mitterrand also took a big part in this. In 1987, this group became the International Advisory Committee of the ITER project, and this committee was headed by Academician B.B. Kadomtsev. An international advisory committee prepared the conclusion in Washington in July 1992 of an international quadripartite agreement on the development of the ITER engineering project. The parties to this agreement were Russia, the USA, Japan and the EU (European Commonwealth). A very difficult issue was financing the development of the project. His personal contacts with all the leading reactor physicists and his undisputed scientific authority in this field ensured a successful, albeit not very quick, solution to the problem. Finally, in May 2006, an agreement was reached in Brussels on the creation of an International Organization for the implementation of the project, which will have the rights of a legal entity capable of concluding agreements with states and international organizations. In June 2006, in Moscow, the first six countries participating in this agreement determined the site for the construction of the reactor. In September 2006, the Russian government decided to support this agreement, thereby Russia became a participating country. In December of this year, several dozen contracts were already concluded with specific project implementers. A governing body was created - the ITER Council. At first, its general directors were Japanese reactor physicists - after all, it was in Japan that the most advanced reactor, Tokamak-16, was created. Russian scientist, academician E.P. Velikhov became the main scientific director of the project. In 2015, a 65-year-old man was elected general director of the ITER Council.
