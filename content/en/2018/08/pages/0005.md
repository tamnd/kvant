In the atomic nucleus, there are electrical repulsive forces between protons (Coulomb forces) and special attractive forces between nucleons, called nuclear forces. In modern physics, interactions corresponding to nuclear forces are preferred to be called strong interactions. They have nothing to do with the electrical charge of the particles. Objects endowed with a special property—baryon charge—have strong interactions. It is the same for both protons and neutrons and is considered equal to unity for them. Thus, the total baryon charge of the nucleus is equal to the number of nucleons in it. It is this value that is indicated in nuclear reactions at the top before the element symbol. The law of conservation of baryon charge (the number of nuclear particles), along with the law of conservation of electric charge, determine the result of a nuclear reaction. A very important feature of strong interactions is their short-range interaction. At distances less than $10^{-} 15$ m, they manifest themselves as attractive forces, approximately 100 times greater in magnitude than the electric repulsion forces at such a distance. This is why neutrons are needed in nuclei, and the overall attractive forces of nucleons balance the electrical repulsion of protons. Two nuclei that have managed to overcome their mutual repulsion (overcome the Coulomb barrier) and come closer to a distance of $10^{-} 15$ m, fall into the field of action of nuclear attractive forces, rush towards each other, merging into one whole. The resulting complex is complex; it is not just a few stuck together balls, but a drop of the primordial matter of the Universe - quark-gluon plasma. The objects formed as a result of the merger, for example, the nucleus of a helium atom and a neutron, have a total kinetic energy greater than the energy that was expended to overcome the Coulomb barrier. What average kinetic energy must be given to the nuclei of hydrogen isotopes so that they can overcome the Coulomb barrier? A simple calculation - equating the potential energy of electrical repulsion with the initial average kinetic energy of the particles - gives a temperature of the order of $10^{9} K.$ The fact that in this case some of the particles have a kinetic energy significantly greater than the average, and the presence of the quantum tunneling effect make it possible for hydrogen nuclei to approach each other at a lower temperature - “only” at 20 million degrees. It seemed that it was impossible to create such a temperature on Earth. But everything changed in the early morning of July 16, 1945, when the first nuclear (uranium) explosive device was tested in the United States at the Alamogordo test site in New Mexico.
