The Future of Global Energy

Evgeniy Pavlovich Velikhov was concerned with the protection of the internal walls of the reaction chamber from accidental emissions of plasma with its multimillion-dollar temperature, and the protection of these walls from the destructive action of fast neutrons, and dozens of other difficulties that had to be overcome. The dream of 5–10 years to solve the problem of “taming” the thermonuclear reaction has remained a dream. In 1985, Tokamak-15 and Tokamak-16 were built. These were joint developments between the USSR-China and the USSR-Japan, since the Soviet Union no longer had the opportunity to spend millions of dollars on this work. But the scientific leaders of the projects were Russian scientists - academicians B.B. Kadomtsev and E.P. Velikhov (E.P. was even awarded the Japanese “Order of the Rising Sun”). The duration of the steady state of plasma in these reactors already exceeded a second. But most importantly, it became clear that for a long-term stable state of plasma, much larger reactors are needed - about 10 times larger than Tokamak-16. The cost of constructing such a reactor was already estimated at several billion dollars. And no country in the world (including the USA) could afford such a scientific experiment. At the same time, by the end of the twentieth century it became clearly clear that humanity was facing the threat of a worldwide energy famine. Then, twenty years ago, 80% of the world's electricity was produced at thermal power plants using coal and natural gas as an energy source, 15% came from hydropower, 10% from nuclear (uranium) power plants. The energy of solar radiation, wind, sea tides and the heat of the earth's interior accounted for only hundredths of a percent in the overall energy balance. The work of a number of international organizations has made it possible to estimate the world's reserves of natural sources of electricity and the growth rate of its consumption. According to the most optimistic forecasts, there should be enough natural gas for 100 years, coal for 300 years, and uranium for 500 years. It is not possible to significantly expand hydropower production for environmental reasons. Powerful hydroelectric power stations require the creation of huge reservoirs, and this can cause irreparable harm to nature. For example, during the construction of the world's largest hydroelectric station, the Three Gorges hydroelectric station on the Yangtze River (with a capacity of 22.5 GW), the created artificial lake with an area of ​​$630 км^{2}$ required the resettlement of three million people. And the Russian hydroelectric stations Bratsk and Ust-Ilimsk, which are several times smaller in power, are “supplied” with reservoirs of area $5400 км^{2}$ and $1830 км^{2}$. These are no longer lakes, but entire seas. We specifically do not say anything about oil, the reserves of which, as we know, are the most limited (for a maximum of 60–80 years).
