Each of us observes a potential hole almost every day. Imagine, for example, that there is a glass of tea with a teaspoon inside and a saucer on the table. After stirring the tea, you want to place the spoon on the saucer. To do this, you need to do work - raise the spoon higher than the glass, overcome the potential barrier. The position of the spoon both in the glass and on the saucer is stable. If tea drinking takes place, say, in the carriage of a moving train, then small shocks or shocks will leave the spoon in place both in the glass and on the saucer. In this simplest situation, the bottom of the glass and the saucer are two potential holes, and the spoon is a ball rolling from one hole to another. It turns out that such an abstract “spoon” is present in many structures around us, where instead of the potential energy of gravitational interaction with the Earth, we must consider the potential energy of a deformable spring.
