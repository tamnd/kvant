Mendeleev, soon it will be possible to say that it is cheaper to burn banknotes in cars than gasoline. What has happened in the global energy sector over the past 20 years? The main change is a significant increase in the share of natural gas used as an energy source. It increased from 5 to 15%. Accordingly, the share of hard coal decreased to 50%. Hydropower provides 20% and nuclear energy provides 12%. The role of solar radiation and wind energy in the energy balance remains extremely insignificant. Increasing the share of natural gas in the energy balance certainly reduces the environmental damage from burning coal, the combustion products of which contain a number of harmful gases and microparticles of heavy metals. Capturing these contaminants before releasing the gases into the atmosphere is very expensive. The USA, where burning coal provides 60% of energy, could afford this. But the environmental situation in densely populated areas of China is deteriorating catastrophically. In this country, almost devoid of natural gas, 75% of its electricity is produced by burning coal. Russia in this regard is a prosperous country. Already, only 20% of electricity is produced by coal-fired power plants, and 55% comes from natural gas. The combined share of hydropower and nuclear energy is approximately equal to the global average of 32%.

Atomic era

There is a sharp differentiation in the use of uranium as an energy source in the world. Total currently working

191 nuclear power plants with 451 nuclear reactors (another 60 reactors are under construction). Of this number, 100 reactors were built in the United States and provide this country with 20% of its electricity. In Russia, 36 reactors provide almost a fifth of the electricity. There are countries in which nuclear energy is a third of the energy in its overall balance (South Korea, Finland). There are countries where this share is almost half of all energy (Slovakia, Ukraine). But in China and India, the share of nuclear energy in the total balance is less than 5%. Nuclear energy is not used at all in Australia, in most countries in South and Central America and in numerous small states in Oceania. France is ahead of all countries in this indicator, with its 58 nuclear reactors producing 77% of all electricity generated in the country. It is no coincidence that the Wikipedia article on the French economy begins with the words: “France is a highly developed country, a nuclear and space power.” This is partly explained by the fact that in France, back in the 30s of the last century, work on nuclear physics began to develop. Irene and Frederic Joliot-Curie (as well as Enrico Fermi in Italy) became Nobel laureates for the production of new isotopes (“labeled atoms”). But they did not understand that in their experiments the fission reaction of uranium was also observed. German radiochemists and physicists O. Hahn, F. Strassmann, L. Meitner guessed this. The atomic age has begun. Enrico Fermi continued working with uranium in the USA. He invented and built a nuclear reactor, where in November 1942, for the first time in the world, a nuclear fission chain reaction of uranium was carried out. But the purpose of creating the first reactors was not to generate electricity, but to produce plutonium, an artificial transuranium element capable, like uranium, of explosive fission reactions. After the end of the war and the terrible events of August 1945 in Hiroshima and Nagasaki, the interests of many nuclear physicists focused on peaceful research.
