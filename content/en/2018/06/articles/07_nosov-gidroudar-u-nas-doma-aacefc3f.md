Water hammer at our house

Y.NOSOV

GETTING PREPARED FOR BREAKFAST, I TOOK OUT OF THE REFRIGERATOR a glass jar of canned Tomatoes in Their Own Juice and took it to the table. The jar was almost full - it had been opened the day before. The lid barely covered the jar without any sealing twist. I held the can by the top edge, but, to my horror, it slipped out of my hands and fell. The can hit the laminate floor, but did not break, and its entire contents spilled immediately. What was left to do? Quickly, before my family came into the kitchen, I began to collect the remains of “tomatoes in their own juice” from the floor. Having almost finished the work, I looked with pleasure at the clean floor, BUT! Looking up, I saw a terrible picture: the white walls of the kitchen and the ceiling were splashed with tomato juice and even my clothes, including my shirt, were covered in red stains. Why did tomato juice, falling in a jar from a height of no more than 1.2–1.3 meters, fly up to a height of 2.5 meters after hitting the floor? It turns out that in this case we are dealing with water hammer. Water hammer (water hammer) is a pressure surge in a system filled with liquid caused by a rapid change in the flow rate of this liquid. It is unlikely that we will be able to solve the proposed problem quantitatively. However, qualitative reasoning can be given. In our “experiment”, the juice in a jar is the liquid whose movement instantly stops when the jar hits the floor. Having encountered an obstacle, the fluid flow, by inertia, continues to flow at the speed at which it was moving before the obstacle appeared. The first layers of liquid in contact with the obstacle are compacted at the same speed due to the entry of subsequent layers. Due to the constant injection of new layers of flow, the fluid pressure rapidly increases (water hammer occurs), and the fluid “looks” for a way to reduce this pressure. The final result for us is that a sharp increase in pressure in the liquid at the moment of impact leads to an explosive release of the contents of the can.
