Probability and last digit of the product

Our reader E. Znak noticed and proved that the number “1” has the following interesting property: it is no more probable than any other number, as the last digit of the product of two random natural numbers, and it is no less probable than any other number, as the last digit of the power of a random natural number with a random natural exponent. First of all, we need to decide in what sense we are talking about random natural numbers. We will assume that the probability of choosing a number with a given last digit is the same for all digits and is equal to 1/10. Similarly, the probability of choosing a number with a given remainder when divided by 4 is 1/4, etc. This arrangement is fully consistent with the following model for choosing a natural number: from a given large number of N consecutive natural numbers, one is chosen with equal probability so that the probability of choosing a particular number is 1/N. Studying Physics

[…] Simultaneously and independently of each other, we [with Yu.I. Manin] decided to study quantum physics (my $брат^{6}$ told me that mathematicians must know quantum theory). At first I tried to learn quantum field theory the same way mathematicians usually learn new things, and I found the task completely impossible; Perhaps such an attempt is completely stupid. The style that I really like is the style of Einstein's lectures or the best lectures of Landau. I realized that the basis of this science is naturalness, and this is exactly what I had previously understood from reading books on topology. Topologists of the time, such as Jean-Pierre Serres, René Thom or John Milnor, sometimes omitted definitions in their lectures, they simply said: “it is defined naturally.” And so I recognized this style of “naturalness” in Einstein’s lectures and in Landau’s best books. (Not all Landau's books are equally good, but the collection of them all is very valuable. Our students who want to study theoretical

Manin and I discussed various paradoxes and ambiguities of quantum theory, to which we had the same attitude. I remember Manin telling me that every mathematician will discover these ambiguities, but that it would be a mistake to stop and criticize these ambiguities. Many mathematicians, including my students, have serious difficulties studying theoretical physics. They try to teach it the same way they teach math: if they come across something they don't understand, they stop. I can say with confidence that physicists also stumble upon a lot of strange things; nevertheless, we must continue to move forward, and we need to think through these incomprehensible things only after reaching a certain level and having practiced enough. It is very difficult to execute Hilbert's program and rewrite theoretical physics in an axiomatic style. Hilbert did important work after Einstein discovered general relativity: he realized that Einstein's equation is the Euler–Lagrange equation for some functional. Thus, he found confirmation (for the case of the Einstein equation) of the general principle that the axioms of any fundamental physical theory must begin with the principle $Лагранжа^{7}$. Thus, Hilbert's program turned out to be useful for himself, since he applied it in this way. However, I do not like the attempts of some of my friends - very good specialists in mathematical physics, working on the Hilbert program - to make physics rigorous. I think this is impossible. You can prove a good theorem here, a good theorem there about some physical situation.
