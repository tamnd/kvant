The corresponding technology involves the use of multi-element phased antennas for electronic focusing. figure 6 shows a schematic representation of such an antenna for focusing ultrasound radiation into the brain through the bones of the skull. The brain is an organ where the use of focused ultrasound therapy has its own characteristics. The fundamental difficulty here is the fact that ultrasonic waves do not travel well through the skull due to absorption in the bone and reflection at its boundaries. In addition, the bones of the skull are heterogeneous in thickness and are characterized by a higher speed of sound (compared to the soft tissue located behind them), which leads to difficult to predict refractive effects. Solving the problem of ultrasonic exposure and visualization through thick skull bones is possible using recently developed methods of wave physics related to compensation of losses and aberrations during wave propagation in an inhomogeneous medium. It is based on the holographic principle, according to which the distribution of the characteristics of a wave field on a certain surface in this field contains information about the entire three-dimensional structure of the field, as well as the principle of reversibility of non-dissipative wave processes in time and the associated method of wave front reversal. The wavefront inversion method, used in radar and in studying the structure of underwater acoustic channels in the ocean, involves the use of a probe wave, which, passing through an inhomogeneous medium, is recorded by a multi-element antenna. Let us draw attention to another feature that requires consideration when using intense focused ultrasound: acoustic nonlinearity. The fact is that in ultrasound surgery systems already used in practice, acoustic intensity levels in the focal area reach 10,000–30,000 W $см^{2}$. At such intensities the wave behaves nonlinearly. The speed of propagation of a sound wave becomes dependent on its phase: the wave in the compression region has a higher propagation speed than in the rarefaction region. Therefore, in a sinusoidal wave, the compression phase catches up with the rarefaction phase - discontinuities are formed in the wave and the wave turns into a sawtooth, which in spectral representation corresponds to the enrichment of monochromatic ultrasonic radiation with higher harmonics. The enrichment of the spectrum of the emitted signal also affects the diffraction process. Diffraction and, accordingly, focusing of ultrasonic radiation become nonlinear, i.e. amplitude-dependent processes. The distance at which a discontinuity occurs in a plane harmonic wave with a frequency typical for medical applications of 1.5 MHz is only 3–5 mm. This scale is comparable to the size of the focal region of the ultrasonic beam; therefore, when describing the acoustic fields of such systems, it is certainly necessary to take into account nonlinear effects.
