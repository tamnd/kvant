masters in creating “whispering” galleries. Such galleries have curved or closed walls. If you speak in a whisper near such a wall, then the sound rays are concentrated near it and at a distance of several tens of meters you can clearly hear your whisper, even if you are also near the wall. There are such whispering galleries in St. Paul's Cathedral in London and St. Peter's in Rome, in the Temple of Heaven near Beijing, and perhaps elsewhere. The nature of sound propagation in an acoustic waveguide is similar to the propagation of laser radiation in an optical waveguide. Currently, the features of sound propagation in an underwater acoustic waveguide are used for ocean thermometry. The ocean can be viewed as a giant thermometer covering a huge area. By monitoring changes in deep ocean temperatures, climate warming can be monitored. The fact is that large-scale climate changes are extremely difficult to reliably determine due to large fluctuations in time and space. Huge masses of water in the ocean average out these fluctuations. The average temperature of the deep layers of the ocean on scales of several thousand kilometers can only be determined by acoustic methods; electromagnetic waves in sea water do not propagate over a noticeable distance. The speed of sound increases with increasing temperature. figure 1 below shows two series of recorded acoustic pulses, differing in that in the second series the upper layers of the ocean had a slightly higher temperature than in the first. As you can see, signals propagating along the red beam, which comes as close as possible to the heated surface of the ocean, arrive slightly earlier than signals propagating along other beams. For a distance of 250 km, these changes in propagation time can be fractions of a second. There are no changes in propagation time for other rays. Thus, from such experience one can

find out how many degrees and to what depth the water in the ocean has warmed up. It is clear that the greater the sound propagation distance, the higher the sensitivity of this method. Sound travels 250 km in the ocean in 167 s, which corresponds to a propagation speed of about 1500 m/s. Note that the fastest signals arrive first, propagating along the steepest rays lying in the layers of the ocean with a higher propagation speed. And the most intense signals arrive last along gentle rays located in the vicinity of the axis of the underwater sound channel, where the propagation speed is minimal. This feature of sound propagation is used for remote monitoring of heat transfer in the ocean, which is important for climate forecasting. The ocean shapes the weather on earth. The Arctic Ocean is the weather climate for Europe and large parts of Asia. A system of sound emitters and receivers distributed throughout the ocean can solve a wide variety of problems. Among them, we can highlight the measurement of the propagation time of signals along long paths to determine the heat content and circulation of ocean waters both on the scale of the entire ocean and in its individual parts; providing underwater positioning and navigation under ice; monitoring of ice dynamics, earthquakes and the movement of marine animals while passively listening to the ocean. The system can perform all these procedures in real time. Atmospheric research. The propagation of sound in the atmosphere is subject to the same laws as the propagation of sound in the ocean, with the difference that the speed of sound propagation in the air under normal conditions at the surface of the earth is 340 m/s. This is significantly less than the speed of sound in water. figure 2 shows a diagram of sound rays emerging from a sound source in the atmosphere. As can be seen, in the presence of wind, the rays behave differently depending on the direction of propagation.
