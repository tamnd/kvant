It was expected that for a large ocean far from the coast, the acoustic signal should propagate according to a spherical law from a point source, which could be considered a depth charge. In this case, the sound intensity should be inversely proportional to the area of ​​the sphere enclosing the source, i.e. should have decreased inversely with the square of the distance traveled by the sound. An explanation for this interesting effect was given by L.M. Brekhovskikh - later an academician and laureate of the USSR State Prize. He noticed that the water temperature quickly drops to a depth of 100–200 m, and then takes a constant value of about 4 q C. The drop in temperature leads to a decrease in the speed

Thus, depending on the speed of sound propagation and depth, there appears to be a minimum in which the acoustic energy is concentrated. On figure 1 it is clear that if the emitter is placed at the minimum level of the speed of sound, then the sound rays emerging from the emitter will be kept near this minimum as a result of refraction. As a result, some of the sound rays that exit the source at not very steep angles remain as they propagate in a layer several hundred meters thick. This layer is an underwater acoustic waveguide, or underwater sound channel. It is worth noting that the acoustic waveguide effect was used in the Middle Ages.
