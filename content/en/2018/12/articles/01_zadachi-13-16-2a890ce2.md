Physics of sound

I.ESIPOV

ACOUSTICS IS A BRANCH OF PHYSICS that studies the excitation, propagation, reception of sound waves, as well as their interaction with the medium. A feature of sound waves that distinguishes them from electromagnetic or gravitational waves is that they can propagate only in a continuous elastic medium. Sound surrounds us everywhere: in the atmosphere, under water, underground, in biological environments and materials, and even in space. Only sound can propagate through terrestrial structures and under water without significant attenuation, so it is widely used in the study of natural environments. Usually we call what we hear sound. It is generally accepted that the frequency range of the sound we hear lies in the range from 20 Hz to 20 kHz. This corresponds to 20–20,000 vibrations per second. Sound waves whose oscillation frequency exceeds this range have received their own special names. Ultrasound is the name given to sound waves whose vibration frequency is above 20 kHz. The technologically advanced range of ultrasound applications ranges from about 20 kHz to 100 MHz. The higher frequency region of ultrasound is called hypersound. Sound waves of hypersonic frequencies can propagate only in crystals with low sound absorption, such as single crystals of quartz, sapphire, lithium niobate, yttrium iron garnet, etc. Hypersound is used in processing large amounts of information, including optical images, and studying the structure of solids. This is the science of acoustoelectronics. The range over which hypersound is excited in an artificial, controlled manner is limited by

Seismic waves propagate in the ground in a similar way. They can be either natural or artificial. We can name earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, and mountain collapses as natural sources of seismic waves. Seismic waves are most effectively excited artificially by an explosion or special multi-ton vibrators. If only longitudinal sound waves propagate in the ocean and atmosphere (there is no shear elasticity in liquids and gases), then seismic waves can be both longitudinal and transverse. Transverse waves, depending on the plane of oscillation, can have different polarization. The speed of propagation of transverse waves is, as a rule, 2–3 times less than the speed of propagation of longitudinal waves. The presence of seismic waves of two types expands the capabilities of seismic sounding in comparison with sounding of the ocean or atmosphere. The central task of seismic sounding is to study the structure of the earth and search for minerals. Both of these tasks require conflicting approaches. Let us draw attention to another feature that requires consideration when using intense focused ultrasound: acoustic nonlinearity. The fact is that in ultrasound surgery systems already used in practice, acoustic intensity levels in the focal area reach 10,000–30,000 W $см^{2}$. At such intensities the wave behaves nonlinearly. The speed of propagation of a sound wave becomes dependent on its phase: the wave in the compression region has a higher propagation speed than in the rarefaction region. Therefore, in a sinusoidal wave, the compression phase catches up with the rarefaction phase - discontinuities are formed in the wave and the wave turns into a sawtooth, which in spectral representation corresponds to the enrichment of monochromatic ultrasonic radiation with higher harmonics. The enrichment of the spectrum of the emitted signal also affects the diffraction process. Diffraction and, accordingly, focusing of ultrasonic radiation become nonlinear, i.e. amplitude-dependent processes. The distance at which a discontinuity occurs in a plane harmonic wave with a frequency typical for medical applications of 1.5 MHz is only 3–5 mm. This scale is comparable to the size of the focal region of the ultrasonic beam; therefore, when describing the acoustic fields of such systems, it is certainly necessary to take into account nonlinear effects.
