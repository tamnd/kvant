What will happen if a substance consists not of individual atoms or ions, but of polyatomic molecules - such as solid hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, and halogens? Nothing terrible, it’s just not individual atoms that will diffuse, but, naturally, molecules. What if these molecular crystals consist of molecules that themselves consist of different atoms - for example, $H_{2} O, CO_{2}$, nitrogen oxides, many organic substances? Again, nothing fundamentally new - although we are already dealing with different atoms, they make up molecules that behave as a whole. The picture changes significantly when the substance consists of different atoms that are not connected into molecules. Then each atom can diffuse, and different atoms diffuse differently. But the diffusion of each depends on the diffusion of the others. Let's consider a common case when we have a matrix and a small impurity. However, this case is not simple either, because there are several diffusion paths. An impurity atom can diffuse, so to speak, on its own, jumping from one position between the atoms of the main substance to another - just as a mouse would diffuse in a human crowd. But it can diffuse, taking advantage of vacancies, i.e. empty spaces in the grid. Moreover, vacancies diffuse on their own; this is one of the mechanisms of self-diffusion. Thus, the diffusion of the impurity turns out to be related to the self-diffusion of the matrix material. But the matter does not end there. Diffusion begins to be influenced by properties that are commonly called chemical. If one substance diffuses into another and in some zone the concentration exceeds the solubility limit, then a new phase appears, i.e. a connection is formed. Sometimes these are separate sections, the so-called inclusions of the second phase, and sometimes these are continuous layers. Inclusions of the second phase significantly change the mechanical properties of the substance, and continuous layers affect further diffusion processes. As already mentioned, in the matrix - unless it is an ideal single crystal, and at absolute zero - there are dislocations and disturbances. Jobs are just one type, but there are others whose concentration

and mobility increase with increasing temperature. Some types of dislocations create opportunities for the diffusion of impurities, and these opportunities depend in complex ways on temperature. In polycrystals, boundaries between crystals, the so-called grain boundaries, are added to this whole picture. And they can also be pathways for the diffusion of impurities. The boundaries between crystals are their surfaces. And diffusion over the surface is easier than through the volume, because an atom on the surface has fewer bonds with other atoms than an atom in the volume, and it is easier for it to break some of these bonds in order to move to a new position. Surface diffusion became important to technology when powder metallurgy became fashionable in the 1960s. It is still important today, if only because it allows the sintering of powders (and this is surface diffusion) to obtain materials that cannot be obtained by other methods. In the coming decades, the role of surface diffusion and surface effects in general will only increase - the smaller the object, the greater the surface-to-volume ratio, and therefore the more significant the role of the surface.
