This is a figurative description of the effect that occurs when the soil characteristics are such that the body fits tightly to it, water is displaced from the gap, and the pressure from above remains (in water at a depth of 100 m this is already 10 atm). Now we are transported to the distant past, to the end of the century before last. Once upon a time there lived an engineer, Carl Edward Johansson, in Sweden, and in 1896 he came up with a new way to create reference dimensions. Strange phrase, isn't it? The fact is that for high-precision measurement of dimensions it is very useful to use some kind of standards, i.e. something of precisely known size. He came up with the following idea - to produce sets of plates of very precise length, and processed so that two plates, if pressed tightly against each other and slightly moved one over the other, “rubbed”, as they say in technology, would stick together. Mostly words are spoken about van der Waals forces and surface tension. Surface tension can hold a water Fig. 2 measure and a needle on the water, but not a hundred-gram piece of iron suspended in air, and in order for intermolecular attraction (van der Waals forces) to “work”, the surfaces must come closer to an interatomic distance, and over the entire area. Foreign sources, however, mention atmospheric pressure, but only as one of the factors, and not the main one. Johanson's tiles (they are also called “end gauges”) are pressed together by atmospheric pressure, and the tiny amount of moisture (and possibly oil) always present on the surface is enough to keep the atmosphere out of the gap. And if the quality of the surfaces is a little worse, you have to breathe on them - is it clear why? But the matter does not end there; this is only the first layer of the task. Atmospheric pressure acts horizontally on vertical surfaces, and gravity acts, as usual, vertically. Therefore, to keep the tiles from falling, you need... dry friction. However, in a simplified model of the world, friction can only be either dry or liquid lubricant. The real situation may be more complicated. For example, when a car drives on a wet pavement, mixed friction occurs - the water has time (well, if it has time) to be squeezed out of the contact zone (and reach passers-by). And when we “grind” the tiles, we just squeeze out water from some areas where direct contact of the tiles and that same dry friction are realized. Therefore, water, on the one hand, does not allow atmosphere into the gap, and on the other hand, it does not exclude dry friction in some areas. However, that's not all.
