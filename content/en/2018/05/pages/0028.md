THERE ARE SUCH QUESTIONS THAT IT’S ALWAYS strange to even ask THEM. For example, why is the support reaction N equal to the gravitational force mg. If a person attended physics classes and studied physics in them, and not communicating on social networks, then he will at least answer - according to Newton’s third law. And he will make a mistake, and such a mistake, even if the equation itself is written correctly, will cost him one test point on the Unified State Examination, i.e. two, and maybe even three, of those points for which he, in fact, is fighting. He will be mistaken because the third law speaks about forces applied to different bodies, but N and mg are applied to one body. A more attentive student will answer more correctly - according to Newton’s second and third laws, and even draw the appropriate conclusion. A student who is even more meticulous will be able to obtain the relationship between N and mg if the support itself has an acceleration a. However, one can confuse him too by asking what will happen if the acceleration at the support is directed downward and is greater than g. Taking advantage of his confusion, we will inform you that physics differs from mathematics in that not a single problem in it can be completely solved. Because physics is one of the so-called natural sciences, i.e. a science directly related to nature, examining processes occurring or supposedly occurring in nature. By the way, mathematics also partially relates to nature, but indirectly, through the natural sciences. But real physical problems can be solved only for certain conditions and with some accuracy. Let us stipulate which laws we consider to be observed, and agree to neglect everything else, in other words, we reduce physics to some limited model, then accurate and final solutions will become possible. This is exactly what we always do in school with physics and chemistry, creating a not entirely correct idea of ​​these sciences. Let's turn to our specific problem about N and mg. Here is a question that should arise: why, when deriving the notorious equality N = mg, do we not take into account the air pressure p? And don’t even think about saying anything about “little” and “neglecting” - the force with which the air on the ground, if you don’t climb mountains, presses on the surface is about 10 N for every square centimeter, or about 2000 N vertically on top of a standing person and 20,000 N for a horizontally lying person. (Ostap Bender clearly knew school physics.) Let us recall that 2000 and 20000 N are the weight of a body weighing 200 and 2000 kg, respectively. It turns out that when you sleep, you have about half an elephant or two bears standing on you - whichever you prefer. A school textbook discusses mechanical stress in solids, liquids, and gases, and a good textbook even explains why stress in gases and liquids is called pressure. Of course, this should have been made clearer by discussing the applicability of the concept of “pressure” for different cases, but now we are not talking about that, but about why we do not take into account the Archimedes force when deriving the formula N = mg. The answer to the question is known: because air is about three orders of magnitude lighter than water, and metals are about four orders of magnitude lighter. Here you can still remember the old joke about which is heavier: a kilogram of nails or a kilogram of cotton wool, and ask for a numerical estimate. By the way, when trying to do this, they often forget that the “true volume” of cotton wool - the one that should enter into the force of Archimedes - is very different from the geometric, external volume, from, so to speak, the volume of the pillow. Mention of Archimedes' law for bodies "peacefully sleeping", i.e. lying on tables, maybe - and should! – point out the key point to the reader.
