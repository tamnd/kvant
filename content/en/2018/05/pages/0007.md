And now that we have clarified Euclid’s postulates, formulated in the third century BC, we will move to the nineteenth and twentieth centuries of our era and prove all the fundamental theorems of convex geometry. The foundations of the theory of convexity (not only on a plane or in three-dimensional space, but also in space of a greater number of dimensions) were laid by the outstanding mathematician of the late 19th and early 20th centuries, Herman Minkowski (1864–1909). The theory of convexity is based on separation theorems. In order to formulate these theorems, it is necessary to define the very concept of separability. The line l is said to separate the set $C_{1}$ from the set $C_{2}$ if $C_{1}$ lies in one half-plane with the boundary l, and $C_{2}$ in another. We say that a line l strictly separates a set C from a point A if it separates C from A, and A does not belong to the separating line l, and the set C does not intersect with it. Theorem 1 (separability theorem). A nonempty closed convex subset of the Euclidean plane $E^{2}$ can be strictly separated from a point that does not belong to it.
