point C lies between A and B. The set of points lying between A and B, together with the points A and B themselves, will be called a segment with boundary points A and B. Such a segment will be denoted by [AB] (Fig.1). The concept of a segment allows us to give the basic definition for us - a convex set. A set C is called convex if, together with any two points A and B from C, the entire segment [AB] belongs to C. We have defined a geometric figure - a segment. Let's define two more geometric shapes. A circle with a center at a point O of radius r > 0 is the set of all points removed from O by a distance r. The circle B O, r consists of all points removed from O by no more than r (designation B from Ball). By definition, a segment is convex. A circle is also convex (prove it!), but a circle is not: if you connect any two distinct points on a circle with a segment, then all points of this segment, except the ends, do not belong to the circle. Here it is reasonable to understand what the boundary of a set (including a convex one) is. Non-boundary, internal points of a set have the property that any such point can be made the center of a circle, albeit a very small one, but which lies entirely in the set. The boundary of a convex set (that is, the set of its boundary points) is something like a solid fence separating the set from the rest of the plane. In what follows, we consider only convex sets that include their boundary. Such sets are called closed convex sets on the plane $E^{2}$. Let's look at a simple example. The interior points of the circle B O, r are all its points that do not lie on the corresponding circle. Indeed, if the distance from point A to O is less than r, |OA| < r, then A is an interior point of $круга^{B} O,^{r}$ by the triangle inequality. The proof that a circle is the boundary of a circle is not as obvious as it seems at first glance. Let's try to prove that the points of the circle are not interior points of the circle. Let us choose a point A on the circle, |OA| = r, and take any small circle with center A. How can we prove that it contains points not from $B^{O},^{r}$? For example, like this: let’s draw a ray OA. It would seem that it should “pierce” the circle at point A, i.e. for any number x > r on the ray OA there must be the points we need at a distance x from O. This consideration leads us to the second property of points and lines on the plane, which we will need in the proofs. It clarifies the second and third postulates of Euclid. 2) A point O on a line divides the points of this line into two convex collections, called rays with a vertex in O. Moreover, (a) if A and B belong to different rays, then O lies in the segment [AB] and (b) each of the rays is indistinguishable from a collection of non-negative real numbers if each point on the ray is associated with the distance from it to the vertex of the ray O (Fig.2).
