The vertical floating of air bubbles upward in distilled water (H = 81) in the presence of a field is replaced by their deflection away from the charged ball. Note that the sign of the charge of the ball creating a non-uniform field does not play a role. The change in the direction of the force acting on a dielectric object, depending on the ratio of its permeability $( H_{1} )$ and the permeability of the medium $( H_{2} ),$ in which it is placed, is well demonstrated by the experiment with a paraffin $( H_{1} = 2 )$ ball. When the ball is in the air $( H_{1} > H_{2} ª 1 )$ the forces at different points of its surface are always directed outward and are maximum in the parts of the surface closest to the charged metal ball. As a result, the effect of attraction of the dielectric to the charges that create the field (Fig.7,a) occurs. If you place both balls in acetone $( H_{1} < H_{2} ª 21 ),$, then attraction is replaced by repulsion (Fig.7, b). The analysis carried out in example 4 allows us to understand this peculiar behavior of the ball - the forces acting on it are determined by the polarization charges of both the ball itself and the environment. And the change in the direction of the resulting force is associated with a change in the sign of the total polarization charge at the boundary of the body with the medium. When the ball is in a uniform field (for example, a flat capacitor), the forces at various points on its surface are balanced and no displacement of the ball will be observed in both cases. In the general case, in an inhomogeneous medium (or in the presence of individual fragments of homogeneous dielectrics), it is impossible to establish any simple expressions for the forces acting even on point charges. Moreover, the presence of dielectric objects near charged bodies should not necessarily lead to a weakening of the forces acting on the bodies (especially by H times), but may even contribute to their increase. Let's look at an example that proves this. Example 5. How will the interaction force between two point charges in air change if a dielectric ball is placed between them? We already know that the force of electrostatic interaction between charges will not change in any way, but will remain equal to the Coulomb force. But the resulting forces acting on the charges will change - this is what is usually meant when asking the question given in the condition.
