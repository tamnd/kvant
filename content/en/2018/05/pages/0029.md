However, our adventures are just beginning. First of all, it is worth asking whether the gap is deep and wide - the one that exists under the body sleeping peacefully on the table. This depends on the mechanical properties and surface topography of the contacting bodies. When we place a real rough body, it first touches another body with one point, then, turning a little, a second point, then, turning a little more, a third, and then lowers a little more, expanding the areas of the so-called true contact around these points, and the final total area of ​​all contact areas must be such that the material can withstand the weight of the upper body. Note that upon contact, contact areas other than the first three could arise. And that both bodies were deformed, and the distribution of deformations depended on the characteristics of the roughness and hardness of both surfaces. If we place, for example, a steel cube with an edge of 10 cm, the mass of which is approximately 8 kg and the weight of 80 N, on a steel surface, then the area of ​​true contact with a material strength of 5 $10^{8}$ N $м^{2}$ will be, as is easy to calculate, $0,16 мм^{2}$. This is an insignificant fraction - 16 millionths! – from the geometric contact area, which is $10000 мм^{2}$. The situation when the true contact area is a small fraction of the geometric one is constant and omnipresent for technology. And it entails many different problems. So, if heat or electric current needs to be transferred through a contact, they have to squeeze through these small spots. When transferring heat, this entails an increase in the temperature difference between parts or, with a fixed temperature difference, a decrease in heat flow. When electric current flows, small contact pads overheat due to their high current density, and the contacts can weld, melt, or even evaporate. And this is just the beginning of the problems. For example, it is the behavior of contacts that determines dry friction and chemical processes occurring at the contact of solids. By the way, when books start talking about diffusion in solids, the story begins with researchers first pressing lead against gold for a long time, and then studying whether gold atoms penetrated into lead and vice versa. But why do they take these particular metals? On what properties of metals does the number of penetrated atoms depend? In particular, those on which the area of ​​true contact, the area of ​​“spots,” depends. And the larger it is, the heavier the metal, if it just lies there and is not pressed by additional force, and the softer it is - the soft metal is pressed more tightly, over a larger area. What happens if one of the substances is so soft that the true contact area is comparable to the geometric one? Then the bodies are pressed against each other by very large forces of atmospheric pressure, and it should be difficult to separate them. Why do we easily manage to separate the rubber band pressed to it from the table? The answer is because it is all soft, when pressed it is completely deformed, partly flattened, and we separate it little by little, gradually reducing the geometric contact area, and not all at once. Now, if we made a rigid part with a thin soft coating and pressed this coating against a hard surface, then it would become more difficult to separate it from this surface.
