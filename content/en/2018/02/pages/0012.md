About density

In many cases, strength and rigidity are important not in themselves, but together with weight, or more precisely, with density. Let's see within what limits it can vary in solids. At one end of the scale are osmium and iridium with a density of 22.6 - 22.5 g $см^{3}$; There is no chance of obtaining material with a higher density. This is at normal pressure, i.e. $10^{5}$ Pa, at higher pressures the density is higher, but even at the bottom of the Mariana Trench, at a pressure of 1000 atmospheres, or $10^{8}$ Pa, the density increases by no more than tenths of a percent. Of the elements that are not too expensive and not too radioactive, tungsten has a high density: 19.2 g $см^{3}$ and uranium: 18.9 g $см^{3}$. They are used when maximum density is needed. As for low density, the situation is complicated. Among the elements, the lightest is lithium, its density is 0.5 g $см^{3}$. In such cases they usually write “he would swim in the water” - but he would not do this for very long. It is impossible to determine the lightest compound; most likely, it is one of the “supramolecular compounds” with a relatively loose structure. They are very diverse, their typical density values ​​are 0.7 - 0.9 g $см^{3}$. Even easier are two-phase structures, when one of the phases is air. The natural record holder is balsa wood with a density of 0.11 - 0.14 g $см^{3}$ (in fact, for multiphase materials it would be more correct to say not “density”, but “average density”). Among artificial two-phase structures, the record holder is airgel with

density 0.03 - 0.3 g $см^{3}$. This is a structure based on silicon oxide $SiO_{2}$ or aluminum $Al_{2} O_{3}$, but with a porosity of 90–99%. The more voids in a material, the lower its density and the lower its weight when weighed in air, despite the fact that the voids are filled with air. If you make a material with “closed pores” and during production you pump air out of them (or make the material in a vacuum), then you can imagine what atmospheric pressure will do to such a material. (What will a vacuum do to a material containing air in closed pores?) For superporous materials, the extremely low density of the material is determined by the required strength, and in principle it can be arbitrarily low. If it is a closed-cell material, then the strength must ensure that the shape is maintained in the presence of a pressure difference between the pores and the environment surrounding the material. If this difference is ordinary atmospheric pressure, then a simple calculation (try it) shows that if the “material itself” of this material has a strength of 5 to 150 GPa, then the achievable average density will be 0.2 - 0.007 g $см^{3}$, respectively. In other words, breaking the natural record (0.11 g $см^{3} )$ is, in principle, possible, but it will be difficult to do. But creating a material “lighter than air” (0.001 g $см^{3} )$) seems impossible in principle. Among gases and liquids, the situation is as follows. The lightest gas is hydrogen, its density under normal conditions is 0.09 g/l. Atomic hydrogen would be even lighter, but for noticeable dissociation hydrogen it must be heated: for example, at $4000 ^\circ$ C it dissociates by 60%, at $6000 ^\circ$ C by 99%. The heaviest gas under normal conditions is tungsten hexafluoride $WF_{6}$, its density is 12.6 g/l. The lightest liquid is liquid hydrogen, its density is 70 g/l, but this is in very cold weather. Under normal conditions, the lightest liquids are probably. pentaborane $B_{5} H_{9}$ and neopentane $C_{5} H_{12}$ with densities of 0.62–0.61 g/l, and the heaviest is mercury Hg, its density is 13.5 kg/l.
