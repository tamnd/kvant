In fact, a straight line connecting point Q with any point M of our three-dimensional space will not have other points in common with it (since a straight line lying in a three-dimensional plane intersects it at most at one point). In the story of the American scientist and science fiction writer Martin Gardner, “The Zero-Sided Professor,” a man performed a trick by folding a sheet of paper in a certain way so that at one moment the sheet disappeared. The secret was that the folded surface was “zero-sided,” which is impossible in three-dimensional space, so it went into other dimensions. There are no zero-sided surfaces, but a vanishing trick is theoretically possible if you bend the sheet so that it no longer lies in three-dimensional space, similar to what we did with our two-dimensional box. Entering the fourth dimension

Now we can solve problems 1 and 2 posed at the very beginning of the article. The answer in each is “yes, but with access to four-dimensional space.” Solution to problem 1. You, of course, already understand how to solve. A four-dimensional simplex has 10 edges and 10 two-dimensional triangle faces, which means you need to make a simplex of 10 matches. First, we make a tetrahedron from 6 matches, and then we go out into four-dimensional space, where we take a point removed from all the vertices of our tetrahedron at a distance equal to the match (see the construction of a simplex in the previous section). By connecting it with matches to all four vertices of the tetrahedron, we obtain a simplex.
