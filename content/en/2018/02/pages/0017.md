Linguistic Problems In 1963, Zaliznyak published an article entitled “Linguistic Problems.” In it, he demonstrated that it was possible to analyze facts from unknown languages ​​using rigorous logic combined with the most basic understanding of how texts in human language are structured. Thus, we have the right to expect that the order of words in a language is not random, but is subject to some regularities: for example, the definition always comes after the word being defined or always before it, words related to each other are located nearby, and not scattered at different ends of the sentence, etc. - and then the use of logical operations will allow us to understand what is what in an incomprehensible text. No special knowledge is needed: for example, Zaliznyak’s article gives a task in which 14 phrases in Basque are given with translations into Hungarian, and then the reader is asked to translate three phrases from Hungarian into Basque. The author specifically emphasizes that this is a task for those who do not know either language and, thus, do not even understand what these phrases mean. However, after careful analysis of the examples, the problem turns out to be solvable. To demonstrate how logic works in linguistic problems, we present one of Zaliznyak’s problems (1968). Task 1. Three word combinations of the Tajik language with Russian translations are given: a) du-sti khubi x, amsoyai shumo - a good friend of your neighbor; b) x, amsoyai do-sti khubi shumo - the neighbor of your good friend;

c) x, amsoyai khubi do-sti shumo - your friend’s good neighbor. Exercise. Determine which Russian word corresponds in meaning to each of the four Tajik words found here. Solution. First of all, we note that the difference between the Russian nominative and genitive cases (for example: neighbor and neighbor, good and good) is not reflected in the Tajik text by any difference in word forms (since each of the four Tajik words appears everywhere in the same form). Let's compare phrases a) and c). Russian translations here look like good Z of your Y and differ only in that the nouns ‘friend’ and ‘neighbor’ have swapped syntactic roles. In the Tajik language, the only difference is that the words du–sti and x,amsoyai have swapped places. From this we can conclude that these two Tajik words correspond to Russian nouns. Let us now compare b) and c). Russian translations differ here only in which of the two nouns the definition of ‘good’ refers to: Z of your good Y and good Z of your Y. Let's describe the transition from b) to c) like this: the word 'good' changed its place, which is why other words automatically moved ('neighbor' - from first to second place, 'your' - from second to third). In the Tajik text, the change affected only the words du-sti and khubi. We need to determine how to describe this transition: whether to say that the word du-sti was rearranged (and this automatically entailed the movement of the word khubi), or vice versa. We must move the analogue of the word ‘good’, but we already know that du-sti is a noun; therefore, the change in word order is caused by the movement of the word khubi, which means ‘good’; then the noise is ‘yours’. It remains to establish the meaning of the nouns. There are two possible hypotheses. Let’s check the first of them: let du-sti be a ‘friend’, and x,amsoyai be a ‘neighbor’.
