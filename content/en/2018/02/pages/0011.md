This is all about compact materials, but there are also fibers and “whiskers”. Fiber is something long and thin, and either has a different structure from the solid material, or consists of a material that can only be a fiber. It is of interest to us if it turns out to be significantly stronger than the “massive” record holders. But because it is thin, it turns out to be flexible. Therefore, fibers with particularly high strength or particularly high Young's modulus can be used either when stiffness is not needed or as a component of a composite material. Several high-strength and high-modulus fibers are known, but specific data vary greatly because they are highly dependent on the technology. In general, we can assume that the following can claim the title of the strongest: fiberglass - strength 4.7 GPa, Young's modulus 90 GPa; Kevlar and ultra-high molecular weight polyethylene (UHMWPE) – strength 5 GPa, modulus 180 GPa; carbon fiber – strength 3.6 GPa, modulus 600 GPa (higher values ​​are also found in the literature). It should be taken into account that quite often fibers of extremely high strength have a lower Young’s modulus. The opposite statement is also correct (how would you formulate it?) “Whiskers” are something even thinner, but while the fibers can be of unlimited length, monocrystalline whiskers have so far only been produced very short ones. Their strength for $Al_{2} O_{3}$ and carbon C reaches 20 GPa with a Young's modulus of 400 GPa and 700 GPa, respectively; for BN and SiC – 40 GPa with a modulus of 1000 GPa; for carbon nanotubes (essentially also whiskers) – 150 GPa with a modulus of 1000 GPa.
