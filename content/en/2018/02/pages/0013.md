Plasticity and its brother Poisson

The plasticity of metals and alloys usually amounts to a few percent, and it is of interest mainly to technologists - it is due to it that bending, stretching, stamping, and the like are carried out. But there is such a miracle - superplasticity, when a metal rod easily lengthens several times, but does not break. For example, zinc Zn elongates 10 times, aluminum alloys Al with copper Cu or the same zinc Zn - 6 times. Moreover, there are two types of such behavior. The first is when the metal consists of ultra-fine (microns or less) crystals and viscous layers between them, along which the crystals slide. The second is when the metal is deformed at a temperature at which a phase transition occurs in it and atoms easily, without creating vacancies, occupy new places in the lattice. Let's say that some steels behave this way (with manganese Mn, cobalt Co and vanadium V) in the region $900 \infty C$. When this phenomenon was discovered, journalists rushed to describe how the entire industry would change tomorrow, presses would go to the dump, and so on. Alas, it quickly became clear that the phenomenon occurs only in some metals and alloys, while the industry uses hundreds of others and is not at all ready to abandon them. In addition, heating to certain temperatures is required, and the possible deformation rate is low, and the industry is again not very happy with this. So the method is applied, but to a limited extent; the revolution did not happen. In general, if industry has mastered the use of a material, it is reluctant to switch to another, and it is not easy to lure it by simplifying the technology - the advantages must be very great. As for the Poisson's ratio, which characterizes the relative change in transverse dimensions during stretching, strong surprises await us here. Usually they write that it should be in the range from 0 to 0.5. Value 0 – we stretch, but the material does not compress,

This is a property of cork. (By the way, isn’t this property used in practice?) Some sources write that the value 0 is characteristic of brittle materials - so this is not true. A value of 0.5 is the preservation of volume during deformation, this is how some rubbers behave. For most isotropic materials, this coefficient takes a value from 0.2 to 0.5, for metals - from 0.23 for uranium U to 0.45 for lead Pb. The exception is beryllium Be with a coefficient of 0.024–0.034 depending on the technology, and sometimes wider limits are indicated. But this is all for isotropic materials, polycrystals. There are materials with a Poisson's ratio greater than 1, i.e. reducing volume when stretched, and with a negative coefficient, i.e. swelling when stretched. And it seems that for some single crystals, when deformed along certain directions, values ​​of –3 and +4 can be reached. It’s scary to imagine - we stretch the material by 1%, but it increases in diameter by 3% or shrinks by 4%! (By the way, by what percentage does the volume change?) And for artificially produced porous materials, the compression can be even greater.
