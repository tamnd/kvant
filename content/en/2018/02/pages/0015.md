Very short and very long

“Very briefly” is impact resistance, i.e. the ability of a material to withstand loads that are applied for a very short time, in short, to withstand impact. This ability is measured, roughly speaking, like this. A blow is applied to a block of the test material in a certain way, which destroys it, and it is calculated what part of the energy of the striking body was spent on destruction. The ratio of this energy to the cross-section of the broken bar is called impact strength. Its lowest values ​​are of little interest to anyone, except in situations where we need to make some kind of mechanical fuse or a destructible partition so that some two substances are mixed. To say who has the lowest impact strength, i.e. which material is most easily destroyed by impact is difficult - most likely, it is glass (0.2 kJ $м^{2} ).$ Designers are understandably interested in maximum values. The best steels for this parameter have an impact strength of 3 MJ $м^{2}$, for Kevlar-based composites - 0.75 MJ $м^{2}$, but they are 6 times lighter than steel. According to some data, ultra-high molecular weight polyethylene, UHMW, is slightly superior to Kevlar, and there is evidence in the literature of the development of composite fibers with even higher parameters. As for repeated loads, classic examples are loads during vibration, for example, of airplane wings, or during vibration of anything A-axis.

The wheelset bends back and forth 3 million times on the way from Moscow to Vladivostok. (How many times do you think it bends on the way back?) With repeated cyclic loading, the strength is less than with a single one, and the more cycles, the less strength (there are several alloys that are strengthened during the first deformations, but such situations are rare, they are called cyclic hardening). It is customary to distinguish between two situations: a low-cycle mode, when the loads exceed the yield strength and failure usually occurs in less than 1–10 thousand cycles, and a high-cycle mode, when the loads do not exceed the yield strength and the sample can withstand more than 10–100 thousand cycles. The limits of the regimes are indicated differently in different sources, and many speak of the existence of a “transition” zone or intermediate regime. There is a lot of data on the operation of materials in such modes, but they do not add up to a single picture for the standard reason - there are too many variables (for example, loading speed is important) and too many simultaneous processes. Roughly, we can assume that with a symmetrical loading cycle, when maximum loads have different signs but the same amplitude, the ultimate loads under multi-cycle (10–100 million cycles) loading are reduced by 2–2.5 times for steels and 2–4 times for non-ferrous metals. Moreover, for the strongest steels and alloys this ratio can be greater, i.e. By increasing the strength under single loading, we do not increase the multi-cycle strength by the same amount.
