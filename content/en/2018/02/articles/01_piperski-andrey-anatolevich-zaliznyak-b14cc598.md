Linguistic Problems In 1963, Zaliznyak published an article entitled “Linguistic Problems.” In it, he demonstrated that it was possible to analyze facts from unknown languages ​​using rigorous logic combined with the most basic understanding of how texts in human language are structured. Thus, we have the right to expect that the order of words in a language is not random, but is subject to some regularities: for example, the definition always comes after the word being defined or always before it, words related to each other are located nearby, and not scattered at different ends of the sentence, etc. - and then the use of logical operations will allow us to understand what is what in an incomprehensible text. No special knowledge is needed: for example, Zaliznyak’s article gives a task in which 14 phrases in Basque are given with translations into Hungarian, and then the reader is asked to translate three phrases from Hungarian into Basque. The author specifically emphasizes that this is a task for those who do not know either language and, thus, do not even understand what these phrases mean. However, after careful analysis of the examples, the problem turns out to be solvable. To demonstrate how logic works in linguistic problems, we present one of Zaliznyak’s problems (1968). Task 1. In his book “From Proto-Slavic Accentuation to Russian” (1985), he clearly explained where and how these patterns came from. Let us assign each morpheme (root, ending, etc., as well as prepositions, conjunctions and particles) one of three markings: p, o or –. Let's agree that the emphasis is determined by the leftmost arrow. If it is p, then the autonomous (“real”) stress falls on the syllable marked by it; if it is o, then the autonomous (“present”) stress falls on the syllable to the right of it; if there are no arrows in the word, but only minuses, the word has an automatic (“fake”) stress.
