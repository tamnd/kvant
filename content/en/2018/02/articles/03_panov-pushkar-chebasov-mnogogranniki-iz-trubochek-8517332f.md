Polyhedra made from tubes

D.PANOV, A.PUSHKAR, D.CHEBASOV

THIS ARTICLE TELLS HOW straws and small rubber rings can be used for geometric constructions. The tubes suitable for our experiments are sold in the “Veselaya Zateya” store and are called balloon sticks there. They are quite long - 37.5 cm and at the same time rigid and elastic. Small rubber rings for weaving are sold in almost any store. First, we took three tubes and fastened their ends in pairs with rubber rings. The result is an equilateral triangle (Fig.1). Why not assemble a square from the same tubes? And here a surprise awaited us. We connected four identical tubes properly, but it was not quite a square, or even not a square at all. If you put this structure on the table, it instantly turns into a rhombus, and if you lift it by the top, it ceases to be a flat figure at all (Fig.2). We called this one

the square or this quadrilateral is a naughty square. Mathematicians call shapes that easily change their shape bendable. So, a triangle consisting of three segments is rigid - inflexible, but a square and any other quadrangle, and pentagon, and hexagon, etc. – bendable. This is a well-known fact, and we were able to verify it with the help of tubes and rubber bands. Why not collect something spatial now? And we easily assembled the simplest polyhedron from the tubes - a triangular pyramid (Fig.3).

True, in mathematics, a polyhedron is another object - a frame together with polygon faces (Fig.4). In this case, the common segments of adjacent faces are called edges - they correspond to our tubes. And the points where several edges converge are called vertices - we can say that these are our rubber bands. Here are two more remarkable polyhedra: an octahedron is assembled from 12 tubes, it has 8 faces, and an icosahedron is assembled from 30 tubes, it has 20 faces (Fig.5). All three assembled polyhedra have triangular faces, and these polyhedra are inflexible. Now let's try to assemble a cube from the tubes. Look at figure 6: this cube does not hold its shape at all - it bends, and bends in an absolutely monstrous way. We called it the "loose" cube. And jokes aside, here it arises

Of course, tubes and rubber bands can suggest something, but there are too many different polyhedra to trust these clues without rigorous proof. We had some ideas, but we weren't sure about them. Fortunately, there are now places on the Internet where you can get a qualified answer to a difficult mathematical question. Many issues related to modern mathematical research, as well as unsolved mathematical problems, are discussed there, but non-professional questions or homework-type questions are not at all welcome. and it turned out that O’Rourke was wrong - the polyhedron curved perfectly. And then A. Petrunin gave a simple proof of the bendability of a tubular polyhedron that has at least one non-triangular face. In his proof, he used the theorem of A.D. Aleksandrov. This is how our question was answered. The theorems of Cauchy and Aleksandrov mentioned here are the most famous in the theory of polyhedra. Their formulations are quite simple, and the evidence is accessible to older students. We recommend reading the book by N.P. Dolbilin “Pearls of the Theory of Polyhedra” (Moscow: MTsNMO, 2012), dedicated to the proofs of these theorems and intended for schoolchildren. A few words in conclusion. An important classical question is the question of the rigidity and bendability of real polyhedra - polyhedra with faces. There is a beautiful theory with a long history and unexpected discoveries, partially accessible to schoolchildren. We advise you first of all to look on the Internet for the popular lecture “Flexible polyhedra and bellows”, delivered a year ago by A. Gaifullin at the XXVIII Mathematical Festival in Moscow.
