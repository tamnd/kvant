Both nitrogen and oxygen have absorption and emission lines that are in the visible wavelength range. Therefore, the entire sky that observers saw during the Tunguska event was fiery. Since the air near a strongly heated surface quickly heats up, this is accompanied by an increase in pressure, and the pressure also increases because the evaporating substance of the surface areas increases the concentration of particles that create pressure. This happens almost simultaneously with bright illumination of the surface, so a sharp increase in pressure occurs. Thus, the first thunderclap is associated with radiation that travels at the speed of light. Heating depends on the angle of incidence of the rays on the surface, so the pressure dropped in the direction from the place where the brightness of the illumination was maximum. This caused the wind - the air “ran away” from the heating source. And when shock waves from those parts of the air through which the meteorite flew came to the area of ​​the surface where the eyewitness was located, the person heard a second clap of thunder. This is why eyewitness observers said that their clothes became so hot that they burned their skin, and that there were two explosions separated by time. For every observer, no matter where he is, the second impact is perceived as an explosion, because the speed of the meteorite is significantly greater than the speed of propagation of sound waves (even shock waves) in the air. With a minimal time delay, a sound wave arrives to the observer from the location of the meteorite trajectory closest to him, and then sounds come from more distant parts of the flight path. With a minimum distance of 10 km, the delay will be approximately 20–30 s, and in all other cases it will be longer.

⟦column⟧

Every observant person during a thunderstorm with lightning strikes experiences the same sensations when sound waves come to him from different areas in the air through which lightning has passed. If lightning strikes far from the observer’s position, then he hears long rumbles of thunder, arriving with a delay after a bright flash of light. But if lightning strikes a pole or tree located next to the observer, the sound boom is sharp and very loud. And the sounds coming after the first blow are already perceived as weak, i.e. there are no prolonged “claps of thunder”. As during the testing of nuclear weapons, in particular the “superbomb” over Novaya Zemlya, during the flight of the meteorite, the heated air, along with combustion products on the ground, rose to a height of more than 90 km. It was blown by winds in different directions, and for several days clouds were observed glowing at high altitudes of about 80 km. “White nights” in the northern hemisphere after the passage of the Tunguska meteorite were not only in their traditional places (at latitudes greater than 60°), but also significantly further south. In conclusion, it should be noted that in the considered model of the phenomenon, the body of the meteorite was considered symmetrical. But for a relatively small cosmic body this is not at all a necessary condition. You can, for example, remember your childhood pastime - throwing flat pebbles onto the surface of the water. If thrown successfully, the pebble makes several jumps across the water. Something similar could have happened in the case of the Tunguska meteorite, only there was only one “jump” when it hit the atmosphere.
