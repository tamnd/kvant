OUR OBSERVATIONS

I will grow grass to you

V. PTUSHENKO

CITY RESIDENTS ARE USUAL TO SEE how fragile and defenseless nature is before the onslaught of civilization. But it is even more unusual to observe how she is able to recover, overcoming the most incredible barriers. It’s amazing how a delicate, soft stem of a plant makes its way through the asphalt, which can withstand the pressure of thousands of wheels. No less surprising is that plants can settle on walls. Blades of grass making their way through the cracks between bricks or stone slabs of building cladding can be observed in the city on the walls of completely residential, “functioning” buildings. Where human influence is less - on the upper floors and roofs or on the walls of abandoned buildings - you can sometimes find entire groves (Fig.1).

One of the fundamental conditions for plant life is the presence of mineral elements necessary for growth in the environment in which its roots are located. The walls of abandoned churches, for example, are most often made of red brick. The main raw material for it is clay - of course, not the most fertile type of soil, but still containing many elements that are paramount for the plant: metal elements (in the form of oxides) - potassium, sodium, calcium, magnesium, iron, as well as sulfur. Of the vital elements, nitrogen and phosphorus are missing from this list. People also have to deal with the need to add nitrogen fertilizers to the soil in a normal plant growth environment. In the wild, nitrogen enters the soil through the activity of microorganisms - bacteria that can convert air nitrogen into organic matter. Gardeners are well aware that such bacteria are contained in the nodules of legume plants. However, free-living bacteria can also do this useful work, in particular photosynthetic cyanobacteria, which are ready to settle anywhere - in reservoirs, on the soil surface, on walls, on the walls of aquariums and pots with house plants, forming a green coating. It is quite possible that it is they who, settling next to cereals or young birch trees, provide an influx of nitrogen into the living environment of their neighbors. The situation with phosphorus is more complicated. Maybe it is brought in by animals - ants, mice, birds? However, in this respect, living conditions on the wall are not very different from life in ordinary soil - phosphorus is always bad there, too. In addition, all the necessary elements, including phosphorus, are contained in the cement that holds the bricks together. Of course, when talking about the chemical composition of the brick substrate, we missed one important stage: it is also necessary for the brick to turn into a loose medium suitable for plant growth. This occurs partly due to natural erosion - under the influence of precipitation.
