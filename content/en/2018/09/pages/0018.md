In 1943, he was invited to the Los Alamos laboratory in California, where work began on the construction of an atomic bomb. The young Doctor of Philosophy (Physics) headed the department for the application of mathematical methods in solving numerous physical and engineering questions arising in connection with a completely new problem. Naturally, work in this laboratory was associated with the highest degree of secrecy. But Feynman's privileged position as one of the leaders of the project made it possible to place his young wife in a nearby hospital (in Albuquerque), where he was allowed to visit her on Sundays. The fact is that before leaving Princeton, Richard Feynman formalized his marriage to Arlene Greenbaum, who was already terminally ill with thyroid tuberculosis. He fell in love with her when they were 13 years old, and at the age of 19 they became engaged, but Richard’s parents prevented the marriage from happening for a long time. Arlene died in 1946 in the arms of her husband. For some time Richard felt empty. Added to personal grief was a gradual awareness of guilt for actively participating in the creation of terrible weapons. And it was difficult for him to come to terms with the climate of global secrecy at the nuclear center. His protests against this secrecy were borderline foul. For example, he asked his wife to send him letters of innocent content in envelopes in which these letters were cut into many fragments, forcing the censor to put together such “puzzles” in order to read the text to detect violations of secrecy. The skills of designing and creating complex devices allowed Richard to understand the devices and coding of all types of locks for safes in which secret documents were stored. Sometimes he left a note in the safe: “Guess who?” In the Soviet Union of those years, such antics would hardly have gone without severe punishment. In 1952, Feynman obtained permission to withdraw from the atomic project. Along with his studies in theoretical physics, he identified teaching as the goal of his life. In 1952, he became a professor at the California Institute of Technology and worked in this position for 36 years. He gave his last lecture for masters two weeks before his death. His “Feynman Lectures on Physics” and “The Nature of Physical Laws” speak about the merits of his teaching activities. In all his lectures, both on general physics and in special courses on the most complex problems of modern physics, he avoids unnecessary mathematical formalism and develops his own intuitive approach without regard to authorities. He presents the physical world as a world of amazing mysteries and attempts to solve them. The students idolized their professor. At the funeral ceremony in February 1988, they carried a sign that read, “We love you, Dick.” But that’s not the only reason he became a legend. Feynman had unusually varied interests. Having become interested in the mysterious ancient manuscripts of the Mayan Indians, he entered historical science as a scientist who unraveled the mathematical and astronomical achievements of the ancient inhabitants of the American continent. At the end of his life, when he was already seriously ill, Feynman became unusually interested in the history and culture of the Tuvan people.
