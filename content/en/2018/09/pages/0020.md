in his youth, he began repairing radios by thinking about the task, and now he began to carefully mentally analyze all the stages of launching a spacecraft. Feynman eventually concluded that the only cause of the accident could have been depressurization of the engine compartments containing the rocket fuel components. The separators of these compartments contained elastic rubber gaskets. Of course, the separator materials were thoroughly tested for strength and elastic properties. But this test was carried out at ordinary, earthly temperatures. And all the designers forgot that very sub-zero temperatures can significantly change the properties of rubber - making it not elastic, but hard and even brittle. This cause of the accident, too offensive for the American cosmonautics, was indignantly rejected by almost all members of the commission. And Feynman had to spend a lot of energy proving his idea. He showed real diplomatic skill and in the end, at a press conference, he clearly demonstrated how rubber, when lowered into ice water, changes its elastic properties, and when lowered into liquid nitrogen, it becomes completely brittle. For the edification of future rocket designers, Feynman outlined this “rubber” epic in detail in the second volume of his memoirs, “Why do YOU ​​care what others think?” And the first volume of the memoirs is titled “Of course you’re joking, Mr. Feynman!” These books are completely different from ordinary memoirs or autobiographies. Feynman did not write them - he told them to his friend Ralph Layton, to whom he gave the copyright to the books. Written down almost verbatim by Layton, these creations of Feynman have undoubted literary merit. Such were all his lectures and public appearances. Feynman’s speech “The Value of Science,” which he delivered at a meeting of the US Academy of Sciences in the fall of 1955, is extremely interesting. Then it just appeared

the hydrogen bomb and the Cold War ignited. And the question of the value of science, the question of whether science is good or evil, has become relevant. Subsequently, publishing this speech for the first time, Feynman writes: “...When I saw what horror science could generate, I asked myself the question: what is the value of the science to which I devoted myself as the thing I loved? “The Value of Science is a kind of report containing many of the thoughts I had as I tried to answer this question.” Feynman puts forward three aspects of the value of science. x The first one is “...familiar to everyone: scientific knowledge gives us the opportunity to do different things and create different things.” x The second is “...the intellectual pleasure which some people get from reading scientific books, studying science and thinking about it, and which other people get from working in it.” x The third, to which Feynman attaches particular importance, sharply distinguishes the scientific worldview from the religious one: “We scientists take it for granted that it is absolutely normal to be unsure about something... This is not a new idea, it is an idea of ​​​​the age of reason. This is the idea that guided the people who created the democracy in which we live... If we want to solve a problem that has never been solved before, then we must leave the door to the unknown ajar... Our responsibility, as scientists who understand the great progress that the amazing philosophy of ignorance produces, the great progress that is the fruit of freedom of thought, is to proclaim the value of this freedom, to teach not to be afraid of doubt, but to welcome it, discussing all its pros and cons. And to demand this freedom for all future generations, which is our duty to them.” This is the will of the legendary physicist Richard Feynman, which he left to us.
