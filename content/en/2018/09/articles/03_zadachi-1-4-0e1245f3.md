1. At the entrance to the amusement park there is an electronic board showing the time (hours and minutes). When the board showed 9:00, six attractions opened in the park and operated until the evening for 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6 minutes, respectively, with a minute break. When Oleg came to the park during the day, not a single attraction was working. What time did the board show at that moment? E.Bratsev

3. Quantik and Noutik show such a trick. The viewer thinks of any six different integers from 1 to 125 and communicates them only to Noutik. After this, Noutik names Quantik some five of them, and Quantik guesses the sixth number conceived by the viewer. Suggest a way that Quantic and Noutik could act so that the trick always succeeds. I. Akulich

2. Using a ruler and a drawn part of the coordinate grid, you need to construct the bisector of angle A. What is the smallest number of segments required for this? (Using a ruler is only allowed to draw straight lines.) E. Bakaev, V. Rastorguev

4. The ¥100 square is divided into dominoes. Is it possible to guarantee that it can be cut into two connected parts, symmetrical to each other relative to the center of the square, without cutting a single domino? (A part is called connected if from any of its cells it is possible to reach any other, making transitions to the cell adjacent on the side and without going beyond the boundary of the figure.) E. Bakaev

The oldest mathematician

The famous German algebraist Ernst Eduard Kummer (1810–1893) was very poor at mental math. If, while giving a lecture, he needed to perform a simple calculation, he usually resorted to the help of students. One day he had to multiply 7 by 9. He began to reason out loud: “Hm... it can’t be 61, because 61 is a prime number.” It can't be 65 either, because 65 is divisible by 5, 67 is also a prime number, and 69 is clearly too many. Only 63 left...

One of the most prolific mathematicians of the twentieth century, P. Erdos (1913–1996), in his old age often made fun of his venerable age. So one day, when asked how old he was, he answered: “Two and a half billion.” Because when I was very young, scientists thought that the age of the Earth was two billion years, and now it is believed that it is already four and a half billion years. From the book “Mathematicians also joke”
