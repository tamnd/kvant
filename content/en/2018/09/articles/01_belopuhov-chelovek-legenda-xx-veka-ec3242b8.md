Man-legend of the 20th century

(on the 100th anniversary of the birth of Richard Feynman)

L. BELOPUKHOV

IN 2018, unnoticed by the scientific community, let alone the general public, the centenary of the birth of Richard Phillips Feynman passed. Almost everyone who comes into contact with physics knows about the famous “Feynman Lectures on Physics”. In our country in 1965–67, these nine small books were a subscription publication, and when they appeared on the public market, they instantly disappeared from the shelves of the scientific departments of bookstores. In fact, “Lectures” are notes edited by Feynman of lectures on general physics that he gave in the early 60s to junior students at the California Institute of Technology, the famous Caltech. Physicists Robert Layton and Matthew Sands, who were present at all lectures, carefully took notes on their colleague’s lectures, and Feynman considered them full co-authors of his course. In 1963–64, the Lectures were published as a three-volume course in the United States and England, and then translated and published in 16 additional countries. A little later, in 1965, a natural addition to the Lectures was published - a collection of problems and exercises that were used in student seminars at Caltech. The total circulation of the Lectures in English alone exceeded one and a half million copies.

These diagrams turned out to be very convenient and visual in all cases of interaction of particles through an electric field. Feynman used the concept of the electric field as a continuum of virtual photons and developed a quantum electrodynamic method for calculating the probabilistic production and absorption of photons. In quantum mechanics, he created a method of integration along trajectories, which is increasingly used in various issues every year. This list alone is already enough to rank Feynman among the most outstanding physicists of the twentieth century. But this is not the only reason why he became a legend and remained in the memory of his contemporaries. Here are brief extracts from Feynman's biography. His parents were Jewish immigrants who moved to the United States at the turn of the 20th century, his father from Minsk, his mother from a small Polish town. They lived in Queens, New York's largest suburban borough. Thanks to hard work and abilities, the family became quite wealthy. Feynman's father did not have a systematic education, but was able to convey to his son (and daughter) an interest in natural phenomena and a desire to explain them. From his mother, Richard inherited an inexhaustible sense of humor in relation to all life's collisions. The parents' secret dream was for Richard to become

Moreover, not only the son, but also their daughter Joan also devoted her life to science and became an outstanding astrophysicist. At an early age, Richard became interested in physics, and at the age of 13 he gained fame (and even income) as a repairman of radios, the structure of which could then be understood “at a glance,” since all their parts (radio tubes, coils and capacitors) were large. As Feynman later joked, his main difficulty was opening the cover of the radio. He amazed customers by the fact that, having opened the device, he did not just climb inside with a screwdriver, but began to think (and sometimes walk around the room while doing so). At the age of 15, Richard independently mastered trigonometry and the basics of mathematical analysis. Despite this, he was denied admission to Columbia University in New York because of his nationality (in the 1930s, many US universities had quotas for admitting people of certain nationalities, including Jews).

Feynman began studying physics at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. The sophomore's scientific papers appeared in many physics journals. He completed his master's and doctoral studies at the prestigious Princeton University and began teaching physics there. In 1943, he was invited to the Los Alamos laboratory in California, where work began on the construction of an atomic bomb. The young Doctor of Philosophy (Physics) headed the department for the application of mathematical methods in solving numerous physical and engineering questions arising in connection with a completely new problem. Naturally, work in this laboratory was associated with the highest degree of secrecy. The fact is that before leaving Princeton, Richard Feynman formalized his marriage to Arlene Greenbaum, who was already terminally ill with thyroid tuberculosis. He fell in love with her when they were 13 years old, and at the age of 19 they became engaged, but Richard’s parents prevented the marriage from happening for a long time. Arlene died in 1946 in the arms of her husband. For some time Richard felt empty. Added to personal grief was a gradual awareness of guilt for actively participating in the creation of terrible weapons. And it was difficult for him to come to terms with the climate of global secrecy at the nuclear center. His protests against this secrecy were borderline foul. For example, he asked his wife to send him letters of innocent content in envelopes in which these letters were cut into many fragments, forcing the censor to put together such “puzzles” in order to read the text to detect violations of secrecy. The skills of designing and creating complex devices allowed Richard to understand the devices and coding of all types of locks for safes in which secret documents were stored. In 1952, Feynman obtained permission to withdraw from the atomic project. Along with his studies in theoretical physics, he identified teaching as the goal of his life. In 1952, he became a professor at the California Institute of Technology and worked in this position for 36 years. He gave his last lecture for masters two weeks before his death. His “Feynman Lectures on Physics” and “The Nature of Physical Laws” speak about the merits of his teaching activities. In all his lectures, both on general physics and in special courses on the most complex problems of modern physics, he avoids unnecessary mathematical formalism and develops his own intuitive approach without regard to authorities. He presents the physical world as a world of amazing mysteries and attempts to solve them. The students idolized their professor. At the funeral ceremony in February 1988, they carried a sign that read, “We love you, Dick.” But that’s not the only reason he became a legend. Feynman had unusually varied interests. Having become interested in the mysterious ancient manuscripts of the Mayan Indians, he entered historical science as a scientist who unraveled the mathematical and astronomical achievements of the ancient inhabitants of the American continent. At the end of his life, when he was already seriously ill, Feynman became unusually interested in the history and culture of the Tuvan people. The twenty-eighth of January 1981 became a day of mourning for the American astronautics and the entire country - a short time after launch, the Challenger space shuttle exploded with seven astronauts on board. The causes of the tragedy were completely unclear - even the intervention of aliens was seriously discussed. In the end, a presidential commission was created, in which Feynman was invited to participate. Despite his cancer, he agreed to participate in this commission and, who knows, maybe this extended his life, since his participation was very active and ultimately became decisive for unraveling the cause of the disaster. He did not think about his illness and courageously overcame all the difficulties of the interoperative periods. His colleagues on the commission often did not know about the operations he was undergoing. It is characteristic that the decisive role in Feynman’s agreement to work on the commission was not even a personal appeal from the President of the United States, but a loving wife, who apparently understood that this would help her husband prolong his life. Feynman became the black sheep of the commission. Feynman eventually concluded that the only cause of the accident could have been depressurization of the engine compartments containing the rocket fuel components. The separators of these compartments contained elastic rubber gaskets. Of course, the separator materials were thoroughly tested for strength and elastic properties. But this test was carried out at ordinary, earthly temperatures. And all the designers forgot that very sub-zero temperatures can significantly change the properties of rubber - making it not elastic, but hard and even brittle. This cause of the accident, too offensive for the American cosmonautics, was indignantly rejected by almost all members of the commission. And Feynman had to spend a lot of energy proving his idea. He showed real diplomatic skill and in the end, at a press conference, he clearly demonstrated how rubber, when lowered into ice water, changes its elastic properties, and when lowered into liquid nitrogen, it becomes completely brittle. For the edification of future rocket designers, Feynman outlined this “rubber” epic in detail in the second volume of his memoirs, “Why do YOU ​​care what others think?” And the first volume of the memoirs is titled “Of course you’re joking, Mr. Feynman!” These books are completely different from ordinary memoirs or autobiographies. Feynman did not write them - he told them to his friend Ralph Layton, to whom he gave the copyright to the books. Written down almost verbatim by Layton, these creations of Feynman have undoubted literary merit. Such were all his lectures and public appearances.
