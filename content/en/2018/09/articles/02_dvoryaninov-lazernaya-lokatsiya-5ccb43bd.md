Laser ranging

S. DVORYANINOV

DURING THE SECOND WORLD WAR, British pilots planned a night raid, which had to be carried out at an extremely low altitude - about 20 meters. This saved the planes from being detected by the enemy and from anti-aircraft fire. But how does a pilot maintain this low, constant altitude? It is known that altitude can be determined using a barometric altimeter - the higher, the lower the atmospheric pressure. But at low altitudes these devices do not provide the necessary accuracy. Now we know, for example, about laser ranging of the Moon. A laser beam is directed at its surface, and the reflected signal is picked up by a receiver on Earth. By

the time it takes for the signal to travel back and forth, the distance to the Moon is calculated. Since the 1970s, the accuracy of measuring this distance has reached 2–3 centimeters. In this case, corner reflectors installed on the Moon are used. Laser ranging is a special case of echolocation. The wave sent and then reflected can be an audio wave, an ultrasonic wave, or a radio wave. The corresponding devices are radars, sonars, sonars, echo sounders, flaw detectors, and medical ultrasound devices. But they were not used by British pilots. Everything was much simpler, at the level of school geometry. In fact, the task boiled down to constructing a triangle using a side and the height drawn to it. On the fuselage of the aircraft - at the nose and at the tail - two searchlights were attached, the beams of which could be directed downward at any angle to the aircraft. The result was a triangle with a side equal to the length of the aircraft and two adjacent angles. It is clear that these angles can be chosen so that the height of the triangle is equal to the given flight altitude h. This corresponds to the fact that the searchlight beams intersect at a distance h from the fuselage. Then, in a real British combat operation, h = 18.5 m. And such accuracy was achieved. The required angles for the spotlights were calculated in advance. During an airplane flight at an arbitrary altitude, the navigator saw two spots of light below on the ground or on the water. Their relative position depended on the flight altitude. Looking at them, the navigator gave instructions to the pilot to change altitude. The required height was achieved when two light spots merged into one. Really simple and effective!
