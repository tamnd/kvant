In the vacuum chamber (and inside the cryostat) the pressure should not exceed $10^{-} 4$ Pa, which is a billion times less than atmospheric pressure. To create such a vacuum in the huge volume of the cryostat and vacuum chamber, 400 pumps of various types must work for at least two weeks. The walls of the vacuum chamber are double. Like the walls of the cryostat, they are a cooling thermos in which the coolant is distilled water under a pressure of 4 MPa. Arriving at a temperature of $70 \infty C$, water will pass through the walls of the vacuum chamber at high speed, heating up to $120 \infty C$ and taking with it the bulk of the energy released in the thermonuclear reaction. At the same time, the water, leaving the walls of the chamber, is just beginning to boil and makes a noise exactly like a kettle starting to boil. This noise will be carefully monitored by acoustic instruments - as an indicator of the normal operation of the reactor. Active boiling begins when water leaves the chamber and its pressure begins to drop. The tokamak does not provide for the use of water steam to rotate the turbine generator turbines and generate electric current. This is not an industrial, but an experimental reactor. It is designed to test all individual elements of a thermonuclear device. Therefore, the steam-water mixture, after cooling, cleaning and checking for purity, will again flow to where it takes away thermal energy - into the “thermos” space of the walls of the vacuum chamber. This has some similarities with the first atomic (plutonium) reactors (1942, Chicago, E. Fermi; 1946, Moscow, I.V. Kurchatov). The inner walls of the vacuum chamber, facing the plasma cord, are lined with 440 removable blankets. These are the main objects of the tokamak, which absorb the main energy of the thermonuclear reaction - the kinetic energy of the generated neutrons, which have enormous speeds of the order of 20,000 km/s. As a result of numerous successive collisions, neutrons transfer their energy to beryllium nuclei and are slowed down. The blanket heats up, but not very much (up to $240 \infty C ).$ Further heating is prevented by the continuous washing of the inner cavity of the chamber with water, which, when heated, carries away the reaction energy. Thermal radiation of the plasma cord, which has a temperature of 150 million degrees, also makes a significant contribution to the heating of the water. The remaining unabsorbed part of the radiation passes into the interior to the walls of the cryostat and gives up its energy to cold liquid nitrogen. There is still more the “tail” of radiation is gamma quanta. To absorb them, the cryostat is surrounded on the outside and on top by a powerful concrete protection from 1.5 to 3 meters thick. Standing behind this concrete wall, it will be difficult to imagine that inside there is a “glass” of the cryostat, surrounded by various pipes and mechanisms. And inside the cryostat there is a donut chamber the size of a three-story house, surrounded by giant magnets. If you were to look inside this donut, you would find an unbearably bright ring of hydrogen plasma hanging in a vacuum - a source of thermonuclear energy, like in a fairy tale - a chest, inside the box - an egg, or, if you like, nesting dolls, each of which, however, does not copy the previous one.
