Each of group 1 considers these two pieces in total to be strictly greater than $D_{1}^{⋯} D_{s}$, i.e. more than the entire group's share of loot! Since everyone considers A + I strictly greater than $D_{1}^{⋯} D_{s}$, it means that we can choose a positive number $G_{1}$ such that everyone in the first group considers A + I greater than $D_{1}^{⋯} D_{s} G_{1}$.

The technique we use here: if X > Y, then we can choose a positive number a such that X > Y + a, is also quite typical for mathematical analysis. From the strictness of the inequality we can extract some reserve for further constructions.

What about the second group? Each of them considers the parts A + I to be less than $D_{1}^{⋯} D_{s}$ (these consider I to be less than M ). So, the rest, i.e. part B, each of them counts more than $D_{s} 1^{⋯} D_{n}$. Again, we can choose $G_{2}$ such that everyone in the second group evaluates part B as greater than $D_{s} 1^{⋯} D_{n} G_{2}$. So, we can already see the light at the end of the tunnel: we managed to divide the robbers into two groups, allocate shares to these groups, and everyone is also sure that they get a little more than they need, i.e. We're getting some much-needed stock! Let's choose G as the minimum of $G_{1}$ and $G_{2}$ in order to further work with one number symbolizing our margin of accuracy. Now, in accordance with our plan, we must use the inductive assumption so that the first group divides the combined piece A + I into its robbers, and the second - piece B. Why do we not exceed our margin of accuracy? Let us choose H to be much less than G. Now let's look at the division process, which is provided to us by inductive assumption. This division consists of a sequence of elementary divisions, when some robber divides something in some proportions. So let such elementary divisions be carried out in such a way that everyone else agrees, and with precision,

which doubles with each subsequent elementary division. Thus, let the first section be carried out in such a way that all other n robbers agree up to H 2, the second so that everyone agrees up to H 4, and so on. In other words, if the kth section is carried out (no matter which robber), then everyone else agrees with it up to $H 2^{k}$. With such divisions, it turns out that the opinions of any two robbers on any two non-overlapping pieces of loot can differ only by less than H. Now let’s check that there really won’t be any envy. Within the first group, no two robbers envy each other, according to the inductive assumption. Likewise within the second group. Let's look at robbers from different parts. Their opinions on the resulting pieces can differ by no more than H, with $H d G_{1}$ and $H d G_{2}$, i.e. section errors are negligible compared to our existing inventory. At the same time, each robber thinks that he received a little more than he was entitled to (since a little more was allocated for everyone). Algebraically, this can be expressed as follows: $A_{ii} D_{i}$! 1. And about the robbers from the other part, each of them thinks that they received less than they were entitled to: $A_{ij} D_{j} < 1.$ But then $A_{ii} D_{i}$! $A_{ij} D_{j}$. And therefore, $A_{ii} A_{ij}$! $D_{i} D_{j}$, i.e. the envy condition is satisfied.

Epilogue

Let's summarize our work. We managed to find the key to the problem when we played a little with the condition: first we weakened it a little, then we strengthened it a little, considering the generalized condition of envy. Two ideas are key. The first is to consider dividing the spoils with some precision. And the second is the possibility of replacing an elementary section with a sequence of sections, but such that everyone agrees with the section with some accuracy.
