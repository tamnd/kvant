KVANT 2018/№10

Generally: if several robbers value some small piece of loot as greater than $G_{2}$, and the rest - as less than $G_{1}$, and $G_{1} G_{2}$, then we can accept $H G_{2} G_{1}$. Now if you give this piece to the first group, then the second group will not envy the first within H, i.e. we have that very small margin to set the accuracy of further divisions and apply the technique that we came up with. It is curious that if all the robbers value just any small part of the loot differently, then we can order their ratings in ascending order: $P_{1} d P_{2} d^{…} d P k P k_{1} d^{…} d P_{n}$ and, taking any two unequal consecutive ratings $P k P k_{1}$, divide the robbers into the two groups described above: the first group will include robbers with ratings no less than $G_{2} P k_{1}$, and the second group - with ratings no more $G_{1} P k$. If we give such a piece to the first group of robbers, they will be able to divide it among themselves according to the assumption of induction, this is understandable. But having given a small piece to some of the robbers, the rest of the loot will no longer have to be divided equally, but in a certain ratio, and for this case we do not have an induction assumption! What to do? Let's generalize the condition again. We will consider not a division equally into n envious robbers, but a division with some assigned rule $D_{1} : D_{2} :^{⋯} : D_{n}$. The condition of envy here will be generalized: everyone, relative to everyone else, wants to receive a share no less than that suggested by the given proportion. Let's put it this way. Let $A_{ij}$ be how the i-th robber evaluates the j-th piece of loot. Then for any pair i and j the inequality $A_{ii} A_{ij} t D_{i} D_{j}$ must be true. In other words, the i-th robber estimates the ratio of his share to the j-th robber's share as no less than the ratio of their shares in the assigned rule. If all $D_{i}$ are equal to 1, then the usual equal division problem with envy results.

If we now carry out the section with a generalized condition of envy, then the inductive hypothesis can also be used with a generalized condition, and this is much more convenient! But, really, we need to re-prove the base. However, the basis for two robbers is precisely the problem about the division in the ratio D: E. For two robbers, the envious case is no different from the non-envious one.

Final algorithm

Let us now return to the problem with the generalized condition of envy. Let’s say that for the number of robbers from 1 to n – 1, we already know how to divide. We need to make an inductive transition. Let's try to formalize all the ideas and constructions that we have come up with so far. Let the first robber divide the spoils in the original proportions. If everyone agrees, then the algorithm is completed. Otherwise, there will be a piece I for which some robbers value more than a certain number M (let’s call it group 1, let these be the first s robbers), and the other part values ​​less than M (group 2).

We can assume that everyone considers piece I small, i.e. less than any number chosen in advance. Indeed, we can divide I into a billion identical parts with an error less than the maximum estimate of I among robbers. In addition, we can assume that exactly in M ​​no one values ​​piece I (see the reasoning above).

Note that there are only n differences between the robbers’ estimates of piece I and the number M; for the first group they are positive, for the second they are negative. This means that we can choose a small positive number Z that is less than the absolute values ​​of all these n differences. Thus, within Z, the second group will not envy the first. This piece I is for group 1, let's put it aside for now. And we will divide the rest of the spoils into two parts A and B in the ratio $D_{1}^{⋯} D_{s} M : D_{s} 1^{⋯} D_{n}$ so that everyone agrees with this division up to Z. Then how does group 1 evaluate the two pieces A and I together?
