Consider the surface defined by the equation z = xy – a hyperbolic paraboloid (Fig. 6,a). This surface contains a lot of straight lines. Straight lines on it are divided into two types - “vertical” and “horizontal”. For any real number b we denote by $H_{b}$ the horizontal line of the form $( t, b, bt ), t \in R$. It can be seen that it really lies on a paraboloid. Similarly, we will call vertical lines $V_{a}$ lines of the form $( a, t, at ), t \in_{R}$. It is easy to see that any two lines of the same type do not intersect, any two lines of different types intersect, and no two lines lie in the same plane. This means that if we take as L the set of $n^{2} 2$ vertical lines of our paraboloid and $n^{2} 2$ horizontal ones, then we will get $n^{4} 4$ intersection points - again more than our desired estimate. What to do? The hyperbolic paraboloid in this case is an example of a doubly ruled surface - an algebraic surface (i.e. the set of zeros of some polynomial in three variables in three-dimensional space - in this case it is the polynomial z - xy), through any point of which there are exactly two straight lines. The conditions for the surface are quite strict, and indeed, a remarkable fact from the science called differential geometry tells us that all such surfaces essentially reduce to hyperbolic paraboloids or single-sheet hyperboloids (Fig.6,b) - surfaces of type $x^{2} + y^{2} - z^{2} = 1$. As mentioned above, we call an algebraic surface in three-dimensional space a set of points of the form f ( x, y, z ) = 0, where f is a polynomial in three variables. For any finite set of points, there is an infinite number of algebraic surfaces that can be drawn through them. In some cases, these may be very simple surfaces, defined by polynomials of not too high degrees - for example, all points may turn out to lie on the same straight line.
