There are several mechanisms for the formation of such a potential well for different atoms or molecules, but they are not considered in detail. In the simplest model, it is considered that the interaction of molecules is “pairwise”, i.e. for the two nearest molecules there is a binding energy $U_{0}$, and their interaction is not affected by the presence of other molecules. In other words, molecules that are not located side by side in a condensed substance do not interact in this model. Then, if there are Z nearest neighbors, the molecule is in a potential well of depth $ZU_{0}$. The heat of fusion and the heat of vaporization of a substance per molecule are proportional to the depth of such a hole and depend on the number of nearest neighbors that are located next to each molecule. It is logical to assume that in order to destroy the crystalline structure during the transition from solid to liquid, large pieces of a substance and pieces of small volumes will require different amounts of heat per molecule. After all, in small pieces of solid matter, a significant proportion of molecules are located on the surface, where they have a smaller number of nearest neighbors and, accordingly, are located in potential holes that are shallower in depth. Experiments measuring the melting temperature of thin films of different substances showed that the melting temperature of the film of each substance decreases with decreasing film thickness. This is the so-called size effect. It also turned out that the melting temperature depends not only on the size, but also on the shape of the sample, i.e. There is also a shape effect. This is due to the fact that the ratio of the number of molecules living on the surface to the total number of molecules depends on both the size and shape of a piece of the substance. If we assume that the melting temperature is proportional to the depth of the potential well, averaged over all molecules that make up a piece of the substance, then the detected effects can be “explained” within the framework of the model of pairwise interaction of molecules.
