Another numerical characteristic of a graph is its independence number, which is denoted by ind (G). This is the largest number of vertices that can be selected so that no two are connected by an edge. For example, for the Petersen graph this number is four. There is a beautiful conjecture due to Graffiti, according to which for any connected graph its independence number is not less than the average distance: if the average distance is large, then there should be many independent points. This hypothesis was proven by my favorite co-author Fan Chun. So, this proof is not easy, but what is interesting here is that Graffiti is not a person, but a computer program created by Simeon Faitovich from the University of Houston. This program has formulated about 6000 hypotheses in graph theory and geometry. The creator of this program is a specialist in the field of artificial intelligence. The hardest part about these computer-generated hypotheses, he says, is figuring out whether a particular hypothesis is interesting. He took 50–60 different invariants of the graph (well, for example: eigenvalues ​​of the incidence matrix), and then, using a computer program, he looked for relationships between these invariants. He tests them on various graphs, on graphs that are considered especially difficult, and if these tests do not find a counterexample, he formulates a hypothesis. Many of these hypotheses have been studied. Some turned out to be false - a "big" counterexample was found, some were proven or turned out to be trivial, but about many we do not know whether they are true or not. So while computers are bad at proving theorems, they are good at formulating hypotheses. Will we ever catch up with them?
