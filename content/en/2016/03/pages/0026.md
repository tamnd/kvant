"QUANT" FOR JUNIOR SCHOOLCHILDREN

Tasks

1.

Dunno has five cards with numbers: 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5. Help him make two numbers from these cards - a three-digit one and a two-digit one - so that the first number is divisible by the second. A. Shapovalov

⟦column⟧

4.

One angle of a triangle is equal to $60\infty$, and the side opposite this angle is equal to one third of the perimeter of the triangle. Prove that this triangle is equilateral. M. Volchkevich

2.

Two snails crawled across the donut-shaped surface of the planet, leaving traces behind them: one along the red line, and the other along the blue line (as on figure). How many parts do the snail tracks divide the surface of the planet into? S. Smirnov, I. Yashchenko

5.

Anya wanted to write in each cell of the table

5 ¥ 8 one digit at a time so that each digit occurs in exactly four rows. (We count both columns and rows of the table as rows.) Prove that she will not succeed. E. Bakaev

These problems are primarily intended for students in grades 6–8. These problems were proposed at the XXVII Mathematical Festival.

Illustrations by D. Grishukova

3.

At the competition “Come on, monsters!” 15 dragons stand in a row. The number of heads among neighbors differs by 1. If a dragon has more heads than both of its neighbors, it is considered cunning, if it has fewer than both neighbors, it is considered strong, and the rest (including those standing on the edge) are considered ordinary. There are exactly four cunning dragons in a row - with 4, 6, 7 and 7 heads and exactly three strong ones - with 3, 3 and 6 heads. The first and last dragons have the same number of heads. a) Give an example of how this could happen. b) Prove that the number of heads of the first dragon is the same in all examples. A. Shapovalov, I. Yashchenko
