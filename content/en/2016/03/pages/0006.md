in cryptography, public key encryption, the strength of which is based on the fact that it is believed that it is difficult to factor large numbers into a product of prime numbers. In the photo next to it is Ron Rivest, whose last name is part of the acronym RSA, which is the name for one of the public key encryption systems. One way to solve such a cipher is to factor a large number into prime factors. Rivest published a 129-digit number and invited everyone to factor it. He was confident that using the methods known at the time, it would take about 4 ◊ $10^{16}$ years. This number was published in an article in The Times about 20 years ago. In fact, the decomposition was found in about 20 years, so the estimate of $10^{16}$ turned out to be, let’s say, overly pessimistic. Nowadays, in many situations where confidentiality is required (for example, when working with bank cards), the strength of encryption depends on the difficulty of decomposing large numbers. If an effective way to find such expansions is discovered, then a lot will change. It turns out that the number written in 1031 consecutive units is prime. Is this the last prime number of this type? Paul Erdős would say that every broad-minded person knows that there are infinitely many prime numbers of this kind, but he cannot prove it. It looks like a number written in 270343 consecutive ones could be prime: it hasn't been proven yet, but it behaves very similarly, and it satisfies many of the necessary conditions for primeness.
