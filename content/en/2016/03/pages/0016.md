Exercise 7. Estimate the angular width of Alexander's dark space in degrees.

Let's make a small addition. Judging by figure 9, the apex of the cone with a half-angle of $42\infty$ lies near the rear surface of the drop. And figure 14 shows that the apex of the cone with a half-angle of $51\infty$ is located near the front surface of the drop. This leads to the fact that near the drop the larger cone is located inside the smaller cone, which is also confirmed by figure 15. Experiment with a flask. We are almost done with Descartes' theory. Let's try to reproduce his experiment again. The “drop” will be a 250 ml round-bottomed flask. A white circle is drawn on a computer on a black background - an image of the sun, and this image is launched through a projector. To observe the rainbow, a sheet of Whatman paper with a circular hole in the middle was used as a screen. At figure 16 the bulb and the screen are visible, and the projector, together with the computer, is located behind the screen and illuminates the bulb through a hole. The flask is, of course, not a spherical vessel; it has a neck, so only its spherical part was filled with water. Therefore, the circular arcs on figure 16 are only partially visible, while the lower and upper parts of the image on the screen are formed by the horizontal surface of the water in the bulb. Where is the first arc, and where is the second? Here are the signs by which we can distinguish them. The second is less bright, due to the fact that the rays that form it experience additional reflection inside the drop. Moreover, in the first arc the colors from inside to outside go from violet to red.

The arcs face each other with violet sides, and the space between them is filled with violet. This space, located near the drop, can by analogy be called the violet Alexander space. Judging by figure 15, the standard relative position of the arcs, when the first lies inside the second, is restored if the distance from the drop to the screen is more than ten radii of the drop. And, of course, when observing an ordinary natural rainbow, the distance from raindrops to the observer’s eye many times exceeds this limit.
