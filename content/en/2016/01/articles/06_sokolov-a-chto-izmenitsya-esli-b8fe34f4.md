You, of course, know that due to their established reputation, Alice the fox and Basilio the cat are always simply forced to give incorrect answers. What exactly are the mistakes these characters have made now? Let us recall the basic principles that underlie geometric optics (Fig.3). Light is a stream of rays. They are emitted by luminous bodies (light sources) in infinite quantities and in all directions. A non-luminous surface becomes illuminated when light rays fall on it. We see objects when light rays coming from them enter our eyes. So says theory number 1. But there is also theory No. 2 in geometric optics, it says everything

The reminder is over. Now about the mistakes of Alice the fox and Basilio the cat. The fox correctly said that all 100% of the rays falling on the mirror will be reflected from it. This is already true by the definition of a mirror surface. But Alice did not take into account the fact that the reflected rays can simply pass by point O. Basilio the cat correctly constructed the path of the beams of rays reflected from the mirror (see Fig. 2). From his constructions it is clear that after reflection, only the rays of the first lamp will actually reach point O. But this does not mean at all that such rays will add to the illumination the same contribution $E_{1} = E_{0}$ as given by the rays coming directly from the first lamp. In fact, their contribution will be less. This is what Basilio the cat didn’t even think about. You can think about what determines the contributions to illumination created by various light sources when completing the following two tasks.

Lecture given by the Fairy with Azure Hair What is the main difficulty of geometric optics? The fact is that it works with an infinite number of rays. Of course, no one can examine an infinite number of rays. Therefore, the art of solving problems of geometric optics comes down to the ability to choose the right beam. Sometimes the so-called rule of extremes helps with this: If you don’t know where to start a solution, consider the most extreme elements (leftmost, rightmost, largest, smallest, etc.). Try to find the “outer” rays yourself in the following tasks.

The second method, which is useful to get acquainted with, is to transform the “real light source – mirror” system given to us into the “imaginary light source – wall with window” system (Fig. 6). This is done in two steps. First, we construct an image of our source S in the mirror, call it an imaginary source $S '$ and assume that light rays emerge from it, just like from a real source. Do you know where the image of an object is in the mirror? At the same distance from the mirror as the object itself, only on the other side - in “Through the Looking Glass” (Fig. 6,a). Secondly, we turn the plane of the mirror into an opaque imaginary wall, and the mirror into a window in this wall (Fig. 6, b). What is the point of such a construction? And the fact is that now, drawing along a ruler all the rays that come out of the imaginary source and pass through the window, we will construct the entire set of rays reflected from the Fig. 8 mirror. And note that we don't have to bother plotting the angles of incidence and reflection. The beam we need is highlighted at figure 6, b, in red. To construct it, we simply drew a straight line along the ruler through the points $S '$ and A. Try using this technique to solve the problem about Pinocchio.
