In total, 1000 roosters were eaten. How many chickens did you eat? Admitting defeat, I asked for an extension until tomorrow and in the evening I took up the task in a calm atmosphere. At first I tried, at least approximately, to depict the chicken “family tree” - and was horrified by the abundance of possible options already in the first generations. In fact, from a dozen eggs laid by the “original” (i.e. bought on the market) chicken, from zero to ten chickens could hatch (however, zero is impossible, because then the chicken race would end there, and where would a thousand roosters come from?). In the second generation, the range of options is even wider... in general, darkness! However, to clear my conscience, I drew a certain “average typical” beginning of the process described in the problem, indicating the chickens with bold dots, and the transitions to the next generations with segments directed like a fan downwards (with exactly 10 segments drawn from each chicken). Therefore, the segments that simply break off without continuation are the same roosters that ended their mortal journey in someone else’s stomach. The result is approximately what is shown in the diagram. Is it really true that the required number of chickens eaten (with a constant thousand roosters) does not depend on the type of tree? Well, let's take some piece of wood, starting from any chicken (and further down), and hook it to any free end (no matter what generation). At the same time, we seem to swap places of one of the roosters with some kind of chicken. As a result, the total number of both roosters and hens will not change, but the tree will become different, but in compliance with all the requirements of the condition! Let's reconnect again - we get a new tree with the same numerical characteristics. Perhaps, by using such transfers it will be possible to obtain some simpler tree and directly count the number of chickens in it? For example, a tree where in each generation, except the last, exactly

one chicken. To do this, you can each time choose an arbitrary generation where there are at least two chickens, and any of these chickens (along with the entire piece coming from it) can be re-hooked... well, of course - to the very bottom of the tree (to any free end in the lowest generation). Then the number of generations after each recoupling will increase. But it cannot grow indefinitely - after all, we are simply rearranging the pieces in the final tree. This means that the process of recoupling will be completed, namely at the moment when in each generation, except the initial and last, there will be exactly one hen and 9 roosters. What happens? Even without drawing anything, you can easily determine that in total in all generations except the initial (the original hen) and the last (“ten-rooster”), 1000 –10 = 990 roosters were bred, and therefore there were 990 such generations: 9 = 110, and together with the last generation it turns out 110 + 1 = 111. But each such generation is generated by one hen, so there were also chickens exactly the same amount, i.e. 111 goals. All! The next day I proudly gave this answer to my friend. “That's right,” he said. - How did you decide? I explained, anticipating praise for my clever solution to a difficult problem. “It’s too complicated,” he dumbfounded me. – We need to decide differently. Let's calculate the number of eggs laid in two ways. Let there be x chickens in total. On the one hand, each chicken laid 10 eggs, and since there were x chickens, there were 10x eggs. On the other hand, all the chickens, except the very first one (bought at the market), hatched from eggs laid by the same chickens. Therefore, it took x – 1 eggs to “produce” chickens. Well, all 1000 roosters that were eaten also hatched from eggs. In total, it turns out that (x – 1) + + 1000 = x + 999 eggs were laid. So we determined the number of eggs laid in two ways. Since this is the same value, then 10x = x + 999. Hence x = 111, just like you. I had to admit that this really turns out to be clearer and easier. But who would guess this? A few days later, I couldn’t resist and suggested the same problem to another friend of mine, hoping in advance to laugh at his clumsy attempts. So what? He answered almost immediately, and began seemingly in exactly the same way: “Let there be x chickens,” he said, but then suddenly changed the music. – Since each hen gave birth to 10 other birds and 1000 of them were roosters, in total they were born from eggs
