Physicists, engineers and people in general are interested in the interaction of radiation with matter precisely because it can be dangerous to humans. It occurs during the operation of many devices and during the technological processes necessary for their operation. There are many such devices: a nuclear reactor, an accelerator, a bomb, an X-ray machine, a gamma flaw detector and many others. And the processes are diverse - this includes research, and the development of materials and structures, and testing, and waste disposal, and accidents, and the elimination of their consequences. Different types of radiation have different penetrating powers. The range of $\alpha$ -particles in the air does not exceed several centimeters; $\beta$ - particles can travel several meters in air, and millimeters and centimeters in condensed matter; $\gamma$ -quanta and neutrons – tens and hundreds of meters in the air (they can only be detained by a relatively thick layer of metal or, for example, a thicker layer of concrete). There are many processes of interaction between particles and matter; for each type of radiation and depending on the energy they are combined differently, and in addition they differ for different isotopes. As already said, $\alpha$ -radiation is absorbed by micron layers of matter, $\beta$ -radiation has greater penetrating power, and the rest - even greater. Therefore, if it is necessary to measure $\alpha$ -radiation, a dilemma arises - either the device’s sensor must be “open”, not separated from the radiation source in any way, or the “wall” must be micron thick and made of materials with low specific density that transmit radiation better. The first option is semiconductor sensors with a “bare” p–n junction. Usually the p–n junction is hidden in the thickness of the transistor; it is probably very unusual for it to stick out openly and wait for the $\alpha$ particle. The second option is a Geiger counter, but here you need a “window”, because there is no atmosphere inside at all. We need a low-density material from which we can make a thin, thin film that can withstand atmospheric pressure and is vacuum-dense.
