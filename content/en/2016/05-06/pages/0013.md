Let's talk about mechanics

We just have to discuss some mechanical problems. It would seem that what the problem is - a bulletproof vest gives a man confidence that he is a real man. But we are interested in materials that respond intelligently to load and deformation. In other words, they allow you to create a coating that is soft and does not interfere with graceful movement until the moment of impact, becoming hard only when necessary. First of all, we note that in technology there are examples of systems that, if the integrity of the shell is damaged, repair the damage themselves. These are car tires, which themselves “heal” when the tire is punctured, aircraft fuel tanks (Sikorsky, 1914), in 2009 it was reported about the development of similar coatings for spaceships (the idea itself was contained in the works of the Strugatskys fifty years earlier). All of them are based on the fact that something enters the puncture from within and seals it. Therefore, all these are not exactly materials, they are partly “devices”. In addition, “self-healing” materials and coatings are being developed - restoring microdamages:

What about specifically the mechanical properties that change upon impact and at the location of impact? Are materials possible that can provide the aggressor with a so-called “hard block”? Impact - from the point of view of physics - is large and rapidly increasing stress. Accordingly, two classes of materials may be effective: those that harden under load and those that harden when attempting to deform quickly. As for the latter, thixotropic media that “liquefy” upon impact are well known. This is exactly what swamps are dangerous for. However, the opposite phenomenon is also known, which is naturally called antithixotropy. It would seem that it solves the problem, but this phenomenon is observed very rarely and so far only in liquids. The situation is approximately the same with liquids, the viscosity of which increases with increasing mechanical stress. This phenomenon is called dalatansion, and it is characteristic of suspensions and melts of some polymers. It is possible that the liquid in such materials is located between solid particles, and - like a lubricant - reduces friction between them. At significant shear rates, a disruption of the structure occurs, as if squeezing out the lubricant, and an increase in friction. But again, these are only liquids for now. Although body armor with cells filled with a viscous liquid is possible, development of such materials is ongoing. But the main task - creating anti-wipe pants and shirts that protect from aggressors - has not yet been solved by materials science. And it seems that a solution is far away.
