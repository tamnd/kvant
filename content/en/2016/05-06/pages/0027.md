Exercise 10. Let x and y denote the oriented distances to two intersecting lines. Prove that any linear equation defines a line, and a linear inequality defines a half-plane.

Let us now go out again into three-dimensional space. Let's consider a dihedral angle and for a point lying inside it, we will use x and y to denote the distances to the planes of its faces. Then the equality y = x defines a bisector plane. For a trihedral angle, three bisector planes can be drawn between pairs of faces, which will divide it into six regions (trihedral angles), each of which is specified by its own ordering of the variables x, y, z, where x, y, z are the distances from the point lying inside the trihedral angle to the planes of its faces.

Exercise 11. Prove that bisector planes divide a trihedral angle into exactly six regions.

Let's take a trihedral angle whose plane angles are all equal to $90^\circ$, and consider the Cartesian coordinate system associated with it (so that the trihedral angle coincides with the first octant). Then the bisector planes of dihedral angles are given by the equations x = y, y = z, z = x. They divide our trihedral angle (the first octant) into six regions (trihedral angles), which are given in coordinates by the familiar inequalities x < y < z, x < z < y, y < x < z, y < z < < x, z < x < y, z < y < x. Due to the equality of variables, these areas are equal figures. Let us cut off a unit cube from the first octant (which is given in coordinates by the inequalities $0 \le x \le 1, 0 \le y \le 1, 0 \le z \le 1$. The planes x = y, y = z, z = x divide our cube into six equal figures (equal - again due to the equality of variables). These figures are equal tetrahedrons. So, we have obtained a beautiful division of the cube into six equal tetrahedrons. If you look at the cube in the direction of its large diagonals, then it will look like in figure 11, the planes x = y, y = z, z = x are shown with a red dotted line.

Distance to arbitrary sets

In the first part of the article we dealt with distances to points, in the second part - with distances to lines and planes. A more general statement: let X, Y, ... be arbitrary sets, and x, y, ... be the distances from an arbitrary point to the sets X, Y, ..., respectively. Here, by the distance from the point M to the set X we mean $минимум^{2}$ from the distances MN, where $N \in X$. If X and Y are not very exotic figures on the plane, then as a set of solutions to the equation y = x we ​​will, as a rule, obtain some curve or several curves, and the solutions to the inequalities y > x and y < x are the regions into which the curves divide the plane (Fig.12). The sets y > x and y < x can be called generalized Voronoi regions, or, following the above-mentioned article by N. Dolbilin, “areas of influence” of the sets X and Y. A remarkable interpretation is given by A. Savin (see Fig. 12 “Kaleidoscope «Kvant» No. 4 for 1994): let X and Y be the territories of two countries between which the entire land area is distributed (thus, everything that does not belong to either X or Y is the sea), and the maritime boundary between countries X and Y is required to be fairly drawn.

2 It is better to say “exact infimum” rather than “minimum,” since the minimum is not always achieved, for example, if X is a circle or a polygon without a boundary.
