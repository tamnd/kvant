There is a lot of talk about photochromic glasses on the Internet, but before reading what they write on company websites, it is useful to brush up on your school physics course. To create photochromic glasses, very specific and few photochemical reactions are used. But it is possible, if not limited to glasses, that other photochemical reactions could be used to control optical properties. Textile workers have done a lot in this direction. In particular, they created fabrics that reversibly change color in light. Here is what G.E. Krichevsky writes about this (http:/www.nanonewsnet.ru/articles/2011/umnye-krasiteli-pigmenty-ikh-primenenie-po-novomu-naznacheniyu-khromiya-sposobnost-obr): “These are mainly spiropyrans and their derivatives, spirooxazines, diarylethanes, triarylmethane dyes, stylenes, quinones. Let us give an example of photoinitiated photochromic transformations of spiropyran, as the most studied photochrome. The photochromism of spiropyrans and their derivatives is based on reversible reactions: the breaking of covalent bonds in the molecule under the influence of ultraviolet radiation and their restoration under the influence of rays of quanta of the visible part of the spectrum or due to heating. figure 〈2〉 shows a diagram of the photochromic transformations of spiropyrans and their derivatives. As can be seen, the original form of spiropyran does not have a conjugated system of double bonds and, accordingly, these compounds are colorless. Photoexcitation initiates the cleavage of the weak spiro-(C-O) bond, as a result of which the new two forms (cisi trans-) cyanine derivatives acquire a conjugated system of double bonds and, accordingly, color.” The second, hypothetical and fantastic example is electronic light-controlled paper. We know what it is from the e-book. It contains white and black balls charged with opposite charges, and the electric field in the desired direction moves particles of the desired color to the surface. The only “trifle” left is to do it in an appropriate way so that when light hits the coating, its material becomes electrified as a result of photoemission so that an electric field arises sufficient for the correct orientation of the balls. The third, also hypothetical, but less fantastic, option is through heat. There are so-called thermochromic paints that change their color when heated, reversibly or irreversibly. If this is a change, for example, from black to white, then the paint will protect. The minimum control power and response time depend on the thickness of the paint layer, which intercepts most of the radiation. For conventional paints this value is about 0.03 mm. A layer of this thickness will require about 100 J $м^{2}$ to be heated by $10 \infty C$ in order to reliably change color, i.e. ordinary sunlight during the day under a cloudless sky will work in 0.1 s - a value that is of practical interest. However, in this case, the paint must be applied to a heat-insulating base, otherwise the absorbed heat will partially flow into the depths of the material and the reaction time will be longer. It is impossible to simply cover an object with reflective paint because in some cases, until the power increases, we do not need to reflect it at all.
