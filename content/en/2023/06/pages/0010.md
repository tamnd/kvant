Kurchatov arrived in Kazan already in a typhus fever and spent a long time in a hospital bed - first a rash, then pneumonia, then problems with the heart. All these months he did not shave, and he developed a wonderful “shovel” beard, which then had an intense black color. From that time on, Kurchatov’s absentee “name” became Beard. That’s what everyone subsequently called him, respectfully and at the same time lovingly, from ordinary employees to marshals and people’s commissars. During his illness, he had to learn sad news. His father died in besieged Leningrad, and his mother, evacuated along the Ice Road, died of a heart attack in Vologda. The recovering Kurchatov joins military missions, supervising work on the creation of a new type of armor (layered, or, as they began to call it for secrecy, “constructive”). Both the navy and military aviation are demanding it. But Ioffe does not let him leave Kazan, and Kurchatov already understands the reason for this. In the institute’s business trip log on January 9, 1943, a laconic entry appears: “The head of the laboratory I.V. Kurchatov and corresponding member of the USSR Academy of Sciences A.I. Alikhanov are supposed to be on a business trip.” The duration of the trip, its purpose and place are not specified. The new life of Igor Vasilyevich Kurchatov began. Let us quote Y. Golovanov, the author of remarkable biographies of scientists: “How could he understand then that he would become a scientist of a completely new type, character and work practice? Vedki received detailed materials about the development of atomic (nuclear) work in the USA and Great Britain. These materials fell on the table of the head of state security of the country, L.P. Beria. At first he did not attach any importance to them, but nevertheless reported them to I.V. Stalin, the chairman of the State Defense Committee (GOKO), the highest government body during the war). At the same time, Stalin received another piece of news not related to Beria’s department. Kurchatov's employee, Georgy Nikolaevich Flerov, was mobilized at the beginning of the war to take courses at the Air Force Academy and at the front became an engineer in an aviation regiment for urgent repairs of damaged aircraft. One of the discoverers of the phenomenon of spontaneous fission of uranium, he felt that the uranium problem should acquire great importance, and wrote about this to his colleagues and friends. In 1942, the regiment was recalled to reorganize in Voronezh, which had not yet been captured by the Germans. And there, first of all, Flerov went to the university and began to look through scientific journals of recent years, available in the university library. And he was amazed that since the autumn of 1940, there had not been a single scientific article on nuclear physics in English or American scientific journals. Flerov understands that nuclear work could not stop. This means that the point is different - nuclear physics has acquired military significance. Considering that the nuclear fission reaction of uranium was discovered by German radiochemists, it became clear to Flerov that American and English physicists had agreed not to publish articles about their work, in which they had probably achieved some success, in order to hide them from German scientists. And he begins to write letters about the need to expand work on nuclear physics and uranium. He writes not only to his colleagues, but also to the Presidium of the Academy of Sciences, to the government and, finally, to the State Defense Committee.
