KURCHATOV AND THE SOVIET ATOMIC PROJECT

Chief Scientific Director, Chief Designer and Chief Theorist. The monument to I.V. Kurchatov, Yu.B. Khariton and Ya.B. Zeldovich, tearing apart the atom of the Evo variant (RDS-1), was based on data received by intelligence from the English physicist Klaus Fuchs, who worked for several years at the American nuclear center in Los Alamos, and from several other American physicists. Another option, uranium (RDS-2), was entirely developed at KB-11. Both options were ready by the fall of 1949, but Stalin decided to be the first to test the American option, so as not to take unnecessary risks. Yes, the RDS-1 product was similar to the bomb that destroyed Hiroshima on August 6, 1945. But only the principle and general scheme of its design were known. The main secret that such a bomb could be made ceased to be a secret in 1945. But hundreds of secrets of individual structural units required enormous labor to perform theoretical calculations, engineering projects, create mock-ups and test them. Here's just one example. It was known that the initiation of the explosion should be carried out using a converging spherical shock wave from the explosion of a chemical explosive surrounding the plutonium ball. Plutonium initially has a subcritical density. This means that individual randomly appearing neutrons cannot start an avalanche (“chain”) process.

plutonium nuclei melt and fly out of the core without accomplishing anything. To ensure the start and development of a chain reaction, plutonium must be highly compressed in order to increase the likelihood of neutrons meeting atomic nuclei. The term “critical mass” has found its way into popular literature. It would be more correct to talk about critical density. Plutonium is compressed to a critical density by exploding a spherical layer of explosives. But to ensure uniform compression of the plutonium charge, the shock wave must have an ideal spherical shape. How to create an explosion to make this happen? After all, no one has ever exploded such charges. From Fuchs's messages it was known that for this it was necessary to detonate simultaneously at 32 points of the sphere. Not knowing how this was calculated, they decided to take 32 separate explosive prisms, placing them closely in a spherical layer, and at the same time carry out an explosion using electric detonators. But for a very long time nothing worked. The wave did not turn out to be spherical and instead of a uniformly compressed mock-up aluminum ball, a wide variety of shapes were obtained, very interesting from the point of view of abstract art, but completely unsuitable for a bomb. There were explosions at the training ground in KB-11; several hundred of them had already been made. Kurchatov received daily information about the results of this work, or rather about the absence of the necessary results. The head of this direction, I.A. Terletsky, a talented engineer from the military department, came up with and calculated an original system of 12 pentagonal and 20 hexagonal prisms that make up a common spherical layer. And the “anniversary” thousandth explosion showed that the shock wave finally received a spherical shape and the model of the plutonium charge after explosive compression was a perfect ball. The highest chic of the participants in these works was to light a cigarette while it was still hot after crimping the model. And this is just one episode from the work of one of 10 KB-11 laboratories.
