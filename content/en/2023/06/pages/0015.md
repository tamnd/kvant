And when the question arose about the uranium bomb dropped on Nagasaki on August 8, 1945, there was no longer any intelligence data about it. And much of it was already different, not similar to a plutonium bomb, not to mention the problem of obtaining the ultra-pure uranium-235 isotope. The test of a uranium bomb, the RDS-2 product, took place at the Semipalatinsk test site on September 24, 1951. And this was the first time in our country that a nuclear bomb was dropped from an airplane (the then newly created TU bomber); the finished uranium bomb had been waiting for its carrier for almost two years. So what is the significance of information obtained by intelligence in the creation of Soviet nuclear weapons? Certainly some time was saved. But there is no way to plausibly estimate this time. And it seems that the main help should be considered not the individual details themselves, but reports about the enormous difficulties that arose, both scientific and technical, and organizational and governmental, which had to be overcome to solve the problem. And the fact that these difficulties were overcome inspired Kurchatov. And his ardent faith in the success of the business infected those around him. Among the nuclear physicists, only Kurchatov could get acquainted with Fuchs’s materials. And he had to, without proving this to anyone, make a unilateral decision on the accelerated construction of nuclear reactors (experimental and industrial) to produce plutonium, and it was necessary to create a new branch of chemical and metallurgical technologies. We can safely say that success in this was achieved only thanks to the will and perseverance of Kurchatov, thanks to his ability to attract the most suitable energetic and knowledgeable scientists to the leadership of each direction, as well as thanks to the talent and erudition of Kurchatov himself, not only in physics, but also in many areas of chemistry and engineering. I.V. Kurchatov and A.D. Sakharov’s workplace moved to construction sites, where he delved into all the details of design, construction and installation. Not to mention the testing of “products”, for which he was always the chief scientific leader. And during his lifetime there were more than 50 of them. Involving a group from the Physical Institute of the USSR Academy of Sciences (I.E. Tamm and A.D. Sakharov) in the design of the hydrogen bomb, Kurchatov already had in mind the future use of the thermonuclear fusion reaction to produce electrical energy, and not just for an explosion. He organized the first research in this direction at the Institute of Atomic Energy (the Ogra installation) and began work on creating the Tokamak. These works were strictly classified in the 50s. But Kurchatov objected to this secrecy and obtained permission in 1956 to make a report at Harwell (English nuclear center) on the achievements of Soviet scientists in this direction. This report became a major event not only in the scientific world. It turned out that research in the USSR was far ahead of the work of English and American scientists. Therefore, the international political aspect of this declassification was also important. At the end of the 50s, Kurchatov understood better than anyone that the main political task had been completed, that the creation of an arsenal of nuclear weapons in the USSR was a reliable guarantee that the cold war would not develop into a hot war.
