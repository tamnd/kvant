It is generally accepted that gold, platinum and their alloys with a high content of precious metal do not oxidize (or oxidize so weakly that electrons tunnel through the film). Additives to precious metals are used either to increase strength and reduce mechanical wear, or to increase manufacturability (Fig. 6).

Two contacts, compressed for some time, can weld together and remain in this position after the force is removed. This phenomenon is called “adhesion”, it is explained by the fluidity and diffusion of materials and, as a result, the formation of a “true contact” of a large area. The process accelerates when heated by flowing current. Adhesion is possible only for pure metals that are not coated with a layer of oxide or other film. In the presence of films, the adhesion effect is weakened or stopped. Separation of contacts made of absolutely pure annealed noble metals, such as platinum or gold, is only possible after applying great forces, almost equal to the forces required to destroy the monolithic structure of the metal. Therefore, to reduce welding, additives are used that increase strength and inhibit diffusion.

AT

CONTACT

⟦column⟧

For medium-power contacts, pressing can usually be achieved, and the resistance of the films is no longer so significant - they will be destroyed upon contact. Moreover, relays and switches often make the contacts “jump” towards each other, hit each other or slide against each other, destroying the films. Then the electrical and thermal conductivity of the material itself turns out to be important - this means that copper or silver will have an advantage, perhaps with strengthening additives. Welding may also be important, which means the material must be refractory and have a low vapor pressure. This requirement indicates carbon, tungsten, molybdenum. But they have great resistance. The output is a composite: tungsten sponge impregnated with copper, or a pressed and sintered mixture of copper and carbon powders. A specific situation is sliding contacts with large and inevitable wear, if there is work to be done outdoors with the ensuing consequences in the form of rain or snow. Therefore, you need to take either something very cheap and replace it often, or something with little friction and wear. This is the case of a trolleybus with contacts for wet weather - made of steel or cast iron, the cheapest metal material, wear-resistant and anti-friction, and for dry weather - made of carbon materials (Fig. 7). Or is it a tram with contacts made of aluminum or steel.
