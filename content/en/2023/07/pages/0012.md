Over the last quarter of a century, it has been established that in spaces of dimensions d 3, 8 and 24, the densest lattice packings are absolutely the densest packings, i.e. the altyn that the gentleman from B.N. Delaunay’s favorite joke was looking for. It is very likely that the packing $D_{4}$ is also an optimal packing in 4-dimensional space. Moreover, it seems that the packing $D_{4}$ has no less reason to claim this place of honor than its “younger sister” – the fcc-packing $D_{3}$: the centering of the hypercube is very successful. However, strange as it may seem, modern methods that solved the problem for d 8 and 24 do not yet work in the d 4 dimension. As for the densest lattice packings, in spaces of dimension starting from 9, the method of rolling layers not only becomes incredibly complex, but also stops working altogether. The reason is that even in dense lattice packings of balls, holes appear so large and, therefore, deep that balls from the adjacent layer above fall into these holes and collide with balls from the adjacent layer below. Moreover, a ball of an adjacent layer placed in it may be flush with the first layer, i.e. a dense lattice turns out to be an unsaturated packing. At the same time, adding new balls to a lattice package makes it not lattice, but multi-lattice. These circumstances indicate that at d t 9 the “altyn” is most likely “not under the lantern.” This assumption is also supported by the fact that in 10-dimensional space, as has already been established, there are multilattice packings that are denser than the densest lattice packing known today. However, whether the “known densest” 10-dimensional packing is really the densest lattice packing - there is no answer to this question yet.

So not only finding a 9-dimensional altyn, but even establishing that it is not under the lantern is a very difficult problem. Moreover, it is unknown whether the closest packing of balls in a multidimensional space is multilattice or not. Multidimensional packaging conceals many things that contradict our geometric intuition. For example, in multidimensional spaces, even the densest packings of balls occupy an insignificant fraction of space. Thus, the famous 24-dimensional Leach lattice represents in 24-dimensional space the absolutely densest packing of balls, the share of which is only about 0.19% of the space! It is difficult, but also interesting, to imagine a dense package into which not a single ball can be inserted, despite the fact that more than 99.8% of the volume of the space remains not covered with balls. Of course, questions about the closest packings of balls in multidimensional spaces are of interest to mathematicians. Why are they interesting to other representatives of the three-dimensional world? Answer: in our time of information technology – to many! Let us recall that at the end of the 19th century, Minkowski reduced a problem from number theory to the problem of the densest packing of balls. And in the 20th century, thanks to another outstanding scientist, the founder of modern information theory, Claude Shannon, the very important problem of choosing a system of codes that correct errors that arise when transmitting information over a communication channel was also reduced to the problem of the densest packing of balls in a space of arbitrary dimension.
