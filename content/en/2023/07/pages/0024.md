In essence, a person of average ability can obtain a mathematical education in five years. But, apparently, for this it is necessary to somehow radically change the style of teaching. You write in your letter: “We should not forget what the level of preparation of the bulk of first-year students is and to what extent they could immediately begin to work at one or another level of activity and independence.” I don’t forget this, but I only understand the following. a) “The bulk” (the conventional name for students who came from schools with a conventional level of teaching mathematics - soon they will no longer form the majority, but you will still need to think about them!) are not capable of a “different level of activity and independence” in mastering the material that we offer them in the first year. b) This majority reaches the fifth year without ever rising to a different level of activity and independence. <...> You also know my opinion about ways to change this situation: do in the first year what is required so that the knowledge and skills brought from school move into an “active” state. Meanwhile, the opposite is happening now. Out of a desire not to lose authority among the stronger part of the students, first-year lecturers intensively raise the level of presentation in a modern way. It seems to me that we are no longer facing the prospect, but the fact of the existence of “two kinds” of students in the most harmful form - all students understand that a division exists, many teachers, through their activities, aggravate this division, rather than soften it, and at the same time, formally, everyone must undergo the same program at the same time. Since the presence of schools with advanced training in mathematics is a fact (and

not created at all with the advent of the boarding school I led), then the only reasonable solution, in my opinion, is to create separate streams that, at different times and according to different programs, receive the minimum of general mathematical knowledge that the faculty recognizes as mandatory. Specifically, a stream of graduates from special schools could take an analysis course in two years, which includes organically (and not as a superstructure) the content of “Analysis-III”, from the very first semester have a “combined” course of linear algebra and geometry, etc. They would start working in special seminars and specializing in departments earlier and would “gain” a free year at the end (and those who wish would finish at four years). (At the same time, from the third year they would completely merge with the “main mass” of students.) I agree with you that the amount of knowledge required for all our graduates should be established in some kind of workable commission. If there is such a “minimum”, it seems to me that the department will not do anything frivolous, allowing certain groups of mathematicians to try to teach individual experimental streams all of mathematics for two to two and a half years (of course, under control, but not too pedantic control - one should still strive less to “uniformize” all the work at the department). It seems to me extremely important that all the required material included in the university teaching program in the first two years is presented systematically and accessible in a small number of educational books, preferably in one book for each subject (analytic geometry, analysis, differential equations, etc.).
