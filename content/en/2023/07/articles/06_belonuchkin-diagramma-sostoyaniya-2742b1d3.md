This means that the liquid-gas (L-G) boundary goes from the left - from bottom to right - to top. 2) The melting point weakly depends on pressure, so the solid-liquid (S-L) boundary runs almost vertically. 3) There is a special point - critical $T_{кр}, p_{кр}$. As you approach it along the liquid-g boundary, the properties of the liquid and gas (primarily the densities) differ less and less; at the critical point the differences disappear. The G–G boundary ends at this point. 4) For all substances (with the exception of helium) there is a triple point - a set of parameters $T_{тр}, p_{тр}$ at which all three phases (liquid, solid and gaseous) coexist. And since at low temperatures, far from the critical $( T^{=} T_{кр} ),$, the difference between the liquid and solid states is much smaller than between them and the gas, the behavior of the T–G boundary is similar to the behavior of the L–G boundary. The usual option is 1 – the melting temperature increases with increasing pressure. Qualitatively, the dependence of the phase transition temperature (as the transition of a substance from one state - solid liquid or gaseous - to another) on pressure can be understood based on one of the most general laws of nature - the Le Chatelier-Brown principle. Somewhat simplified, it can be formulated as follows: Any external influence causes changes in the system that reduce the effect of this influence. So, if saturated vapors are in equilibrium with a liquid, then as the volume occupied by the system decreases, the vapor pressure will begin to increase. The density of liquid is greater than the density of vapor. If some of the vapor condenses, the density, and therefore the pressure of the remaining vapor, will decrease. The effect of external influence is partially (and if the temperature is maintained constant, then completely) compensated. Consequently, this compensating process - vapor condensation - will begin immediately. To prevent condensation, i.e. In order to still increase the vapor pressure, obviously, it is necessary to increase the temperature of the system. Thus, relying (except on the Le Chatelier-Brown principle) only on the fact that the density of the liquid is greater than the density of the vapor, we came to the conclusion that a higher temperature corresponds to a higher saturated vapor pressure.
