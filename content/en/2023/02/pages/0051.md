speech. Let's start with the concepts of “interpolation” and “extrapolation”. You will probably have to meet them in school and work, so why not now? So, you do some experiment and get, among other things, our famous four - $x_{1}, y_{1}, x_{2}, y_{2}$. You don’t have any other data and you can’t get it, but you needed the value y for some x, and such that $x_{1} x x_{2}$. So, finding y is called interpolation. It is clear that you cannot find it, but everyone does it somehow. The simplest option is to draw a straight line from point $x_{1}, y_{1}$ to point $x_{2}, y_{2}$ and... you get the idea. The method is called linear interpolation. There are other options - for example, you can interpolate with a piece of a polynomial, specifying not only the points themselves, but also the derivatives at these points. From the point of view of physics, it is important for us to know at least something about the entire function y x - well, say, about the maximum value of its derivative, roughly speaking, how smooth it is. So here, too, more general knowledge is needed, a deeper understanding of the processes. Well, in conclusion, let’s turn to where we started – to the points themselves. Do you need many of them? How many times should measurements be taken? There is no general and reliable answer to this question. It is sometimes said that any quantity must be measured three times. In fact, this is not so, the required number of measurements depends on what we are measuring and what we already know about it, why we are measuring, what accuracy and reliability we need, as well as on the cost and complexity of the measurements. For example, if you are checking a battery removed from a flashlight that has become poorly lit, and the device shows a voltage noticeably less than normal, you are unlikely to repeat the measurement and certainly will not take three measurements. But if it shows normal, you will be surprised and will probably check it (and then check the voltage when operating under load - think about why). Repeated measurements are made if random errors are noticeable and you want to increase the accuracy by averaging. But in this case, it may be more useful to think about where they came from and whether there are systematic errors lying next to them, from which averaging will not save. But understanding what, how and why you measure can save you. So happy measuring, reliable interpolations, careful extrapolations and meaningful drawing of curves through points!
