However, for two points you will get out by writing the family of lines in some form, which will tell you your own figure. But if there are several points, at least three, this trick will not work. This is already a topic for a little research and, perhaps, writing a computer program. Therefore, let’s stop, take a breath and look at the question from a slightly different, more fundamental side. Why did we even undertake to conduct a live broadcast? In a learning task - because the task is formulated that way. What about in real life? In real life, a physicist, seeing how someone draws something (no matter what) through two points, will smile and advise getting at least a dozen more, or better yet, several dozen points. But the experiment can be complicated and expensive and the amount of experimental data is somehow limited, and the main thing is, what curve to draw? The most natural situation is when there is a theory and you need to test it, or there are several theories and you need to choose the one that best agrees with experiment. Everything is clear here - we draw the curve that the theory predicts, or those curves that are predicted by different theories, and see whether they agree with experiment and which one agrees better. A common situation is when in theory there is some parameter whose value we do not know. But having experimental data, we can select such a value for this parameter that the points will fall on

But what if there is still no theory to which the points fit well and the data are reliable? Although we are talking about science, there are situations when we, strictly speaking, do not need a theory, we only need a simple and compact way to store data. We'll talk about data processing a little later, but there is a simple, but not very physical way to store and use it. We take any function (as a rule, we take a polynomial) and select the coefficients so that the curve passes through our experimental points in the entire studied range, or more precisely, through the error fields that are shown near these points. But what if we are interested in the physical meaning? It happens that the theory is simplified and approximate, but at least there is some kind of theory. Then watch how our points deviate from this primitive theory. For example, it may turn out that agreement is good for small values ​​of the argument and bad for large values, or vice versa, or that discrepancies arise precisely in some narrow area. All this may be important for refining the theory. It is even more interesting if the deviations themselves depend on some parameter, for example, time. What do you say if deviations from Ohm's law occur at high currents and increase over time? Of course, there is a direct road into the arms of Joule and Lenz. Sometimes you can say that this problem is similar to such and such or that this process is similar to such and such, and then, probably, the solution will be a combination of exponentials, or sinusoids, or... But such reasoning requires experience - both generally accumulated in physics, and available to someone who looks at the points and thinks about what to do with them. In addition, experience can fail, anything can happen.
