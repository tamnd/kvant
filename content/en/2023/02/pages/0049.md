True, there is nothing infinite in physics, not even the number of electrons in the Universe, but let’s not talk about terrible things. Here’s a funny question: is it always possible to accurately determine a quantity that can take an infinite but countable set of values ​​as integers? It turns out not. Let a certain value take the values ​​0 (zero) and all values ​​1 n, where n is an integer. This quantity has an infinite number of values ​​that it can accurately take, but there is one that it definitely cannot! You probably already guessed which one, understood why, and can prove it. Let's move on, however, to more physical situations. In school physics, and in metrology in general, when it comes to accuracy, they write, for example, that the voltage is 10 r 0.1 V. This means that a certain value lies between 9.9 V and 10.1 V, and is equally likely anywhere in this segment, and cannot lie outside it. In real life this is not the case. Random errors usually obey the so-called normal distribution - the closer to the edges of the segment indicated in a particular case, the rarer they are; outside it, they are naturally even rarer, but do not disappear at all. Nevertheless, the error limits in many cases (and in technology and engineering almost always) are indicated exactly this way - for simplicity. Accordingly, this is done in exam tasks.
