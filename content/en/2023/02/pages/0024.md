Epicycles could not provide a significant change in the distance from the Earth, since the radius of the deferent is much larger than the radius of the epicycle. Then Copernicus considered the heliocentric version of the system, when the celestial spheres revolve around the Sun (Fig. 4). In this model, the distance between Earth and Mars will change. The case of the closest location is called opposition, and the case of the farthest is called conjunction. At the same time, the change in the brightness of the planets during conjunction and opposition received an explanation - the distance to the Earth is different at these moments (Fig. 5). The need for epicycles disappeared, and the calculations were significantly simplified. By the way, a planet in a conjunction cannot be observed in its pure form due to the fact that it occurs during daylight hours; in fact, you can observe the position of the planet not far from the conjunction at sunrise or sunset. Copernicus's fundamental work was called "On the Revolution of the Celestial Spheres." In fact, the usual transfer of the origin of the coordinate system made it possible to greatly simplify the calculations of the positions of the luminaries. But the church carefully guarded its “only true” understanding of the world. She allowed calculating the positions of the stars using the Copernican method, but no more, the Earth was still at the center of the Universe.
