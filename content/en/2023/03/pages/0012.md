The discovery of the 118th element summed up the filling of the periodic table with new elements. Lawrence in 1947 named the elements after actinium (No. 89) actinides, by analogy with the lanthanides (elements No. 57 to No. 72). In 14 lanthanides, with a constant outer shell of two 5s electrons, the inner 4f subshell is gradually filled with electrons. In actinides, with a constant outer shell of two 7s electrons, the inner 5f subshell is filled with fourteen electrons. The last element for which it is filled is the 102nd element - nobelium. Lawrence predicted that the outer shell of element 103, consisting of two electrons, would remain unchanged, and the inner shell 6d would begin to fill. And so it turned out - this was confirmed by a study of the chemical properties of the 103rd element. Is that why it was called lawrencium? There can be no more than 10 electrons in the d-subshell, and 10 elements, starting with lawrencium, are now commonly called superactinides (this word was also coined by Lawrence). The last of these is element 112, copernicium. Therefore, it was very important to know how the 113th atomic electron would behave. It turned out that it was more profitable for him to become in the 7p state of the outer seventh shell than in the states of the 5g or 6f inner shells. A study of the chemical properties of the 113th element (nihonium), carried out in Dubna, reliably showed this. The 7p subshell became completely filled in oganesson, the 118th element. Today, elements No. 103 to No. 118 are called superactinoids. What's next? Glenn Seaborg, who continued Lawrence's work, called the subsequent elements superactinoids. Physicists are concerned about theoretical predictions of the special stability of some superactinides, which have been called the “island of stability.” But for 7 years now there has been no information about the synthesis of new elements. The rumored candidates for seats No. 119 and No. 120 have too short a lifespan for reliable conclusions to be drawn. In Dubna, they are waiting for the full commissioning of a new powerful accelerator - the ion collider. Similar problems exist in other world centers for the synthesis of transuranium elements. Is it necessary to continue work on the synthesis of transuraniums? Maybe we should stop this super-expensive research and limit ourselves to the 118 elements of the periodic table? There are several answers to this question. First of all, these studies develop the entire nuclear physics. Today, a quark-gluon model of the atomic nucleus is being created. It should replace the shell model on which all the syntheses made were based. In the coming years, the scientific community of nuclear physicists is waiting for the discovery of new elements that will have long-lived isotopes. Then it will become clear whether nuclear theorists are on the right track. But is the price for a new version of nuclear theory too high? After all, all nuclear physics experiments are very expensive. The construction of giant accelerators, transuranium fusions, and the problem of radioactive waste disposal are multi-billion dollar costs. However, an analogy with the exploration of near space and astrophysical research of the Universe suggests itself. This is also very expensive. But it is impossible to stop the development of work both in space research and in nuclear physics. In addition to issues of national-state prestige, this is necessary to satisfy the thirst to learn new things about the world in which we live.
