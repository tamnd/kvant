On the other hand, for any arrangement of disjoint circles on a plane, we can draw a vertex inside each circle, another vertex - the root - outside, connect with an edge each pair of vertices separated from each other by only one circle, and get a tree with a root. If we consider as equivalent the arrangements of circles that are obtained from each other by their movement and change in size, but without intersections, then a one-to-one correspondence is established between the arrangement of n circles on a plane (or on a disk) and a tree rooted at n 1 vertices. What are trees without roots? You can draw a tree and circles on a plane in the same way as in the case of a tree with a root, assigning any vertex as a root. In this construction, as in the case of a rooted tree, two vertices are connected by an edge if and only if their corresponding regions are separated by a single circle. In the case of a tree with a root, there is always an area outside corresponding to the vertex that is closer to the root, but in the case of a tree without a root there should be no difference. There is an operation that transforms a plane divided by circles into regions, just so that the set of regions is preserved and the relationship between them is “bordered through the circle”, but can make any region external. This operation is the inversion of a plane relative to a circle. Thus, each unrooted tree with n 1 vertices corresponds to a configuration of n disjoint circles on the plane up to inversions, displacements and changes in size (without the occurrence of intersections). figure 5a shows the effect of one such inversion. The only important thing is that one or more circles are “turned inside out.” Or we can supplement the plane with an infinitely distant point (strictly speaking, this is necessary if we consider all possible inversions) and, through a stereographic projection, collapse the plane into a sphere, which will completely eliminate the difference between the inside and outside of each circle (Fig. 5, b). Accordingly, the sequences A000055 and A000081 count not only unrooted and rooted trees, but also configurations of circles on the plane and on the sphere, respectively. But only non-overlapping ones. Configurations of intersecting circles on a plane are a much more complex story, where we only have computer search at our disposal; they are counted by the sequence A250001.
