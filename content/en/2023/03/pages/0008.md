Ernest Orlando Lawrence built the laboratory and remained in this post until the end of his life (until 1958). In August 1958, the laboratory began to bear the name Lawrence, abbreviated as LBL, and received the status of a US National Laboratory. The Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory concentrates breakthrough work in physics, astronomy, chemistry and biology, one way or another related to atomic and nuclear physics. Among the laboratory employees (as of 2022) there are 16 Nobel laureates. The laboratory’s motto is “Bringing scientific solutions to the world.” In the creation of the atomic bomb, work quickly came to the fore to obtain an ultra-pure isotope of uranium-235, capable of an effective fission reaction and the production of a second fissile element - transuranium, as Lawrence called it. Deuterons were accelerated at the accelerator, and the target was the nuclei of the uranium-238 isotope. In a nuclear reaction

$238 2 238 1_{92} U o_{1} H o_{93} Np 2_{0} n$

the result is the nucleus of an atom of a new element with the number of protons 93. Its name is “neptunium”

Transuranium element No. 94 was of main interest for the creation of an atomic bomb. Lawrence subsequently named it plutonium, completing the "planetary" series of names. He, however, could not know that soon astronomers would deprive Pluto of the title of planet and transfer it to the group of dwarf planets. It quickly became clear that 3 out of 20 isotopes of plutonium have the ability to undergo fission when hit by a neutron, releasing a large amount of energy. One of them, plutonium-239, turned out to be the most suitable for use in a bomb. It is now called “weapons-grade plutonium.” This isotope is not produced in accelerators, but in the cycle of nuclear reactions

$238 1 239 239 239 0_{92} U_{0} n o_{92} U,_{92} U o_{93} Np_{1} E$,

$239 239 0_{93} Np o_{94} Pu_{1} E$.

A large number of slow neutrons needed for the first reaction of this cycle can be obtained through the fission reaction of uranium-235. E. Fermi began to study the production of plutonium. Under his leadership, the world's first graphite nuclear reactor was built at the Metallurgical Laboratory in Chicago. Work on its creation began in December 1941, and a year later the reactor was launched and began producing the first tangible grams of plutonium. And three years later in the USSR, physicists again covered this entire path - from obtaining the first atoms of the transuranium elements neptunium and plutonium to industrial reactors that became weapons-grade plutonium factories and peaceful nuclear power plants.
