TRANSURANAN

One of the nuclei to the required energy required powerful accelerators. They were built in these two centers, in Darmstadt - linear, in Dubna - cyclic. Identifying new nuclei has become even more difficult. Their lifetime was so short that identification, such as obtaining characteristic X-ray spectra, could not keep pace with their radioactive decay. It was necessary to develop special research methods for each element. The third stage of the synthesis of transurans, from No. 113 (nihonium) to No. 118 (oganesson) is a priority of the Dubna Institute (with the participation of scientists from the US Livermore National Laboratory for two elements). Developing the shell theory of nuclei, Yu. Ts. Oganesyan calculated that the most suitable nuclei for bombardment should be calcium isotope nuclei. And so it turned out. And many theoretical predictions of the properties of new elements were confirmed, for example, the density of darmstadtium. It turned out to be the highest density for all elements, as predicted, equal to 34.8 g $см^{3}$. This element has an unusual ending for all other transuranium elements. The fact is that the oganesson atom has an outer, seventh electron shell with 8 electrons. Such outer electron shells have chemically inert atoms (“noble gases”). All of them (except helium) have the ending he. The heaviest noble gas on Earth is radon. These atoms are little susceptible to combining with other atoms - they are chemically neutral. They cannot even combine into their own molecule, as hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen do. Theory, however, predicted that the liquefaction temperature of this new substance would be approximately 350 K, and the solidification temperature even lower. At room temperature, the substance from oganesson should theoretically not be a noble gas, but a “noble” solid, oganesson ice. True, in 2007, only three (!) atoms of oganesson were synthesized and it was impossible to verify these predictions. But many years have passed, they have learned to obtain noticeable fractions of a gram of this element (by bombarding a target from California with charged particles), and the theoretical predictions are coming true. The question may arise: why is it so difficult to identify new elements and determine their physical and chemical properties? It would seem that after the discovery of an element and the conviction of its existence, it is necessary to move on to its mass production using a California target (other transuranium isotopes are not suitable due to their short lifetime).
