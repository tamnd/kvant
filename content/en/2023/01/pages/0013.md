True albedo, or Lambertian albedo, which coincides with the diffuse reflectance coefficient, is the ratio of the flux scattered by a flat surface element in all directions to the flux incident on it. If the surface is illuminated and observed vertically, then this true albedo is called normal. The normal albedo of pure snow is about 1.0, and that of charcoal is about 0.04. The albedo value depends on the spectrum of the incident radiation and on the properties of the surface. Therefore, albedo is measured separately for different spectral ranges (optical, ultraviolet, infrared), subranges (visual, photographic) and even for individual wavelengths (monochromatic albedo). In astronomy, geometric, or flat, albedo is often used - the ratio of the illumination near the Earth created by the planet in full phase to the illumination that would be created by a flat absolutely white screen of the same

In principle, the geometric albedo can be greater than unity (partially specular reflection). For example, the geometric albedo of Enceladus (a moon of Saturn) is 1.38. To calculate the energy balance of planets, the spherical albedo, or Bond albedo, introduced in 1861 by the American astronomer J. Bond (1825–1865), is used. This is the ratio of the flux of radiation reflected by the entire planet to the flux incident on it. Bond's albedo for Earth is 0.306, for the atmosphereless Moon it is 0.067, for Mars it is 0.25, for cloud-covered Venus it is 0.77, and for snow-covered Enceladus it is 0.99.
