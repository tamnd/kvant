PHYSICS + SPORTS

The history of interaction between science and sports, albeit timidly and unconsciously, dates back at least to the Olympic Games that originated in Ancient Greece. And since then, for a long time, athletes have been worried about the question: how to improve their achievements? By persistent training, developing strength and speed - so to speak, just relying on natural abilities? Or rely on the achievements of scientists, new products of inventors and engineers? Moreover, when unusual sports began to appear at first, where you can’t do without equipment and special equipment: auto, motorcycle and bicycle racing, sailing regattas, mountaineering, surfing, alpine skiing, ski jumping... And yet, what else is a person capable of? It seems that we are entering an era of merging the natural and the artificial, when new technologies can fundamentally change ideas about our capabilities, which, of course, will greatly affect sports. In the meantime, armed with the Olympic motto, let's try to find a lot of sports stories in the school physics course.

... the rules of the ancient Greek Olympic Games allowed long jumping with any heavy objects in their hands, which had to be thrown down and back during the jump. Perhaps this led to an increase in flight range, sometimes exceeding the results of current record holders. ... not a single animal can compete with a person in a marathon, since it is not capable of running 42 kilometers without resting and stopping. Our tendons, playing the role of a kind of springs, return about 90% of the potential energy to kinetic energy, which leads to high efficiency of cyclic movements. The power expended by an adult varies from 80 W at rest to 10 kW during sprinting. ... already in 1671, Isaac Newton explained the deflection of a rotating ball by the occurrence of a transverse pressure difference during its flight. In 1852, a similar interpretation was given by the German physicist Heinrich Magnus, after whom this effect is named, and its mathematical theory was presented in 1877 by Lord Rayleigh. ... in 1697, the Swiss scientist Johann Bernoulli showed that the shape of the steepest descent curve in the absence of friction, which was of interest to Galileo, is a cycloid.
