Ferenc Kraus, Anne L'Huillier, Pierre Agostini example: an attosecond is the same number of times less than a second as the second itself is less than the age of the Universe (check for yourself by taking the conditional age of the Universe to be 13.8 billion years). Physicists will tell your friend differently. It is known that in one nanosecond light travels a distance of 30 centimeters, and in one picosecond – 0.3 millimeters. Let us take the conventional size of an atom of a light element to be 0.1 nanometers. Light will travel this distance in 0.3 attoseconds. We can say that if a nanometer is a spatial characteristic of an atom, then an attosecond is its temporal characteristic. One of the most important means of “communication” with the elements of the microworld, the electrons of atoms and molecules, is light, more precisely, electromagnetic radiation. It interacts with the electron, transferring energy to it, and the electron changes its state. An atomic electron, for example, moves to a region of space more distant from the nucleus. In the language of quantum mechanics, the probability density of its location has a maximum at a greater distance from the nucleus. A visual, but too rough interpretation of the situation is that the electron moves to a more distant orbit. The electron can be completely removed from the atom (photoionization) or even leave the substance (external photoelectric effect). There are also more complex cases. All these processes, like everything in the microworld, are not obligatory, but probabilistic. And for practical purposes it is important to be able to calculate these probabilities. Using the semiclassical (Bohr) model of the atomic electron and the uncertainty relations, one can estimate the order of the wavelength of radiation that is needed to excite the electron. This wavelength is of the order of $10^{8}$ m, i.e. refers to the range of extremely hard ultraviolet or soft x-ray radiation. But it is possible to obtain a powerful “beam” with a high degree of coherence (consistency of all quanta) only with the help of infrared lasers. One should not think that the result of the experiments will be photographs of the electron. Of course not. An electron emitted from an atom or molecule enters a device that measures its energy and determines the direction of its flight. And the corresponding mathematical processing will indicate from which place in the atom it flew out when a short pulse of radiation acted on it.
