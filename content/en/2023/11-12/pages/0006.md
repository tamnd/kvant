KVANT 2023/No. 11-12

There is an analogy with the “photograph of a black hole,” which the press diligently reprinted several years ago. Of course, this was not a photograph in the usual sense of the word. This was the result of a huge number of individual observations and measurements of the radiation coming from the space around the black hole. Laser physicists more than 30 years ago began to study the problem of how to convert laser infrared radiation into ultraviolet radiation while maintaining coherence and radiation power (the number of photons incident per unit time per unit area). The task was how to cut out a small part from a “long” infrared sinusoid, which itself would already represent a very short length of the ultraviolet sinusoid and would operate for an extremely short time. Figuratively speaking, it was necessary to create a “shutter” that would turn on the light for this unimaginably short time of exposure of the electron to radiation. And about 25 years ago such laser instruments were created. A radiation time interval of several tens of femtoseconds was achieved. These instruments, in addition to the lasers themselves, contained many other optical devices (polarizers, mirrors, diffraction gratings, etc.) and were not cheap. In 1999, the Nobel Prize in Chemistry “for studies of transition states during chemical reactions using femtosecond technology” was received (the entire prize) by Ahmed Zewail, an Egyptian-American chemist, a professor at the California Technological University, who has 16 of the most prestigious awards and medals for a chemist, and an honorary doctorate from six universities (including Baku State University). Egypt considers him its national hero. Of course! Zewail is the only Nobel laureate of Egyptian origin in the scientific fields (physics, chemistry, physiology and medicine). He is the founder of femtochemistry, a branch of chemical dynamics that studies molecular processes in their real-time femtosecond time intervals.

Femtosecond laser systems have already found application in the creation of new technologies for producing nanometer masks for semiconductor lithography. A wide field of applications opens up for femtosecond technologies in medicine, for example, for the manufacture of high-quality stents in vascular surgery. Physicists initially did not find areas of application for femtosecond lasers in which the experiment would make it possible to identify any new theoretical principles for the interaction of atoms and molecules with each other. In order to “look” inside the atom, shorter attosecond radiation pulses were needed. And laser physicists continued to improve femtosecond laser devices. The transition to attosecond pulses was first made by the French physicist Anne L'Huillier, who headed a laboratory at the French nuclear research center. In 1987, she discovered an interesting phenomenon. When a powerful infrared laser beam was passed through an inert gas (argon), radiation appeared at high frequencies - a “rainbow”, consisting of sections of waves of higher frequencies, up to hard ultraviolet rays. This radiation was quite weak, and no one had noticed it before. L’Huillier was able to do this because in her experiments the gas was placed inside the thinnest tubes (fibers) of quartz glass, where the laser beam was focused. This was done in order to exclude the interaction of radiation with different atoms - to leave the interaction only with argon atoms. And argon was chosen because of the comparative simplicity and spherical symmetry of its electronic configuration $( 1s^{2} 2s^{2} 2p^{6} 3s^{2} 3p^{6} ).$ To her surprise, these waves that came from nowhere had the same intensity, starting from the 5th to the 33rd frequency.
