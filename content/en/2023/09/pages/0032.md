MATHEMATICAL CIRCLE

Paid scales

A. GRIBALKO, A. ZASLAVSKY

IN THE FALL OF 2022 AT THE TOURNAMENT OF Cities, the following task was proposed (in a slightly different wording). Problem 1. There are 8 identical coins, one of which is counterfeit. All real coins weigh the same, but the counterfeit one differs from them in weight in an unknown direction. There are cup scales, for each weighing on which you have to pay one coin. Moreover, if a real coin is paid, the scales show the correct result, and if a counterfeit coin is used, it shows a random result. Is it possible to find and not spend 5 real coins? Solution. Let the coins be assigned numbers from 1 to 8. We will pay coin 8 for the first weighing and put coins 1 and 2 on one bowl, and coins 3 and 4 on the other. If the bowls remain in balance, then these four coins are real. Indeed, if coin 8 is real, then the scales show the correct result and, therefore, the counterfeit coin cannot be among those being weighed. If coin 8 is fake, then all the other coins are real. Then, by the second weighing, we compare coins 1 and 5, paying with coin 7. If the scales are in balance, then coin 5 is also real, otherwise either coin 5 or coin 7 can be fake, i.e. coin 6 is obviously real. Let now in the first weighing one of the bowls, for example with coins 1 and 2, outweigh. Then coins 5, 6, 7 are definitely real. Let us now compare coins 1 and 2, paying for the weighing with coin 4. If the scales are in balance, then coins 1 and 2 are real. If one of them outweighs, then the lighter coin and coin 3 are real. In all cases, we get five obviously real coins. Finding the above solution is not very difficult, but readers may have a question: why are the numbers 8 and 5 given in the problem statement? You can understand this by solving the following exercise.

Exercise 1. How many real coins can you find if there are 4, 5,..., 9 coins at the beginning?

⟦column⟧

From Exercise 1 it is clear that if at the beginning there are $n d^{8}$ coins, then it is possible to find at least n 3 real coins, but with $n^{9}$ this becomes impossible. It is natural to assume that even with $n !^{9}$ at least four coins will be “lost”. In this regard, the following question arises. Problem 2. For each k, find the largest n for which it is possible to find n k real ones from the n coins initially available. Before solving this problem, let's consider a simpler analogue, when it is known in which direction the weight of the counterfeit coin differs from the weight of the real one. To be certain, let's assume that the counterfeit coin is lighter than the real one. The case is analyzed similarly when a counterfeit coin can be either lighter or heavier than a real one, but for each of the available coins it is known in which direction its weight may differ from the real one. Let us make a few preliminary remarks. 1. When planning the next weigh-in, you can assume that real coins were paid in all previous weigh-ins. Indeed, if, guided by this assumption, we identified some coins as real, then these coins will be real even in the case where a counterfeit coin was once paid. 2. For each weighing you must pay with a suspicious coin (i.e. one that is not yet clear whether it is fake or real). Indeed, if it is known for sure that a coin is counterfeit, then further weighing is not needed at all, and if the coin is known to be real, then by paying a suspicious one instead, we will be able to determine, according to the previous remark, no less new real coins, but we will save the existing one. 3. The number of weighings made cannot exceed $k^{1}$. Indeed, after k weighings we will have only n k coins left, among which there may be a counterfeit one. Let's now see how the number of suspicious coins changes during the next weighing.
