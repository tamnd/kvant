KIRCHHOFF (TO THE 200TH ANNIVERSARY OF THE BIRTH)

Kirchhoff and Bunsen become work colleagues and great friends for many years to come. This friendship was not hindered by the difference in their ages and appearances - Bunsen is a head taller and a yard wider in the shoulders, his mass is twice as large. Try to imagine their scientific disputes! Unfortunately, there is no surviving evidence of these disputes. But the truth was born in them - a method of spectral analysis of chemical elements and their compounds was created. Kirchhoff more than 150 years ago gave the coined formulation of this research method: “A solid at a high temperature, surrounded by a rarefied gas at a lower temperature, emits light in a continuous spectrum with intervals at discrete wavelengths, the position of which depends on the chemical composition of the gas.” These gaps (dark lines against the background of a solid color spectrum) are called Fraunhofer lines. They were discovered back in 1802 by the English physicist and chemist William Wollaston and studied and described by the German physicist Joseph Fraunhofer in 1814. But it was Kirchhoff

In 1852, Bunsen was invited to the University of Heidelberg, where at that time the famous humanities university was being transformed into a European center of mathematics and natural sciences. Kirchhoff and Bunsen became professors at this university. Kirchhoff worked there for 20 years, where he did important work on spectral analysis. First of all, he carefully studied the Fraunhofer lines of the solar corona and determined the presence of many chemical elements in the Sun, in particular sodium, magnesium, zinc, iron, and copper. Together with Bunsen, he discovered elements such as rubidium and cesium in the waters of mineral springs (in the German city of Bad Durkheim, famous for its climate, medicinal waters and wines). They were named according to the colors of the spectrum - “deep red” and “blue-blue”. Kirchhoff initially used only the prismatic spectrum in the visible region.
