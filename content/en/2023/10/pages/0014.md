There are no such solitary bodies. But the history of technology is structured in such a way that it is more convenient to work not with any kind of bodies, but with cylindrical ones. figure 6 shows trimmer capacitors with cylindrical plates that move when rotated. If by some miracle you remember the equation for the electric field between cylindrical electrodes, then you will get the formula for capacitance instantly. Of course, we neglect edge effects, and taking them into account is a solvable problem, but not a school one; we can only get an estimate. At figure 7 we see two examples of another implementation of cylindrical geometry. One of the electrodes is a cylinder, the other is a screw (naturally, also cylindrical), coaxial with the cylinder; when rotated, the screw moves along the axis. There is a fundamental difference here, from the point of view of electrostatics, from previous examples. The dimensions of the screw and its location are such that the entire change in capacitance occurs in the zone of edge effects. Therefore, the “school” calculation, which is generally possible here, does not work with these specific sizes. However, this circumstance does not prevent these capacitors from working. By the way, there are two questions. First: how could one make a cylindrical capacitor with a screw that does not operate due to edge effects? Second: how in this case could one implement a nonlinear dependence of capacitance on angle
