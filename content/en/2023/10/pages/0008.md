Since at most one edge of a given color comes out of each vertex, there is only one such path. If we swap the colors of the a and b edges along this path, we get a different coloring of the edges of G without vertex v, which again uses only colors 1, 2, ..., m. In this case, in the corresponding pile $S_{x}$ the a-card is replaced with a b-card from the deck. If the path does not end at a vertex adjacent to v, then no other stack is changed (i.e. the devil missed his turn). Let us assume that the path ends at a vertex adjacent to v, and denote it y. Then y is not x, because x has no edges of color a and only one edge of color b. If G is a bipartite graph, then the length of the path in question is even, since otherwise this path, together with the edge vx and the edge vy, would create a cycle of odd length. Therefore, since the path started with an edge of color b, it must end with an edge of color a. Changing colors along the path corresponds to replacing a b-card in the $S_{y}$ pile with an a-card from the deck. We see that the change of colors of the edges along the path in the bipartite graph corresponds to the player's move, followed by the move of the Koenig devil. If G is not bipartite, then the last edge of our path can be colored either color a or color b and, therefore, the change in colors of the edges along the path corresponds to the player's move, followed by Weezing's move. Now suppose that one of the theorems is false. Consider a graph with a minimum number of vertices for which the theorem is false. Since a graph with only one vertex has no edges, it can be colored with zero colors. Therefore G has at least two vertices. Let v be a vertex of degree k and in G there are no vertices of degree greater than k (so k is the maximum

degree of vertices in G). The minimality condition on G implies that removing vertex v will result in a graph that can be colored using m colors, where m equals k for König's theorem and m equals k 1 for Viesing's theorem. Then for each neighbor x of v there are m 1 d cards in the stack $S_{x}$, where d is the number of neighbors of x. Since d d k, this is at least m 1 k cards, i.e. at least one map for König's theorem and at least two maps for Viesing's theorem. After playing the k-game, the player will collect a winning set of k cards of different values, taking one card from each pile. Then coloring the edges emanating from v in colors with the numbers of these cards will give an edge coloring of G using m colors. Contradiction. Therefore, both theorems are true!
