A few more days passed, and Pinocchio went to the Properly Triangular Forest. This forest had the shape of a regular, in other words, an equilateral triangle. Now the navigator indicated the direction perpendicular to the side of the triangle. – And I will return to the starting point again? - Pinocchio asked in the morning. “Yes, but only for a special starting point,” answered Papa Carlo. – There are exactly two such points at the edge of the forest. They divide the edge into three equal parts. From one you must move clockwise, and from the other counterclockwise. After completing three straight sections, you will certainly return to the beginning of the path. Pinocchio did just that (Fig. 1, g). He walked along one three-link broken line (red) and another (blue). And one day he decided to find out what the path along the perpendiculars would be for an arbitrary starting point. And so, having put a piece of pie and a flask of water in your school bag, what if the journey turns out to be long? – he went on the road. At first there was nothing interesting. Having passed three segments (they are shown in blue on figure 2), he again found himself at the edge of the forest near the city wall. Here he, without stopping, continued moving, following the directions of the navigator. Now he has completed three more blue sections. The next three sections of his path are red. Eventually he got tired and returned home. - Papa Carlo, what would happen if I continued and continued my path along perpendiculars to the sides of an equilateral triangle? – Pinocchio asked after talking about his observations. – Would I end up on a closed triangular trajectory? How many laps would I take in the forest? “The answer is this, my young traveler and lover of geometry,” began Papa Carlo. – Speaking theoretically, it is impossible to get onto a triangular trajectory. Your path would consist of an infinite number of links. But the longer you walked, the closer the three successive links of your path would be located near the sides of the triangle that you passed, moving from a special point at the edge. Accordingly, at the edge of the forest you would find yourself closer and closer to a special starting point, dividing the length of the edge in a ratio of 1:2. - Very interesting! - Buratino exclaimed. – Can I prove this strictly mathematically? - Try! This requires knowledge of geometric progression. So the proof of what I told you can be put aside for now. And conducting a mathematical experiment on paper is not at all difficult.
