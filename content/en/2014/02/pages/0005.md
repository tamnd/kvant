So, it was discovered that beta rays (and this is the beginning of beta spectra, neutrino physics) have a charge. Following this, it was found that these were fast electrons. And once you have fast electrons and a magnetic field, you can build a beta spectrometer. Beta spectrometers were built quite quickly, first using photographic plates, then Geiger counters. From about 1910 to 1914, it was shown that the spectrum of beta rays is very peculiar - it is a kind of continuous spectrum on which peaks stick out. Moreover, these peaks were blurred, and it was believed that this was simply bad experimental equipment, and what we call a continuous spectrum was perhaps also an artifact associated with the shortcomings of our equipment. And at the same time, the model of the atom was formulated for the first time. As is known, first Rutherford’s experiment and then Bohr’s theory gave us the first model of the atom, according to which the atom consists of a point nucleus and a very large cloud of electrons (the dimensions of the nucleus are on the order of $10^{-} 14$ m, the dimensions of the electron cloud are $10^{-} 10$ m, i.e. 4 orders of magnitude larger). The question arose: where do beta electrons come from? From inside or outside? Are these electrons that have been knocked out of the atomic shell, or have they come out of the nucleus? The first person to say the word "nucleus" was Niels Bohr, who said: beta rays are emitted from the nucleus. How to study beta decay spectra? Rutherford proposed a strikingly interesting idea: let's turn the situation around and use gamma rays, which are present in radioactive radiation, to irradiate the same nucleus again. Then if gamma rays hit the nucleus, they can knock out the outer electrons, and the energy of the gamma rays can be measured from the energy of those electrons (since there was no way to measure gamma rays at that time). When this idea was implemented in an experiment, it turned out that the peaks that were observed in the beta spectra - they are now called conversion lines - made it possible to measure the energy of the electrons. And it turned out that the energy of electrons (more precisely, the difference in their energies) is quite strictly related to the energies of atomic spectra. Thus, in fact, the electrons that are observed in the spectrum, these peaks, are associated with external electrons, and not with internal ones. But how do they arise? The following model has emerged: gamma rays are emitted by nuclei, these gamma rays are absorbed by electrons, which knock out outer electrons, shell electrons. This is how electrons appear, corresponding to lines that in atomic physics are known as K, L, M lines. But then the kernel is not simple: it is also complex, and not a point, as Rutherford was; in fact it is a complex education.
