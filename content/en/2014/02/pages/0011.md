Geometry on parquet

V. DUBROVSKY

MODERN GEOMETRY PARQUETS, OR PAVINGS, i.e. Covering a plane with polygons without gaps or overlaps is a subject for independent study. In this article we will see how they can be used as a tool for proving “ordinary”, even classical theorems that initially have nothing to do with tilings. The evidence we will discuss is not the only one, nor even the shortest. But they are certainly beautiful, and I hope the readers will enjoy them as much as the author. Measuring areas by counting nodes

Let's consider a parquet of parallelograms and an arbitrary figure on a plane. The area of ​​this figure is approximately equal to the number of tiles it contains multiplied by the area of ​​the tile. The smaller the tiles, the more accurate this equality. Here we consider that the tiles are crushed and the figure is fixed. But one can imagine that the parquet is fixed, and the figure swells, maintaining its shape. Then, considering the area of ​​the tile to be equal to 1, we find that the area of ​​the figure is approximately equal to the number of tiles that lie entirely inside it. And the more inflated the figure is, the smaller the relative error of such a measurement. But instead of tiles, you can count dots - the tops of the tiles! The set of vertices of all parallelograms of our parquet is called a lattice, and the vertices themselves are called its nodes. Each lattice node is the lower left vertex of some (and only one) parallelogram from the corresponding tiling. Therefore, the number of nodes inside a figure is not less than the number of parallelograms contained in it, and not more than the number of parallelograms that have common points with it. This means that, again taking the area of ​​one parallelogram as one (let’s agree to continue to count this way unless otherwise specifically stated), we can say that the area of ​​the figure is approximately equal to the number of nodes that it covers. By “inflating” the figure, the relative error of this equality can be made as small as desired, but ideal accuracy cannot be achieved for all figures. However, for some types of figures, counting nodes allows you to calculate the area absolutely accurately. One of these formulas is given by Pick's theorem: the area of ​​a polygon with vertices at lattice nodes is equal to i + b 2 - 1, where i is the number of nodes inside the polygon, and b is the number of nodes on its border. We will remember this formula later (see also the article by N.B. Vasiliev “Around the formula $Пика»^{1} ),$ but here we will use another method of calculating areas.
