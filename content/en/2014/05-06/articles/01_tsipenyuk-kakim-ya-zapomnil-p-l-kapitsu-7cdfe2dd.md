FROM THE HISTORY OF SCIENCE

How I remember P. L. Kapitsa

Y.TSIPENYUK

ON THE 8TH OF JULY 2014, THE 120th anniversary of the birth of the outstanding scientist Pyotr Leonidovich Kapitsa was celebrated. I was lucky enough to work next to him for more than 20 years at the Institute of Physical Problems (IPP), which he created, which now bears his name. The Institute of Physical Problems is very small - it has about 50 researchers, and, having become an employee of the IPP, I, naturally, often met with Pyotr Leonidovich. Communication with him left a very vivid impression, since he was a wise man, his actions and judgments were always extraordinary. Pyotr Leonidovich does not have students in the usual sense, when the leader constantly discusses with the student methods for solving the problem, the results obtained and ways for further development of research. But in reality he has a lot of students - those who inherited the style of his work, the ability to critically analyze both their results and the results of other scientists, those for whom his active civic position became their own position. The portrait of Pyotr Leonidovich Kapitsa for me comes together like a puzzle from many episodes. Some of them are given below.

* * * I first appeared at the IPP in 1960, while still a fourth-year student at the Moscow Institute of Physics and Technology (MIPT). The fact is that the training of senior students in research institutes was laid down by the creators of MIPT, one of whom was P.L. Kapitsa. He believed that in order to educate creative young researchers, it is necessary that they become involved in scientific work as early as possible under the guidance of scientists actively working in science. I ended up in the group of his son Sergei Petrovich, who was recruiting students to work on the electron accelerator he created - the microtron. At first, I dealt with the issues of measuring high-frequency fields in accelerating cavities, and as a thesis, S.P. Kapitsa suggested that I consider the question of how to study the recently discovered Mössbauer effect on a microtron. It took 3 months of studying this phenomenon, but I was unable to suggest any reasonable experiments. Sergei Petrovich called for help from Academician Yu.M. Kagan, who spoke categorically: “You won’t succeed.” A stalemate has created. Sergei Petrovich talked with his father, who said: “He did a good job in the fifth year, even if he defends it ahead of schedule. There was already a precedent with Andreev, a student of Landau. Why can theorists defend themselves ahead of schedule, but experimenters cannot?” That’s how I successfully defended myself, and they left me to work at the institute. In this regard, it should be noted that Pyotr Leonidovich was always very attentive to students’ theses. He believed that the beginning of a young man’s journey must be successful so that he

* * * In 1960, Niels Bohr came to Moscow, and, naturally, he visited the Institute of Physical Problems, with whose director, P.L. Kapitsa, he had long years of friendship. There was a fear that many people would want to come to Bohr’s speech, and “face control” was installed at the entrance, i.e. Only familiar research assistants were allowed through. The conference room was, of course, filled, but there was no rush. When this became clear, we students were allowed to sit in the back rows. I will forever remember the kind smile on Niels Bohr’s face, Kapitsa’s sparkling wit, and the fantastically friendly atmosphere in the hall. Introducing Bohr to the audience, Pyotr Leonidovich said: “Every schoolchild knows that a Bohr atom is not a boron atom, but a hydrogen atom.” I especially remember one episode from this seminar. Kapitsa asked Bohr why young people from all over the world were so eager to work at his Institute for Theoretical Physics in Copenhagen. Evgeny Mikhailovich Livshits translated the entire discussion. As Lifshits translated, Bohr responded: “We are never afraid to tell a young man that he is talking nonsense.” And then the hall exploded, they heard: “Zhenya, you translated incorrectly.” Livshits asked Bohr again and, apologetically, corrected himself: “Nils said that they are never afraid of looking like fools.” Kapitza immediately reacted to this, saying that the difference between Bohr and Landau is that when the interlocutor is wrong, Landau says: “You are a fool,” and Bohr says: “This is amazing.”

* * * In the 60s, a new youth entertainment program “3+2” appeared on television, in which they competed in erudition, first in the field, and at the final stage right in the studio. Participants had to answer questions on various fields of knowledge - biology, physics, chemistry, radio engineering, etc. Pyotr Leonidovich was asked to be a member of the physics jury, but he, of course, refused and recommended me (I don’t know why he decided that way). When I showed up at the Institute the next morning, Kapitsa’s assistant found me and told me that in his office

When I entered the office, everyone was assembled, and Kapitsa said: “And here is our television star.” I became embarrassed and replied: “Well, what kind of star am I?”, to which Kapitsa instantly responded: “But I didn’t say what size star!”

* * * In the early 60s, quite a lot of young, enterprising employees appeared at the Institute, and, quite naturally, they had a desire to somehow express themselves, to show their youthful enthusiasm. So, for example, the idea arose to organize a special youth physics seminar, where it would be possible to discuss not only completed works, but also those that are “slipping.” The reason for this, it would seem, was reasonable: at an adult seminar, a young person is often embarrassed to express his opinions. When Kapitsa was asked about the formation of a youth seminar, he did not object, but unobtrusively said: “Of course, try, but this idea seems unviable to me. Science cannot be for adults and youth; its criteria are the same for all ages. And young people need to learn to participate in scientific discussions; discussion is the driving force of science, it is programmed in its very nature.” Such a seminar was organized, but, as Kapitsa predicted, it did not last long.

* * * Pyotr Leonidovich invariably emphasized the paramount importance of the experiment. In his opinion, one should strive to set up an experiment in such a way that it leads to the discovery of new facts, on the basis of which statements can be made that are independent of existing ideas. Especially valuable is such work, the results of which contradict existing ideas. Piotr Leonidovich often jokingly called experimental work that only confirmed existing predictions of the theory “closure.”

* * * While developing the theory of short wave generators of a new type, Kapitsa, in particular, put forward his hypothesis about the nature of ball lightning. The main problem was the question of why ball lightning exists for so long and where the energy comes from to maintain a high temperature. Pyotr Leonidovich suggested that energy comes from the clouds through the discharge channel, like a waveguide. Young employees of the Institute, led by the proactive E.L. Kosarev, proposed to test this assumption, for which an expedition was organized to the mountains of Armenia. If Kapitsa is right, then during a thunderstorm powerful electromagnetic radiation in the ultrahigh frequency region should be observed. Almost no radiation was detected, but based on the results of this expedition, an article was written for publication in the journal of technical physics. As is usually customary, Kapitsa was also included as a co-author of the article - he supported this work, obtained money for the expedition, and was in fact its ideological inspirer. The text of the article was given to Kapitsa for review. A few days later, Pyotr Leonidovich’s referent returned the article, from which Kapitsa had been excluded as a co-author, and said: “Pyotr Leonidovich does not sign works in which he was not directly involved. In this case, since he actually took part in organizing the expedition, he wrote a short introductory article about the purpose and objectives of the expedition, which was sent simultaneously with your article to the press.”

* * * In 1974, our institute was preparing to celebrate Kapitsa’s 80th birthday. A special show was being prepared in which official and unofficial praise was prohibited. All employees came up with various jokes and practical jokes. One day I passed by the room where A.I. Shalnikov worked. Seeing me, he invited me to come in and showed me a caricature cut out from somewhere. It depicted a centaur who, not fitting into the cab of the truck, sat in the back, and his hands were put on the steering wheel through the cab windows (in ancient Greek mythology, a centaur is a creature with a horse’s body and a human head, chest and arms). I, not understanding anything, asked Shalnikov what this meant. Alexander Iosifovich was a very emotional person, he snatched this cartoon from my hands with the words: “Fools cannot understand!” Only later did I find out that Kapitsa was called a centaur and that A.I. I wanted to suggest that I use this caricature in the upcoming skit.

* * * At the end of the 70s, I wrote my doctoral dissertation and was going to defend it at the Joint Institute for Nuclear Research in Dubna. For protection, I had to provide a reference from my place of work. When I made such a request, they answered me - write it yourself, and we will sign it. Just at this time I went to Kapitsa’s office on some matter, and he asked me how things were going with my dissertation defense. I complained to him that I myself should write about what a good, smart and creative worker I am. Here Pyotr Leonidovich’s eyes sparkled, and he said: “It’s customary, this is the rule of the game. Is he really dear to you when you write “Dear Ivan Ivanovich”? Go ahead and don’t hesitate.”

* * * While still working in Cambridge (England), Pyotr Leonidovich organized a seminar at which all new scientific results were presented. Arriving in Moscow, he organized the same seminar at the Institute of Physical Problems, known to everyone as the “capishnik”. The speakers at this seminar were mainly physicists, but sometimes also leading scientists from other fields of science. So, I remember how E.A. Liberman spoke about achievements in biophysics at the kapishnik, and A.M. Zhabotinsky spoke about self-oscillatory chemical reactions. Once at the kapishnik, the famous philosopher G.I. Naan gave a report “The unit is as uncertain as infinity.” Kapitsa always warned the speaker that there should be 10 minutes left for discussion. When Naan finished his speech, Pyotr Leonidovich remarked: “The fact that unit is as uncertain as infinity is quite obvious. But I have this question for you: why do physicists need philosophers?” Silence reigned in the hall, but Naan, without embarrassment, replied: “We must warn you against slipping into idealism, naive materialism and subjectivism.” To this, Pyotr Leonidovich remarked: “It’s a pity that you took on the functions of the police.”

* * * Disagreements happen in any team, this happened with us too, and one of the participants in the conflict was forced to go to work at the university. A few years later he wrote his doctoral dissertation, which included the results he obtained at our institute. The co-author of these works was another participant in the conflict, and at a meeting of the Academic Council he made a statement that these results could not be included in the dissertation, since the role of the dissertation author in obtaining them was not decisive.

I remember Kapitsa’s reaction to this statement forever. Pyotr Leonidovich said the following: “I want to tell you the following parable about this. Three friends and a dog were walking along the river bank. And then one of them said: “Look, on the opposite bank there is some kind of package, there may be something valuable there.” Then the other companion said: “Let our third friend, whose dog is walking with us, order her to swim across the river and bring the package.” They did so, and the package actually contained valuables. And this is where the controversy began. The first says: “I saw the package, and I’m entitled to most of it,” to which the second objected: “But I figured out how to get this package,” and the third remarked: “What would you do without my dog!” This is a typical situation in science. A theorist predicted a certain phenomenon, an experimenter figured out how to test it, and another experimenter assigned his graduate student to carry out the measurements. When the result is accepted, disputes begin as to who made the main contribution. A similar situation arises in many areas of human activity. I asked English lawyers what they did in these cases, and the answer was unequivocal - success is shared equally between the participants. Therefore, in our case, all co-authors have equal rights and can, if necessary, use the results obtained.”

* * * Pyotr Leonidovich believed that he should know everything about the lives of his employees, and, if he could, he never refused to solve various everyday problems. In this regard, I recall the following incident. One of the young employees had trouble - a few months after the wedding, his wife died of cancer. His friends were constantly discussing how this grief could be alleviated. We remembered that he really wanted to visit the Far East, but the young man couldn’t afford to go. There is a way out - to go there on a business trip, but how can this be done? When Pyotr Leonidovich found out about this sad incident, he was very offended that he was not told about it earlier, and resolved the issue with the business trip very simply. At that time, his son Andrei Petrovich Kapitsa was the chairman of the Presidium of the Far Eastern Scientific Center of the Academy of Sciences, and Pyotr Leonidovich sent a young employee, with whom such a misfortune happened, on a business trip to the Far East “to familiarize himself with the work of the Far Eastern Branch.”

* * * In the USSR, it was common practice for workers of scientific institutions to travel to collect crops to harvest crops, work at vegetable bases to sort vegetables, load and unload them. It was believed that scientific work would not suffer from this. Sometimes the command “Emergency!” was heard. – some city services do not have time to do anything. So, one day a demand came from the district party committee that the Institute allocate five employees to work at a waste incineration plant, which the builders were unable to complete on time. Having learned about this demand from the district party committee, Kapitsa firmly said: “No! They cannot dictate to us, this is not our superior academic or government organization!” In response to Kapitsa’s disobedience, the district committee said: “If so, let your employees work at the construction site on weekends.” When Pyotr Leonidovich was informed about this, he reacted to it in a very unique way: “I do not allow my employees to work on weekends. Well, it turns out that they will be so tired these days that when they come to work on Monday, they will sleep. I can't allow this." This epic ended with the Institute providing a car for the work.

* * * Pyotr Leonidovich never allowed anyone to treat him with disrespect. I remember one incident in his life that completely characterizes him from this side. Pyotr Leonidovich received a letter from the country's leadership in which he was informed that he was included in a very representative delegation to participate in the International Conference. There was one week left before the start of the conference, but Kapitsa was assured that all problems with obtaining a visa and tickets would be successfully resolved. Of course, it was quite an honor to participate in such a conference and be part of the delegation from our country, but Kapitsa’s answer was unexpected. Pyotr Leonidovich thanked for the invitation, but at the same time wrote: “I am forced to refuse to participate in the conference. If the leadership of the delegation wanted my participation and I was informed about this in advance, I would prepare a speech in which I could express my view on the problems being discussed. I was given too little time to do this, and I’m not fit to be part of the decoration delegation.” Like this!

* * * P.L. Kapitsa discussed the role of science in the development and functioning of society in his report at the International Symposium dedicated to the 100th anniversary of the birth of Albert Einstein: “History invariably shows that almost any major scientific discovery or theory affects the development of civilization in our society. This is especially clear from the following examples. It seemed that the small-scale and initially ineffective discoveries made over the past two centuries by Franklin, Galvani, Oersted or Faraday in the field of electricity, and their theoretical generalization made by Maxwell, led to modern electrical engineering, on which the life and industrial production of modern civilization are mainly based. The role of science was no less clearly demonstrated in the study of radioactivity, discovered by Becquerel in 1896. At first, his discovery was perceived as a curious, but unimportant natural phenomenon. Scientific research by Curie and Rutherford showed that this phenomenon is of a fundamental nature and is associated with processes occurring in the nuclei of atoms. Less than 100 years have passed since the discovery of this phenomenon, and it has already given humanity the most powerful source of energy. Nuclear energy has also given people weapons of such destructive power that the fear of their use forces states to radically change their attitude towards military conflicts.”

* * * In conclusion, I would like to quote the words of the remarkable Soviet physicist G.A. Askaryan about P.L. Kapitsa: “He helped so many people through the journals he led, with his lectures, books, and activities at MIPT, into the organization of which he invested a lot of soul and time. That is why he was so loved by students, artists, sculptors, and poets, whom he supported in the most difficult periods for them, organizing exhibitions and performances at his institute. And he did all this simply - as a matter of course, because he loved science, humanity, civilization, creativity, art and Russia, to the greatness of which he dedicated his entire life, victorious and tragic, among friends, fans and wolves, and walked through thorns to the stars of the sky of history. His star will shine forever and brightly. Look at her more often, remember and thank your fate for being contemporaries of her earthly ascent.”
