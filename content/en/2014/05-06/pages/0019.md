Typically, several species of predators live in the same territory, feeding on several species of animals, including predators. Each predator-prey system has its own frequency and its own oscillation phase. If there are many such systems and they overlap each other, then fluctuations in the number of animals become smaller. The damping mechanism here is the same as in the case of pendulums oscillating with different periods. And yet there are cases when, over a large area, one type of predator confronts only one type of prey. As a result, the numbers of these species undergo very large changes over time, which is fully consistent with the Volterra–Lotka model. A classic example of this is the lynx-hare community in the Hudson Bay region of North America. figure 3 shows

And if a shark is found nearby, the fish will swim in the direction opposite to the predator. With this algorithm of fish behavior, significant and regular fluctuations in the number of individuals occur much less frequently. Questions about the correctness of the model almost always arise when trying to simulate complex processes in nature and society. On the one hand, any modeling is impossible without simplifying the process, without neglecting minor details. On the other hand, there is a risk of “oversimplifying” the model, discarding important features of the phenomenon, because it is quite difficult to understand which feature of the process is secondary and which is not until it is studied. Therefore, the researcher’s task is to find a middle ground, to create a model of the process without depriving it of its primary features. And here you cannot give any “correct” recommendations - you have to rely only on experience and intuition. Try, reader, by changing the parameters of the described model, to breathe more life into it. Good luck to you!

Ecology and physics (instead of a conclusion) The reader who has reached the end of our discussions will be interested and pleased to know that, while watching the exciting adventures of sharks and mackerels, he simultaneously and completely freely acquired an understanding, for example, of... the kinetics of chemical and nuclear reactions. (Kinetics describes the development of a process over time.) Particles - let's call them reagents - move due to diffusion, meeting each other, enter into reactions in which they “die”, produce new particles, etc. The reproduction of fish corresponds, for example, to a nuclear chain reaction, their dying corresponds to the absorption of particles in the reactor. To solve such problems, one of the techniques we described is usually used. By writing equations similar to equations (1), one gets a rougher, averaged understanding of how the number of particles in the system changes over time. Another approach - computer modeling of the system - allows one to obtain a more detailed (taking into account spatial inhomogeneities) description of the processes, but requires much more computer time. When solving these problems, physicists actively use qualitative express analysis, simulate the system on modern computers, and rack their brains over which “rules of the game” are more consistent with the real system. In a word, they are doing the same thing that you and I did, solving a “purely ecological” problem - studying the size of populations in the animal world. And it would seem - what is common between physics and ecology?
