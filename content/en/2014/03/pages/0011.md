In the general case, it is easy to see that a graph with edges added according to copy number is also Eulerian, since the half-degrees of the outgoing and incoming vertex (corresponding to the (l – 1)-measure) are equal to the number of times this (l – 1)-mer occurs in the genome. Even if they wanted to, they could not meet each other. But in 1988, the paths of three other scientists crossed paths (Fig.15). Radoje Drmanac (Serbia), Andrey Mirzabekov (Russia) and Edwin Southern (UK) simultaneously and independently developed a futuristic and at that time completely incredible method of reading reads using DNA chips. None of these three biologists knew about the work of Euler, Hamilton and de Bruijn; no one could have imagined that the results of their own experiments would bring them face to face with these mathematical giants. In 1977, Fred Sanger and his colleagues sequenced the first virus, a tiny bacteriophage $\phi$ X174, 5375 nucleotides long. Although biologists were regularly sequencing viruses hundreds of thousands of nucleotides long in the late 1980s, the idea of ​​sequencing a bacterial genome (let alone a human one) seemed premature from both an experimental and computational point of view. Drmanac, Mirzabekov and Southern realized that the biggest drawback of the original DNA sequencing technology developed in the 1970s was that it was too expensive for longer genomes. Indeed, reading a single read in the late 1980s cost more than one dollar, and therefore sequencing the genome of any mammal would cost billions of dollars. Because of this high cost, it was impossible to read all l-mers of the genome, a sine qua non for the successful application of the Eulerian approach. DNA chips were invented to make it cheaper to read all l-mers in the genome, albeit with a shorter read length l than the original DNA sequencing technology. For example, while traditional sequencing technologies read reads that were approximately 500 nucleotides long, the inventors of DNA chips focused on reads that were about 15 nucleotides long. DNA chips are designed like this: first, all $4^{l}$ possible l-mers are synthesized (i.e., all DNA fragments of length l), then they are attached to a DNA microchip, which is a lattice with a separate cell for each l-mer.
