Graphs

Having finished with the historical excursion, let's move on to definitions. Let's call a graph a set of vertices connected by edges, where each edge connects two vertices. The abstractness of this definition can be somewhat intimidating, so let us explain that a graph can always be thought of as a map of a road network, where cities are the vertices and the roads connecting them are the edges. Graph theory (the branch of mathematics that deals with the study of graphs) is applicable to many types of problems. Applications of this theory include not only road and telephone or computer networks, but also less obvious examples, such as studying the spread of epidemics or modeling links between web pages on the Internet. In particular, we can apply graph theory to our two historical problems. In the problem of the Königsberg bridges, we obtain a graph K if we match each of the four parts of the city along a vertex, and then draw between two such vertices one edge for each bridge that connects the two corresponding parts of the city (Fig.4,b). And for the “Icosahedral Game” we will build graph I by assigning a vertex to each hole and turning the lines connecting them into corresponding edges (Fig.5,b).

Euler and Hamiltonian cycles

We will now generalize our two historical problems to all possible graphs. Suppose we have a certain graph, which we will denote by G, and imagine that an ant sits on some vertex in G. Just like the residents of Königsberg, who walked on bridges between different parts of the city, an ant can crawl along the edges from peak to peak. If the ant returns to where it started, the result of its walk will be a cycle on G. We ask two questions about cycles on G: 1. Is there a cycle on G such that the ant crawls along each edge exactly once? 2. Is there a cycle on G such that an ant visits each vertex exactly once? The first question is called the Euler Cycle Problem (ECP) - note that the solution to the ECP when our graph is K corresponds to the solution to the Königsberg Bridges problem.
