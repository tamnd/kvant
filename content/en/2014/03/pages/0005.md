GENOME:

Thus, the task of genome sequencing comes down to reading (i.e., determining the sequence) of reads (biological part) and assembling fragments (mathematical part). The first problem has a long and complicated history dating back to the 70s, when Walter Hilbert and Fred Sanger received the Nobel Prize for inventing the first method of reading reads. In the early 1990s, modern sequencers (machines for identifying pieces of DNA sequence) came onto the market, and from then on a new era began for the task of sequencing. In 2000, several hundred of these machines, working nonstop for more than a year, read a sufficiently large number of reads to possibly determine the sequence of the human genome. Assembling this genome required several months of work on some of the most powerful supercomputers of the time.

A little later we will talk in some detail about how reads are read, but for now we will focus on the computational task of assembling fragments - how, using the obtained reads, we can determine the sequence of the entire genome. Although both the recovery of the newspaper and the assembly of the fragments come down to putting together a puzzle, the task of assembly is much more complex, and not only because of its gigantic scale (recall that we may need to process a billion reads). Firstly, the newspaper is written in some understandable language. Its rules will tell us whether any two passages can be adjacent, regardless of whether they overlap or not (Fig.3,a). At the same time, the rules of DNA “language” are largely unknown to biologists, and based on sequence alone, it is almost impossible to understand whether two non-overlapping reads can be located close or far from each other. The second difficulty in assembling fragments is that the alphabet of DNA sequences consists of only 4 letters: A, T, G and C. Working with a small alphabet complicates the restoration of the original sequence, since a larger number of intersections will be caused by random reasons (Fig.3,b). Thirdly, any DNA sequence contains many “conserved regions” - nucleotide “words” that are repeated many times in a row with virtually no changes. For example, in the human genome, the so-called Alu repeat sequence (approximately 300 nucleotides long) occurs over a million times, with only a few nucleotides changed in each occurrence.
