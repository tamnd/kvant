Again about knights and knaves

Our question should be an "indicator" of knights and knaves: i.e. either knights must always answer “ya”, and knaves must always answer “wa”, or vice versa. Let’s ask any of the aborigines the question “Ya – is it Yes?” Option 1: Let "ya" mean "yes." Then the knight must answer “yes” to such a question, i.e. "ya." A liar must answer such a question “no”, i.e. "va". Option 2: Let "ya" mean "no." Then the knight answers “no” to such a question, i.e. it's "yay" again! And the liar must answer “yes”, i.e. "Wah" again! So, the knight always answers “ya” to our question, and the liar always answers “va”. The problem is solved.

L.BOYKO, M.BOYKO

THE READER HAS SURELY ENCOUNTERED INTERESTING PROBLEMS about knights who always tell the truth and liars who always lie. Sometimes other characters appear in similar tasks.

Who are these cunning people? Task 1 (XXVIII Tournament of Cities). It is known that liars always lie, knights always tell the truth, and cunning people can lie and tell the truth. You can ask questions that can be answered “yes” or “no” (for example: “Is it true that this person is a cunning person?”). Before you are three - a liar, a knight and a cunning man, who know which one is which. How can you find out? There are several solutions to this problem. Before you read one of them in the next paragraph, try to come up with your own! The key to the solution may be some question asked of each of the three people, the answer to which will distinguish the knight from the liar. For example, let’s ask each of three people: “Are you cunning?” The knight will answer "no", the liar will answer "yes". Whatever the cunning one answers, among the three answers we receive there is either a single “no” or a single “yes”. In the first case, we unambiguously identify the knight, by asking whom it is then easy to obtain information about the other two people. In the second case, we clearly identify the liar. Further, asking him about other people (taking into account the fact that the liar’s answers are opposite to the truth), we again learn information about other people.

Exercise (XXVIII Tournament of Cities). Let, in the conditions of task 1, there are four people in front of you - a liar, a knight and two cunning people (all four know which one is which). Prove that cunning people can agree to answer in such a way that you, asking these four, will not know for sure who he is.

Language difficulties

But what if you don't know the local language very well? Problem 2. Some of the inhabitants of a certain island are knights, the rest are liars. The islanders understand our language, but we only know about the language of the islanders that in it one of the words “ya” and “va” means “yes”, and the other means “no” (but we don’t remember exactly how). Let us meet two natives, one of whom is a knight, and the other is a liar. Can we, by asking just one question (assuming a “yes” or “no” answer, i.e. “ya” or “va”), find out which of them is which?

A bit of history In 1992, the American philosopher and logician George Boulos published a problem that he called “The Hardest Logical Problem.” Outwardly, “The Most Difficult Problem” is similar to task 1 discussed above, but it has the additional complexity that was in task 2: the characters understand our language, but answer us exclusively in their own language, which is incomprehensible to us. J. Boulos calls the characters mythical gods, but we will allow ourselves to preserve the more familiar characters. In addition, it seems more natural to us, since J. Boulos points out Raymond Smullyan as the author of “The Most Difficult Problem”. We do not know whether this is really the case, but certainly R. In the Russian translation of this book, as a rule, exactly these characters were used: knights and liars.

Statement of the “Most Complex Logical Problem” So, let’s give the exact formulation of the “Most Complex Logical Problem”. Task 3. On the island of Java, people are divided into three types: knights - those who always tell the truth; liars are those who always lie; and cunning ones are those who can tell both the truth and lies. Suppose there are three people in front of us - Mr. A, Mr. B, Mr. C. We know that among them there is exactly one knight, one liar and one cunning, but we do not know which of them is which. You need to determine which of the people is a knight, which is a liar, and which is a cunning person by asking no more than three questions. Questions should be such that they can be answered “yes” or “no”. Each question is asked to only one person. An additional complication is that the islanders understand our language, but answer us exclusively in their own language. We know that in this language there are two words “ya” and “wa”, which mean “yes” and “no”, but we do not know which word means “yes” and which word means “no”.
