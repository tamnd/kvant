Again about knights and knaves

Our question should be an "indicator" of knights and knaves: i.e. either knights must always answer “ya”, and knaves must always answer “wa”, or vice versa. Let’s ask any of the aborigines the question “Ya – is it Yes?” Option 1: Let "ya" mean "yes." Then the knight must answer “yes” to such a question, i.e. "ya." A liar must answer such a question “no”, i.e. "va". Option 2: Let "ya" mean "no." Then the knight answers “no” to such a question, i.e. it's "yay" again! And the liar must answer “yes”, i.e. "Wah" again! So, the knight always answers “ya” to our question, and the liar always answers “va”. The problem is solved.

THE READER HAS SURELY ENCOUNTERED INTERESTING PROBLEMS about knights who always tell the truth and liars who always lie. Sometimes other characters appear in similar tasks.

Who are these cunning people? Task 1 (XXVIII Tournament of Cities). It is known that liars always lie, knights always tell the truth, and cunning people can lie and tell the truth. You can ask questions that can be answered “yes” or “no” (for example: “Is it true that this person is a cunning person?”). Before you are three - a liar, a knight and a cunning man, who know which one is which. How can you find out? There are several solutions to this problem. Before you read one of them in the next paragraph, try to come up with your own! The key to the solution may be some question asked of each of the three people, the answer to which will distinguish the knight from the liar. For example, let’s ask each of three people: “Are you cunning?” The knight will answer "no", the liar will answer "yes". Whatever the cunning one answers, among the three answers we receive there is either a single “no” or a single “yes”. In the first case, we unambiguously identify the knight, by asking whom it is then easy to obtain information about the other two people. In the second case, we clearly identify the liar. Further, asking him about other people (taking into account the fact that the liar’s answers are opposite to the truth), we again learn information about other people.

Exercise (XXVIII Tournament of Cities). Let, in the conditions of task 1, there are four people in front of you - a liar, a knight and two cunning people (all four know which one is which). Prove that cunning people can agree to answer in such a way that you, asking these four, will not know for sure who he is.

Language difficulties

But what if you don't know the local language very well? Problem 2. Some of the inhabitants of a certain island are knights, the rest are liars. The islanders understand our language, but we only know about the language of the islanders that in it one of the words “ya” and “va” means “yes”, and the other means “no” (but we don’t remember exactly how). Let us meet two natives, one of whom is a knight, and the other is a liar. Can we, by asking just one question (assuming a “yes” or “no” answer, i.e. “ya” or “va”), find out which of them is which?

A bit of history In 1992, the American philosopher and logician George Boulos published a problem that he called “The Hardest Logical Problem.” Outwardly, “The Most Difficult Problem” is similar to task 1 discussed above, but it has the additional complexity that was in task 2: the characters understand our language, but answer us exclusively in their own language, which is incomprehensible to us. J. Boulos calls the characters mythical gods, but we will allow ourselves to preserve the more familiar characters. In addition, it seems more natural to us, since J. Boulos points out Raymond Smullyan as the author of “The Most Difficult Problem”. We do not know whether this is really the case, but certainly R. Smullian is the most famous popularizer of problems of this kind. The authors of this article took great interest in solving logical problems from the books of R. Smullyan at school. In the Russian translation of this book, as a rule, exactly these characters were used: knights and liars.

Statement of the “Most Complex Logical Problem” So, let’s give the exact formulation of the “Most Complex Logical Problem”. Task 3. On the island of Java, people are divided into three types: knights - those who always tell the truth; liars are those who always lie; and cunning ones are those who can tell both the truth and lies. Suppose there are three people in front of us - Mr. A, Mr. B, Mr. C. We know that among them there is exactly one knight, one liar and one cunning, but we do not know which of them is which. You need to determine which of the people is a knight, which is a liar, and which is a cunning person by asking no more than three questions. Questions should be such that they can be answered “yes” or “no”. Each question is asked to only one person. An additional complication is that the islanders understand our language, but answer us exclusively in their own language. We know that in this language there are two words “ya” and “wa”, which mean “yes” and “no”, but we do not know which word means “yes” and which word means “no”. Let us give some explanations to the wording that J. Boulos points out in his article. i You can ask more than one question to one person (in which case other people may not be asked any questions).

i What the next question is and who it is asked of may depend on the answer to the previous question. i You cannot ask paradoxical questions that can be answered with both “yes” and “no” or cannot be answered at all. For example, “Are you going to answer ‘ya’ now?” i The sly man answers questions completely arbitrarily. We can assume that every time he mentally tosses a coin and if it comes up heads, he answers the truth, and if it comes up tails, he lies. Let's add to this that, of course, Mr. A, B and C know each other and know which of them is a liar, which is a knight, and which is a cunning one. Before presenting the solution, let us introduce two mental constructs.

Mr. Ya, Mr. Wa and Mr. Hee Above, we called a knight a man who always tells the truth. In other words, we can say this: a knight is a person who answers “yes” if the statement formulated in the question is true. At the same time, a liar is someone who answers “no” to such a statement. Let us remember that the islanders use their own language. Thus, to a question that states a true statement, both the knave and the knight answer either always “ya” or always “va”. The one who answers “Ya” will be given a middle name – Mr. Ya. And the one who always answers “va” will be called Mr. Va. Finally, we will call the cunning man Mr. He. Note that we cannot know about Mr. Ya whether he is a knight or a liar, but the islanders A, B and C themselves must know which is which. Therefore we can use new names in questions. For example, we can ask Mr. A, “Are you Mr. Ya?” or ask him: “Mr. B is Mr. Va?”

Deck of cards Now take three identical pieces of paper and write “Mr. Ya” on the first one, “Mr. Wa” on the second one, and “Mr. Hee” on the third one. We will receive three cards - the Ya card, the Wa card and the Hi card.

There is a natural correspondence between people and cards. Thus, if the concept of “Mr. A's card” comes up in a question, the islanders should understand it this way: they need to look up who Mr. A is - Mr. Ya, Wa or Hi - and find a card with the corresponding name written on it. The same should be done if the question contains the concept of “your card”. Now let’s put these three cards in a deck: the Ya card on top, then the Wa card and the bottom one – the Hi card. If we don't have paper and can't make such a deck, we have to ask the islanders to do a thought experiment: imagine these three cards in that order. Why was this order of cards chosen? Solution to problem 3

First question

Let's ask Mr. A the following: "Is your card in the deck on Mr. B's card?" Let us prove that if the answer is “ya” to this question, Mr. B is not a cunning person (he is a knight or a liar), and if the answer is “va” to this question, Mr. B is not He. Note that if A is Mr. Xi, then neither B nor C can be Xi, which is what we need. Let's assume that Mr. A is not He and consider his options for answering the question. Let A answer "ya". Option 1: Mr. A is Mr. Ya. Then the statement in the question is true, and Mr. A's card is Y's card. Since in the deck the Ya card lies on the Wa card, then Mr. B is Mr. Va. Option 2: Mr. A is Mr. Ba. Since Mr. Wa answered “yah,” then the statement “Mr. A’s card is on Mr. B’s card” is false. This means that the Wa card does not lie on Mr. B's card. By definition of the deck, the Wa card lies on the Chi card. So Mr. B is not He. Let A answer "va". Option 1: Mr. A is Mr. Ya. The statement in the question is false. The Ya card lies in the deck on the Wa card and does not lie on the Chi card. So Mr. B is He and Mr. C is Mr. Wa. Option 2: Mr. A is Mr. Ba. The statement is true. The Wa card is in the deck on the Chi card. So Mr. B is He, and Mr. C is Mr. Ya.

Second question

With the first question, we identified from Mr. B and Mr. C the one who is definitely not a cunning person. The second question needs to be asked specifically to this islander, who is not a cunning one. In both cases, the question will be the same and will be very similar to the previous one: “Is your card in the deck on Mr. A’s card?” As we saw above, the answer to this question shows us among the two islanders (those to whom the current question is not addressed) one who is definitely not Mr. He. To be specific, consider the case when the question is asked by B. Then either A or B is not Xi. Let B. Then Hee is Mr. A. Similarly, in all possible cases we recognize who Mr. Hee is.

Third question

So, with the previous two questions we found Mr. He among the islanders. As in solving problem 2, let’s ask any of the two remaining inhabitants of the island the question “indicator” of knights and liars “Ya - is it Yes?” and thereby find out who is who.
