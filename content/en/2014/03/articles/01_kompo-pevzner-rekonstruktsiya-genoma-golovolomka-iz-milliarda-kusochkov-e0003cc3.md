Genome reconstruction: a billion-piece puzzle

F. COMPO, P. PEVZNER

MODERN BIOLOGY HAS NOT YET LEARNED TO READ nucleotide sequences in large DNA molecules letter by letter. Instead, scientists decipher sequences of short (about 100 to 300 nucleotides long) pieces of DNA called reads, without having any idea where in the genome each piece was cut from. The process of assembling a genome from such reads, scientifically called DNA sequencing, is akin to trying to assemble a puzzle with a billion pieces. This is a serious mathematical problem, and we will tell the fascinating story of its mathematical and biological aspects.

DNA Sequencing - Introduction

DNA sequencing and the overlapping chunks problem

Imagine that an entire circulation of a newspaper was lying in one box, and then this box exploded. Let's assume that the newspapers did not burn, as would happen in real life, but scattered into small pieces like confetti (Fig.1). Natural question:

⟦column⟧

Is it possible to reconstruct from these scraps what was written in the newspaper? The task is more interesting than it might seem at first glance - it is not just a matter of gluing scraps together, as if they were pieces of a puzzle. Firstly, we could very well have lost some of the scraps. Secondly, there were many copies of the newspaper in the chest, and therefore some scraps may overlap and contain pieces of the same phrases. Thus, the newspaper reconstruction problem can be called the overlapping pieces problem. Although our analogy with newspaper scraps is a bit far-fetched, the task of reconstructing the text of a newspaper is inherently similar to the task of assembling DNA from fragments. Let us recall that DNA can be thought of as a chain of links (nucleotides) of four types, designated by the letters A, T, G and C. Unfortunately, the technology that would allow us to read the entire genome sequentially, nucleotide by nucleotide, has not yet been invented. However, scientists have learned to determine the sequence of nucleotides in short pieces of DNA. Currently, the most common technologies deal with reads that are only 100–300 nucleotides long (Fig.2). Many reads are read from multiple copies of the same genome, creating a giant puzzle (in which the pieces may overlap). For example, a mammalian genome 3 billion nucleotides long would require assembling a puzzle of a billion pieces, which seems to be a record for puzzles of this kind.

Thus, the task of genome sequencing comes down to reading (i.e., determining the sequence) of reads (biological part) and assembling fragments (mathematical part). The first problem has a long and complicated history dating back to the 70s, when Walter Hilbert and Fred Sanger received the Nobel Prize for inventing the first method of reading reads. In the early 1990s, modern sequencers (machines for identifying pieces of DNA sequence) came onto the market, and from then on a new era began for the task of sequencing. In 2000, several hundred of these machines, working nonstop for more than a year, read a sufficiently large number of reads to possibly determine the sequence of the human genome. Assembling this genome required several months of work on some of the most powerful supercomputers of the time.

A little later we will talk in some detail about how reads are read, but for now we will focus on the computational task of assembling fragments - how, using the obtained reads, we can determine the sequence of the entire genome. Although both the recovery of the newspaper and the assembly of the fragments come down to putting together a puzzle, the task of assembly is much more complex, and not only because of its gigantic scale (recall that we may need to process a billion reads). Firstly, the newspaper is written in some understandable language. Its rules will tell us whether any two passages can be adjacent, regardless of whether they overlap or not (Fig.3,a). At the same time, the rules of DNA “language” are largely unknown to biologists, and based on sequence alone, it is almost impossible to understand whether two non-overlapping reads can be located close or far from each other. The second difficulty in assembling fragments is that the alphabet of DNA sequences consists of only 4 letters: A, T, G and C. Working with a small alphabet complicates the restoration of the original sequence, since a larger number of intersections will be caused by random reasons (Fig.3,b). Thirdly, any DNA sequence contains many “conserved regions” - nucleotide “words” that are repeated many times in a row with virtually no changes. For example, in the human genome, the so-called Alu repeat sequence (approximately 300 nucleotides long) occurs over a million times, with only a few nucleotides changed in each occurrence. Euler and Hamiltonian cycles

We will now generalize our two historical problems to all possible graphs. Suppose we have a certain graph, which we will denote by G, and imagine that an ant sits on some vertex in G. Just like the residents of Königsberg, who walked on bridges between different parts of the city, an ant can crawl along the edges from peak to peak. If the ant returns to where it started, the result of its walk will be a cycle on G. We ask two questions about cycles on G: 1. Is there a cycle on G such that the ant crawls along each edge exactly once? 2. Is there a cycle on G such that an ant visits each vertex exactly once? The first question is called the Euler Cycle Problem (ECP) - note that the solution to the ECP when our graph is K corresponds to the solution to the Königsberg Bridges problem. For each vertex v in a directed graph G, we define the in-degree of a vertex v as the number of edges going into v, and the out-degree of v as the number of edges going out of v. We are now ready to formulate Euler's result as applied to directed graphs. Euler's second theorem. The presence of an Euler cycle in an oriented graph G is equivalent to the fact that each vertex of G has equal incoming and outgoing degrees (recall that the graph is assumed to be connected). A proof of Euler's second theorem is given at the end of this article, along with a discussion of how one can find an Euler cycle fairly "fast" in the sense of computer theory. The main idea is that we do not need to check all possible cycles in a directed graph G one by one in order to understand whether G contains an Eulerian cycle. It is enough just to count the incoming and outgoing degrees of all vertices: if each vertex has the same incoming and outgoing degrees, then an Euler cycle can be easily constructed; if there is at least one vertex whose incoming and outgoing degrees are different, then it is impossible to carry out an Euler cycle.

Inspired by Euler's theorem, we can ask whether a similar simple result exists for solving the GZ. But despite the fact that winning the “Icosahedral Game” is not so difficult, the solution to the GC for an arbitrary graph remains unknown. The main difficulty is that if we have Euler’s theorem for solving the ZEC, then for the ZGZ a similar simple condition is unknown. Of course, you can always use the “brute force” method to solve the ZGC, i.e. program the computer to iterate through all the paths in the graph and report as soon as it finds a Hamiltonian cycle. This method is quite easy to understand, but imagine a huge graph that does not have a Hamiltonian cycle. For this graph, the computer will have to check every path along it before telling you that there is no Hamiltonian path. The catastrophic flaw of this method is that in the average graph of just a thousand vertices there are more different paths than there are atoms in the universe! ZGZ was one of the first algorithmic problems that even the most brilliant scientists could not solve. After many years of fruitless efforts, computer scientists began to wonder whether the ZGC was effectively decidable. In other words, could it be that their failure to find a fast algorithm is caused not by a lack of intelligence, but by the fact that an effective algorithm for solving the ZGC simply does not exist. Moreover, in the 1970s, computer scientists discovered thousands more different algorithmic problems with the same fate as ZGC: despite the fact that they looked extremely simple, no one could come up with an effective solution for them. ZGCs, together with many of these problems, are now called $«NP-полными»^{2}$ problems. Further adding to the disappointment caused by the failure of all attempts to find a simple condition for the solvability of the GZ, is the following fact: although all NP-complete problems are different, it turns out that they are all equivalent to each other - if you find a fast algorithm for solving one such problem, then it will automatically result in a fast algorithm for all the others! The problem of efficiently solving NP-complete problems (or conclusively proving that such a solution does not exist) is so fundamental to computer science and mathematics that it was included in the Clay Mathematics Institute's "Millennium Challenge" list, published in 2000: find an efficient algorithm for some NP-complete problem, or prove that there is none, and the institute will award you a million-dollar prize. The vertices of a certain graph are divided into three sets A, B, C so that the vertices from set A are not connected by edges to the vertices from set C. It is known that if all vertices of set C are removed from the graph (together with outgoing edges), then the resulting graph can be correctly colored in k colors, and if all vertices in set A are removed from it, then in n colors. What is the minimum number of colors that such a graph can be correctly colored with a guarantee?

Answer. k + n – 1. Let us show that in k + n – 1 color all vertices can always be correctly colored. Let's color B » C with n colors, call one of these colors the first, and let D be the set of all vertices from B colored with the first color. Now let's color A » D in k colors (at the same time we recolor the vertices from the set D), using the first color as one of them (and taking the remaining k – 1 colors different from the n colors already used). We keep the same coloring for vertices not included in A » D. Since no vertices from A » D are connected by edges with vertices from C colored in the first color (from D – due to the correct coloring of B » C in n colors, from A – by condition), we obtain a correct coloring of the graph in k + n – 1 color. Now we will show that fewer colors may not be enough. Let's arrange kn points in the form of a rectangle of n rows and k columns. Let's take this set to be B. Add a zero row of k points at the bottom, take it to be A, add a zero column to the left, take it to be C. Using edges in B, connect any two points that do not lie in any row or column. In A we connect every two points with edges, and in C too. In addition, we connect by edges each point from A with all points from B that do not lie in the same column with it, and each point in C with all points from B that do not lie in the same row. The correct colorings of A » B in k colors (in columns) and C » B in n colors (in rows) are obvious. Let us show that the resulting graph cannot be colored in less than k + n – 1 color. Let's say there is a regular coloring A » B » C in k + n – 2 colors. Since the points of set A are colored in k different colors, and the points of set C are colored in n different colors, then there are $m \ge 2$ colors (let’s call them common) in which the points of both of these sets are colored. Let us mark the rows and columns in which such points are located; at their intersection there are $m^{2}$ points of the set B.
