Levitating magnetic top

A.PANOV

Let's define levitation as the free suspension of bodies in the air. We will consider exclusively magnetic levitation. Indeed, magnets seem to be suitable candidates. To make a body levitate, you need to somehow compensate for gravity. And magnets facing each other with like poles repel. If you place them at a certain distance above each other, then the magnetic repulsion will just be able to balance the force of gravity, and the upper magnet will hover above the lower one. Many tried to act in a similar way, but nothing worked. The magnets somehow twisted in the air, turned out to be opposite poles facing each other and were attracted. And that was the end of it - no levitation. Replacing the repelling magnet with a whole system of magnets located on a plane or in space does not help either - and in this case it is not possible to achieve hovering. It seemed that levitation was impossible. And there was powerful theoretical confirmation of this.

Earnshaw's theorem - the impossibility of levitation

Let’s start with a double Geim-Berry quote from Andrei Geim’s article “Magnetism for Everyone” (http:// www.physics.umd.edu/grt/taj/411c/Everyones Magnetism.pdf, http://www.scientific.ru/journal/ translations/magn_ed.html): “Try, for example, to make a magnetic body levitate. You can, of course, easily lift it with a horseshoe magnet, but you can't make it hang with any clever configuration of magnets. To understand what's going on here, we need to remember Earnshaw's theorem, which (as recently reformulated by Michael Berry) states that no stationary object made of charges, magnets and masses can be suspended in space by any fixed combination of electric, magnetic and gravitational forces." This theorem was proven by the English priest, mathematician and physicist Samuel Earnshaw in 1842. It has a simple formulation, a beautiful proof, and is well known to every physicist. Many physicists considered it an absolute ban on magnetic levitation, but it turns out that there are workarounds.

A simple experiment Let's start with a simple experiment, which at first glance is nothing more than a parody of magnetic levitation. At figure 1 you can see two magnets strung on a wooden rod - one is neodymium, the other is ferrite, but this is not so important yet. The main thing is that the magnets are turned towards each other with like poles and repel each other - the upper one hangs above the lower one. Why can't this be called levitation? But because this hanging is not free - the free movement of the upper magnet is prevented by a wooden stick. Is there a contradiction here with Earnshaw's theorem? No, because except for Fig.1. Let's continue the analysis of our experiment. The height at which the top magnet hangs is the height at which the gravity and magnetic forces acting on it are balanced. We will not remove the top magnet from the stick, but will place it at this height strictly horizontally and release it. It is easy to see that in this case the potential energy of the magnet will decrease not due to vertical fall, but due to its rotation in the magnetic field. If it were not for the wooden stick, the upper magnet would turn $180\infty$ - with its opposite pole to the lower magnet - and be attracted to it. The wand prevents this. If it were possible to replace the stick with something less material, but also preventing rotation, then perhaps the problem of levitation would be solved. It turns out that the gyroscopic effect can help with this. It is the twisting of the ring magnet around the axis that can prevent it from tipping over and further falling.
