Unusual auxiliary graphs

Typical situations that are easily described in the language of graphs are clear: these are cities and the roads (airlines) connecting them, people meeting in a certain company... Next, we will consider problems in which the use of graphs (and specifically the connection theorem) is not so obvious, but very productive. A hint of the use of a graph in solving a particular problem can be connections between pairs of objects or even just a selected set of pairs. Problem 3 (based on the problem by I. Raskina). There are m fans: some of them (perhaps all or none) support Spartak, and the rest support Dynamo. You are allowed to ask any two people whether they support different teams, and they will honestly answer “yes” or “no.” It is required to put fans on two buses so that each contains fans of only one team. In what minimum number of questions can this be done? Answer: m – 1. Solution. Example. If you ask questions to the first and second, first and third, ..., first and m, then it will be easy to seat: those who answered “yes” are put on the bus with the first, and the rest - on another bus. Grade. Now consider a situation where no more than m – 2 questions were asked. Let's build a question graph: the vertices correspond to fans, and the edges connect pairs of fans who were asked the question. Our graph has m vertices and no more than m – 2 edges, which means that by the connection theorem it is not connected. But is it possible to understand from an unconnected graph how to correctly divide fans? Let's consider one of the connected components A. The set A is divided into two subsets A¢ and A¢¢ (Spartak fans and Dynamo fans, respectively). Similarly, the connected component B is divided into subsets B¢ and B¢¢. Fans from the set A¢ need to be put on one of the buses, but it is not known which of the subsets B¢ and B¢¢ should be added to them. In fact, if the set B¢ turned out to be fans of “Dynamo”, and B¢¢ - of “Spartak”, they would give exactly the same answers to all the questions asked of them

Let's look at a few more problems about checkered figures. In the next task, as in task 2, a graph illustrating the proximity of cells will help us. Problem 4. A polygon with an area of ​​n cells is drawn on checkered paper. Its outline follows the grid lines. What is the largest perimeter of the polygon? (The side of the cell is 1.) Answer: 2n + 2. Solution. Example. Rectangle n ¥ 1. Estimate. The sum of the perimeters of n cells is 4n. From these perimeters, the perimeter of the polygon and the double sum of the lengths of the grid segments inside the polygon are added up: after all, cells are adjacent to each internal segment on both sides. To estimate the total length of the internal segments, we build a graph: the vertices are the cells of the rectangle, the edges connect the cells with a common side. This graph is connected: the rook can travel freely between all cells inside the polygon. According to the theorem, the graph has at least n – 1 edges, i.e. the total length of the internal segments is at least n – 1. But then the perimeter of the polygon is no more than 4 n - 2 ( n - 1 ) = 2 n + 2. Comment. Of course, this estimate can also be proven by induction on the number of cells. But an inductive statement must be formulated skillfully. In the math circle classes, many schoolchildren tried to rely on the seemingly obvious (and in fact close to exercise 3) statement: “a polygon from the (k + 1) th cell can be obtained by adding one cell to some polygon from k cells,” but they were never able to prove it... Note that in many problems a more general construction of a “dual graph” is useful, when regions in some “map” on the plane are declared vertices, and if two vertex regions have a common part of the border, then they are connected by an edge.
