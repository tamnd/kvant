Graph connectivity theorem

What do the solutions to problems 1 and 2 have in common? Both here and there there were disparate objects (balls, cells), which we combined into groups (areas), and the key to the solution was the idea of ​​​​tracking the number of such groups (areas). In both problems there are connections between pairs of objects: an edge of a cube connects a pair of vertices, a match serves as a boundary between a pair of neighboring cells. The connections here are very clear, and this makes them easier to count. In order to avoid repeating the same reasoning in different situations, if possible, mathematicians introduce general concepts. Structures in which there are connections between pairs of objects are called graphs. A visual representation of a graph is as follows: a system of points is depicted - the vertices of the graph, where some pairs of vertices are connected by lines - the edges of the graph. In this case, it does not matter where exactly the vertices are located on the plane, or what exactly the edges look like - in the form of segments or curves. When depicting a graph, we also do not pay attention to possible intersections of edges at points other than vertices. You can make a graph model from buttons-vertices and threads-edges. By laying out such a model on the table or confusing it in any way, we do not change the graph. In a model made of buttons and threads, connected pieces - components - are clearly visible: the model can be divided into such components without breaking the threads. Let's talk more strictly about connectivity in graphs. A graph is called connected if from any of its vertices one can reach any other vertex by moving along the edges (i.e., moving several times from vertex to vertex along an edge). Let a vertex A be fixed in an arbitrary graph. Let us call the set of vertices that can be reached from a given vertex A by moving along the edges the connected component of the vertex A and denote it by K ( A ). In this case, it is assumed that the vertex A itself is included in the component K(A). The set of all vertices of the graph is divided into connected components (in particular, the set of vertices of a connected graph represents one component). figure 6 shows an example of a graph that has only 4 connected components. Let us pay attention to the fact that a component can also consist of one isolated vertex. Let us formulate an important and frequently used connection theorem. Theorem. 1. Let a connected graph with n vertices be given. Then it has at least n – 1 edges. 2. Let a graph with n vertices split into k connected components. Then it has at least n – k edges. Proof. (It actually reproduces the reasoning from the solutions to the problems we have already analyzed.) Let’s erase all the edges in the graph. Then all its vertices will become isolated, i.e. the graph will split into n connected components. Let's start restoring the ribs one by one. Each drawn edge either connects two connected components into one, or does not change the partition into components in any way. Thus, restoring an edge can reduce the number of components by no more than 1. Therefore, to reduce the number of components from n to k, it will be necessary to carry out at least n – k edges. In particular, to make a graph connected (that is, to make k = 1), it is necessary to draw at least n – 1 edges.
