Amazing Properties of Electrons

A. SERGEEV

The main thing for them is to come up with a name. Until I came up with it - it’s a pity to look at him, he’s a fool. A SEVERAL YEARS AGO, THEORETICAL PHYSICISTS predicted the existence of unusual substances – topological insulators. A little later they were discovered experimentally. These materials have a whole range of amazing properties, a detailed description of which would take dozens of pages. Therefore, today we will talk about only one, but in a sense the main, property that has become the hallmark of topological insulators: in the thickness of a substance, a topological insulator is a dielectric, and its surface conducts electric current. In other words, electrons move along the surface of a dielectric as freely as in a metal. The reason for such strange behavior of electrons is the prohibition of reflection back. This prohibition, in turn, has deep mathematical premises (it is better not to wonder where the mathematical premises themselves come from). In this article we will try to figure out why an electron sliding along the surface of a topological insulator, having encountered an obstacle, cannot rebound in the opposite direction. To do this, let's look at several loosely related stories from different areas of physics and mathematics. Look closely at each piece of this puzzle and you will see how they fit together to form a complete picture. For experiments - where would we be without them? – you will need a sheet of paper, scissors, a pencil, a globe without a stand, a mug with water and an imaginary balloon.

Foucault pendulum (Low-cost experiment on a global scale)

Let's start from afar. You may have seen a Foucault pendulum. A heavy ball, suspended on a cable going somewhere up, swings slowly and intimidatingly, with each swing passing closer and closer to a wooden plank, the sound of which falling on the marble floor becomes tangible for the audience

proof of the rotation of the Earth. But the proof of a far from obvious connection between the rotation of the plane of oscillation of a pendulum with the rotation of the planet, as a rule, remains behind the scenes of a spectacular experiment. As well as the fact that the plane of oscillation of a Foucault pendulum will not rotate at any point on the globe - at the equator, for example, this will not happen. And at the poles, the speed of rotation of the plane of oscillation will be the highest - within a day it will rotate 360 ​​degrees. Do you want to know how to explain the rotation of the plane of oscillation of a Foucault pendulum without a single formula and, moreover, study the angular dependence of the speed of this rotation on geographic latitude? Then cut a circle out of paper and draw a series of parallel vectors along its edge, as shown in figure 1 on the left.

Then make a cut along the radius and bend a certain sector of the circle. Now close the edges of the empty sector - you will get a cone, along the edge of which there are parallel vectors. However, no, now they are not so parallel! When walking along the base of the cone, the vector rotates. It is easy to see that the angle of rotation is equal to the angle of the empty sector of the cone scan. Now, if the piece of paper was large enough or if your globe is small enough, you can put the cone on the globe so that it touches the globe along some parallel (see Fig. 1 on the right). The angle of rotation of the vector drawn on the cone will be equal to (minus $2\pi )$ the angle by which the plane of the Foucault pendulum located on this parallel will rotate during the day. On another parallel, the angle at the vertex of the tangent cone will be different, which means the angle of rotation will also change. parallel corresponding to $30^\circ$ north latitude, then the vector will rotate by $\pi$ radians. We have just illustrated the mathematical concept of parallel vector translation on a curved surface. Its essence is to move the vector “as parallel to itself as possible”, provided that the vector must always lie in the tangent plane to the surface on which it lives. In other words, if you get so close to a curved surface that it appears flat, the vectors must be parallel in the normal sense of the word. This is what we see when we unfold the cone on a plane. At a point lying on the parallel - on the circle along which the cone touches the sphere - the cone and the sphere have a common tangent plane. This means that the parallel transfer of a vector along a given circle on a sphere can be studied using a cone. But, it would seem, what is the connection between the vectors on the cone and the Foucault pendulum? To answer, you need to remember about angular momentum and the law of its conservation. Let's say we have a gyroscope in a gimbal that allows it to freely change the direction of its axis. Let's direct its axis to some distant star (the North Star is not suitable for us) and spin the disk. Then the angular momentum vector, perpendicular to the plane of the disk, will always be directed towards this star, due to the corresponding conservation law. This means that in a laboratory reference frame associated with the Earth's surface, the gyroscope axis will rotate following the direction of the selected star. Now we apply the law of conservation of angular momentum to the Foucault pendulum. The angular momentum vector will be directed perpendicular to the plane of oscillation of the pendulum. But, unlike a gyroscope, the angular momentum of a pendulum cannot be constantly directed towards one star, since the pendulum oscillates under the influence of gravity directed towards the Earth's center of mass. It turns out that the angular momentum vector should be directed tangentially to the earth’s surface and at the same time move “as parallel to itself as possible,” which is a consequence of the law of conservation of momentum, taking into account the imposed restrictions. Thus, for the angular momentum vector of the Foucault pendulum, transferred by the rotation of the Earth along the surface of the sphere, the condition of parallel transfer is satisfied. Indeed, according to the law of conservation of angular momentum, the pendulum “wants” to oscillate all the time in one plane. However, due to the fact that the force of gravity is directed towards the center of the Earth, the normal vector to the plane of oscillation must always be directed tangentially to the earth's surface. In other words, we are talking about parallel vector transfer on a sphere. It follows from this that a paper cone with drawn vectors is not a “simplified model” or a “fit to answer”: the curvature of the Earth’s surface is the direct cause of the rotation of the plane in which the Foucault pendulum oscillates. In quantum mechanics, the rotation of a vector during parallel transfer is called the Berry phase, named after

Topology (How to determine the shape of a planet)

Do you know the fundamental difference between a bun and a bagel? When you eat a bagel, there is nothing left, and when you eat a bagel, there is a hole left. This kind of difference is what topology deals with. Speaking a little more strictly, topology is a part of mathematics that studies the properties of geometric objects that do not change under smooth deformations. In other words, the object can be bent, crushed, bent, but it cannot be cut and glued different parts. It would seem that if you take any geometric drawing and allow yourself to mentally bend all these segments, circles, straight lines - what will remain of the geometry? The circle will cease to be a circle, the straight line will become a curve, and all possible theorems will be violated. However, there are properties of shapes that will survive these destructive transformations. And the joke about the donut hole is only part of the joke. To verify this, you will need an imaginary balloon. Imagine that you are squeezing it from diametrically opposite points, squeezing it with two fingers (Fig.2). At some point

When you open your eyes again, you will find that now you are no longer holding a balloon, but an excellent bicycle tube. From a topological point of view, a balloon is a sphere, and the surface of a donut is a torus. We have just performed a “glue-break” transformation, which led to a change in the topological invariant - the number of holes: a sphere has no holes, but a torus has one. Topological insulators (Are you still boiling?)

Have you forgotten what this article is about yet? That's right, topological insulators. There's just one last effort left to put all the pieces of the story together. Or maybe they have already formed in your mind? If not, then a little more attention. We can say that a topological insulator differs from a conventional dielectric by the shape of the planet in momentum space on which the electron lives. Let's conventionally depict it in the form of a sphere for any ordinary dielectric (including vacuum) and in the form of a torus for a topological insulator (Fig.7).

⟦figure⟧

Fig.7

Moreover, knowing the curvature of this planet at each point, we can calculate the topological invariant, the so-called Chern index n, very similar to the type of surface - the number of holes. For a topological insulator the Chern index is non-zero, in our example n = 1, while for an ordinary dielectric n = 0. Let us now look at the boundary between an ordinary dielectric and a vacuum. On it, the electron planet should have, on our conditional figure, an intermediate shape between its sphere and a sphere of a different radius, i.e. be a sphere. But at the boundary between a vacuum and a topological insulator, something terrible must happen. The electron planet must be something between a sphere and a torus, i.e., as you already understood, a nitonisoid! This is as inevitable as the fact that a continuous function that takes a negative value at the left end of the segment and positive at the right must go to zero somewhere on this segment. So, we see that an electron sliding along the boundary between a topological insulator and a vacuum (or any other ordinary dielectric - air, for example) lives in momentum space on the surface of a nitonisoid. Now imagine that such an electron collides with an obstacle. To bounce back, it must reverse the direction of the momentum. But this means that it must move along the surface of the nitonisoid to a point opposite to the one in which it is now located.

Since the nitonisoid is curved, during this movement the electron will acquire the Berry phase (remember the Foucault pendulum). It will be equal in modulus to $\pi$. The sign of the Berry phase will depend on the path that the electron chooses, since there are two ways to get to the opposite point, just like on the globe. One trajectory will correspond to a clockwise reversal of the impulse, and the other will correspond to a counterclockwise reversal. Which option will the electron choose? That's right, both. This means that the momentum of one part of the electron will unfold by $180^\circ$, and the momentum of the other by $- 180 ^\circ$. In other words, to get one part of an electron from another, you need to expand its momentum by the entire $360^\circ$. Note that the direction of the electron spin in this case is strictly related to the direction of the momentum, so the spin will unfold in a similar way. And with such a spin reversal, as you remember, the electron wave will change sign, i.e. When added together, the two parts of the electron add up to zero. Consequently, an electron living on the boundary between a topological insulator and a vacuum cannot be reflected back after meeting an obstacle, which was what needed to be explained. As you can see, the world of topological insulators is beautiful and amazing. Perhaps the scientists who predicted their existence listened to the words of Dirac, delivered at a lecture on February 6, 1939: “The tendency towards the unification of mathematics and physics gives the theoretical physicist a new powerful method for studying the nature of the object of interest to him, a method that has not yet been successfully applied in practice, but, I am sure, will prove its significance in the future. The method is to start by choosing the branch of mathematics that is expected to form the basis of the new theory. In this choice, the scientist must be essentially guided by considerations of mathematical beauty.”

By the end of the last century, the more than 50-year history of particle physics was marked by the creation of a theory of “almost everything,” as the theoretical physicists themselves who created it wrote. The most basic (fundamental) “building blocks” of which atomic nuclei, atoms, molecules, substances, biological objects, people, planets, stars and galaxies are composed were identified and classified, and the laws of interaction between these “building blocks” were determined. The theory was called the Standard Model. It is possible that the origin of such a (“clumsy”, as V. Rubakov writes) name is due to the fact that it, like technological standards, includes all the ingredients necessary to create an object, and all the production operations performed. In our case, the ingredients are the “bricks”, production operations are the laws of interaction, and the object is the entire Universe. The ingredients of the Standard Model are 6 quarks, 6 leptons and 5 particles that carry the interactions of quarks and leptons between $собой.^{1}$ A puzzling question may arise - where are our usual protons, neutrons and electrons? All of them are in the Standard Model: both the proton and the neutron consist of three quarks of two types, and the electron is one of the leptons. Other leptons “walk” in huge numbers throughout the Universe. These are neutrinos (and antineutrinos), which very rarely interact with other particles, but they carry the most valuable information about the processes inside the stars where they are born. And quarks in suitable conditions, when grouped, can create not only protons and neutrons, but also many (several hundred) different short-lived particles. There was even the term “zoo” of particles. With the advent of quark concepts, all of them turned out to be not fundamental, not the main “building blocks” of the world. The five carrier particles are sometimes called "glues". One of them is directly called - gluon (from the English glue - glue). The most famous “glue” is the photon. Using his example, it is clear that carrier particles can be spoken of as field quanta. A field is an object containing possible (virtual) carrier particles. Under any influence on the field, such a particle is, as it were, born from it, becoming a real particle. For example, the process of the appearance of a photon occurs during the transition of an atomic electron from one energy state to another.

There is also other terminology associated with fields and virtual particles. When quantum carriers are born from the field, the field energy decreases. It cannot be zero due to the quantum uncertainty relation. The state with the minimum field energy is called a physical vacuum. There are as many types of this vacuum as there are types of “glues”. Note that all “glues” are bosons, and all other particles are fermions. What does this mean? The formal difference is associated with the magnitude of the internal quantum characteristic - the so-called spin (from the English spin - spin, spin). Particles have their own rotational motion. Therefore, for example, all electrically charged particles are small magnets. The intensity of this rotation does not depend on any external causes; it is the particle’s own characteristic. The angular momentum of this rotation (often called angular momentum), or more precisely, the projection of this moment onto the direction of the external influence, like other characteristics of the particle, is fixed (quantized). It is equal to sℏ $, где^{ℏ}$ – Planck’s constant, and s – the so-called spin quantum number. All quarks and leptons have s = 1/2, and carrier particles have s = 1. This difference in spins leads to a radical difference in the behavior of particles. When a certain number of quarks or leptons are in one quantum ensemble (“collective”), subject to the influence of a certain field, for example an electric one, they cannot be exactly the same. Each particle must have its own characteristic of probabilistic behavior - a wave function or a set of quantum numbers. The probability function for particle energy is called the Fermi–Dirac distribution, and such particles are called fermions. Figuratively, we can say that fermions are “individualists.” Electrons belong to fermions, and that is why the electrons of one atom or one molecule must be different (have different sets of quantum numbers). Ultimately, this leads to a huge variety of molecules, substances and biological objects. But the carrier particles in a group can be completely identical (for example, photons in a laser beam). They are “collectivists”. Their probabilistic energy characteristic has a slightly different form than that of fermions and is called the Bose–Einstein distribution. Such particles were given the name bosons. Unfortunately, it is impossible “on the fingers”, i.e. without resorting to the mathematical apparatus of quantum science, explain why such a seemingly insignificant difference in the value of the particle’s own angular momentum leads to a fundamental difference in the behavior of particles. An important role in the Standard Model is played by the principles of symmetry, understood much more broadly than the mirror symmetry or symmetry of crystalline forms that we are familiar with. The essence of these symmetries is the repeatability (immutability) of the “picture” under a certain geometric transformation. In physics, the concept of symmetry extends to any transformation (coordinates, time, internal characteristics of an object). The laws of physics do not change their essence during these transformations, and the corresponding equations do not change their form. It turned out that each type of symmetry corresponds to a certain conservation law (Emmy Noether, 1918). For example, spatial (coordinate) symmetries correspond to the law of conservation of momentum and the law of conservation of angular momentum, time symmetry - the law of conservation of mechanical energy. But there are other conservation laws - for example, the laws of conservation of electricity, barium

But let's return to superconductivity. At the end of the 19th century, in connection with the development of the world telegraph communication system, increasing the conductivity of metals became an important technical problem. Physical theory did not provide an answer to this question, because the mechanism of electrical conductivity was unknown. After the discovery of the electron, the electronic theory of conductivity began to develop. It was started in 1900 by the German physicist Paul Drude. It is remarkable that even today Drude’s theory is included in physics courses as the classical theory of electrical conductivity of metals. In the modern version of this theory, also developed by the German physicist Arnold Sommerfeld in 1928, after the creation of quantum mechanics, electrons are considered as a quantum degenerate ideal gas filling a crystal lattice, at the nodes of which there are ions of metal atoms and, occasionally, impurities. “Quantum degeneracy” of this gas, in particular, means that, unlike classical gas, its particles – electrons – even at absolute zero, are in constant chaotic motion with enormous speeds, calculated as a percentage of the speed of light. The application of an electric field leads to the superimposition of some ordered drift on this chaos - just like on a summer evening the wind blows away a cloud of midges scurrying back and forth over a lake. It is surprising that at zero temperature, electrons in their complex, chaotically directed motion, thanks to the quirks of quantum mechanics, manage to “bypass” the lattice ions. They are scattered either on impurities that replace the ions, the location of which is unknown to the electrons (this also happens at T = 0), or on ions that deviate from their equilibrium positions due to temperature fluctuations (occurring at temperatures other than zero). It is clear that in 1900 Drude could not have known all this - there were still six years left before the creation of the “pudding model”, thirteen years before Bohr’s postulates and more than a quarter of a century before the creation of quantum theory and the study on its basis of the properties of a degenerate gas of electrons and the behavior of a quantum particle in a periodic lattice potential. However, it turned out that the model of a classical ideal gas of electrons placed in an external electric field made it possible to obtain a remarkable formula for conductivity, relating the current density to the electric field strength. Following the already well-known molecular theory at that time, Drude suggested that during their movement electrons, like a set of molecules of an ideal gas, collide and in the collision processes chaotically change the direction of their momentum. The conductivity of the metal turned out to be proportional to the time between such collisions. At the same time, the nature of the collisions for the validity of the result remained not very significant - these could be, as in an ideal gas, collisions between the electrons themselves, or there could be collisions with other objects. Drude himself assumed that electrons were repelled from the cores of impenetrable ions. As already mentioned, a rigorous theory of the electrical conductivity of metals will be created in another quarter of a century, but for now scientists were arguing about the dependence of the dissipation time, and therefore conductivity, on temperature. There were three points of view. In 1907, Einstein, to explain the behavior of the heat capacity of solids at low temperatures, proposed considering the particles that form a lattice as oscillators experiencing temperature fluctuations. Therefore, the first theory, based on Einstein’s model and taking into account that at absolute zero temperatures the movement of oscillators freezes, stated that conduction

The second theory, on the contrary, stated that as the temperature decreases, the movement of electrons also stops, so the conductivity of the metal should tend to zero. Finally, taking into account the presence of impurities, on which electrons could be scattered even in the absence of thermal motion, led to the conclusion that in such a metal the conductivity remains finite even at zero temperature. In this case, in the absence of impurities, electrons have no one to collide with, and the conductivity of a pure metal should tend to infinity - its resistance to charge transfer should disappear. Kamerlingh Onnes takes part in this debate and is among those who believe that as the temperature approaches absolute zero, the resistance of a pure metal tends to zero (that is, its conductivity - the reciprocal of resistance - tends to infinity). Unlike theorists, he is a “monopolist” in the experimental study of ultra-low temperatures at that time. Moving further into the region unexplored, in 1908 he discovered and described an amazing phenomenon - at a temperature of about 2 K, the rapidly boiling surface of liquid helium suddenly calms down, and calm sets in. Almost thirty years later, in 1937, the properties of this unusual state of liquid helium were studied by the Soviet physicist Pyotr Kapitsa. He discovered that at a temperature of 2.18 K the viscosity of liquid helium abruptly drops to zero, in any case, taking into account the accuracy of the measurements, it decreases by hundreds. thousand times. Soon, Soviet theorist Lev Landau gives an explanation for the “quantum calm” observed by Kamerlingh Onnes - liquid helium at this point transforms into a new state, billions of its atoms begin to live as one organism, they are described by a single wave function and their behavior resembles an electron in a Bohr orbit in an atom. So, having learned to work with liquid helium and having at his disposal temperatures that are only degrees different from absolute zero, Kamerlingh Onnes begins to study the behavior of pure metals in this temperature range. And he begins with noble metals - gold, platinum, silver, excellent conductors of electric current. However, studying the temperature dependences of the resistance of gold, silver and platinum samples, Kamerlingh Onnes discovers that all of them, decreasing linearly with decreasing temperature away from absolute zero, “reach saturation”, i.e. reach a certain fixed value at low temperatures accessible to it. He does not find the required zero resistance. Next in line was mercury, perhaps because mercury is a metal that remains liquid even at room temperature.
