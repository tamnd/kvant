But let's return to superconductivity. At the end of the 19th century, in connection with the development of the world telegraph communication system, increasing the conductivity of metals became an important technical problem. Physical theory did not provide an answer to this question, because the mechanism of electrical conductivity was unknown. After the discovery of the electron, the electronic theory of conductivity began to develop. It was started in 1900 by the German physicist Paul Drude. It is remarkable that even today Drude’s theory is included in physics courses as the classical theory of electrical conductivity of metals. In the modern version of this theory, also developed by the German physicist Arnold Sommerfeld in 1928, after the creation of quantum mechanics, electrons are considered as a quantum degenerate ideal gas filling a crystal lattice, at the nodes of which there are ions of metal atoms and, occasionally, impurities. “Quantum degeneracy” of this gas, in particular, means that, unlike classical gas, its particles – electrons – even at absolute zero, are in constant chaotic motion with enormous speeds, calculated as a percentage of the speed of light. The application of an electric field leads to the superimposition of some ordered drift on this chaos - just like on a summer evening the wind blows away a cloud of midges scurrying back and forth over a lake. It is surprising that at zero temperature, electrons in their complex, chaotically directed motion, thanks to the quirks of quantum mechanics, manage to “bypass” the lattice ions. They are scattered either on impurities that replace the ions, the location of which is unknown to the electrons (this also happens at T = 0), or on ions that deviate from their equilibrium positions due to temperature fluctuations (occurring at temperatures other than zero). It is clear that in 1900 Drude could not have known all this - there were still six years left before the creation of the “pudding model”, thirteen years before Bohr’s postulates and more than a quarter of a century before the creation of quantum theory and the study on its basis of the properties of a degenerate gas of electrons and the behavior of a quantum particle in a periodic lattice potential. However, it turned out that the model of a classical ideal gas of electrons placed in an external electric field made it possible to obtain a remarkable formula for conductivity, relating the current density to the electric field strength. Following the already well-known molecular theory at that time, Drude suggested that during their movement electrons, like a set of molecules of an ideal gas, collide and in the collision processes chaotically change the direction of their momentum. The conductivity of the metal turned out to be proportional to the time between such collisions. At the same time, the nature of the collisions for the validity of the result remained not very significant - these could be, as in an ideal gas, collisions between the electrons themselves, or there could be collisions with other objects. Drude himself assumed that electrons were repelled from the cores of impenetrable ions. As already mentioned, a rigorous theory of the electrical conductivity of metals will be created in another quarter of a century, but for now scientists were arguing about the dependence of the dissipation time, and therefore conductivity, on temperature. There were three points of view. In 1907, Einstein, to explain the behavior of the heat capacity of solids at low temperatures, proposed considering the particles that form a lattice as oscillators experiencing temperature fluctuations. Therefore, the first theory, based on Einstein’s model and taking into account that at absolute zero temperatures the movement of oscillators freezes, stated that conduction

The strength of a metal at ultra-low temperatures should tend to infinity. The second theory, on the contrary, stated that as the temperature decreases, the movement of electrons also stops, so the conductivity of the metal should tend to zero. Finally, taking into account the presence of impurities, on which electrons could be scattered even in the absence of thermal motion, led to the conclusion that in such a metal the conductivity remains finite even at zero temperature. In this case, in the absence of impurities, electrons have no one to collide with, and the conductivity of a pure metal should tend to infinity - its resistance to charge transfer should disappear. Kamerlingh Onnes takes part in this debate and is among those who believe that as the temperature approaches absolute zero, the resistance of a pure metal tends to zero (that is, its conductivity - the reciprocal of resistance - tends to infinity). Unlike theorists, he is a “monopolist” in the experimental study of ultra-low temperatures at that time. Improving the cascade liquefaction method proposed earlier by Dewar, $газов,^{2}$ Kamerlingh Onnes in 1906 liquefied helium - the last of the elements of the periodic table, which remained gaseous at normal pressure up to a temperature of 4.2 K (almost $- 269 ^\circ C ).$ It was Kamerlingh Onnes, as a result of careful and lengthy measurements, that determined the boiling point of helium. Moving further into the region unexplored, in 1908 he discovered and described an amazing phenomenon - at a temperature of about 2 K, the rapidly boiling surface of liquid helium suddenly calms down, and calm sets in. Almost thirty years later, in 1937, the properties of this unusual state of liquid helium were studied by the Soviet physicist Pyotr Kapitsa. He discovered that at a temperature of 2.18 K the viscosity of liquid helium abruptly drops to zero, in any case, taking into account the accuracy of the measurements, it decreases by hundreds. thousand times. Soon, Soviet theorist Lev Landau gives an explanation for the “quantum calm” observed by Kamerlingh Onnes - liquid helium at this point transforms into a new state, billions of its atoms begin to live as one organism, they are described by a single wave function and their behavior resembles an electron in a Bohr orbit in an atom. So, having learned to work with liquid helium and having at his disposal temperatures that are only degrees different from absolute zero, Kamerlingh Onnes begins to study the behavior of pure metals in this temperature range. And he begins with noble metals - gold, platinum, silver, excellent conductors of electric current. However, studying the temperature dependences of the resistance of gold, silver and platinum samples, Kamerlingh Onnes discovers that all of them, decreasing linearly with decreasing temperature away from absolute zero, “reach saturation”, i.e. reach a certain fixed value at low temperatures accessible to it. He does not find the required zero resistance. Next in line was mercury, perhaps because mercury is a metal that remains liquid even at room temperature.

2 The cascade method of gas liquefaction is based on a simple idea. When pumping out the vapor phase of a substance in contact with its liquid phase, fast molecules escaping from the liquid are carried away by the pump from the volume of coexistence of both phases. As a result, the average kinetic energy, and therefore the temperature of the liquid, decreases. By correctly selecting a set of gases with successively decreasing boiling points and forcing them to boil in turn by pumping, it is possible to achieve temperatures up to 1 K.
