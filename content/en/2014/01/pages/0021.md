The detectors are mainly artificially grown transparent single crystals, which seem to flare up when a fast particle passes through them (the Vavilov–Cherenkov effect). The flash is captured by a photomultiplier tube and converted into an electronic signal. The signal enters the electronic circuit and becomes digital information. Information is sent via fiber optic cables to the computer shop. There is no other way to describe this room, where there are several thousand multi-gigabyte processors. The first task of computers is to filter out information about known and studied events. And only the squeezed-out information (less than 0.001 of the received information) is transmitted further. Through a special international information network, information is transmitted to laboratories in 25 countries. For insurance purposes, this information is transmitted not only in the usual way through space

Laboratory ATLAS transmission network, but also through fiber optic networks specially laid for this purpose, including along the bottom of seas and oceans. In the formulation of the Nobel Prize, two BA-Kov laboratories are noted - ATLAS and CMS.

Huge superconducting solenoids bend the flight paths of particles formed during collisions and direct them to sensors that record the energy of the particles. The mass of magnets and other devices is 7,000 tons. The laboratory was designed, participated in its construction, and now its work is monitored by 2,900 scientists and engineers from 37 countries, including 200 scientists from Russia and the CIS countries. This is Russia's main participation in the construction and operation of $БАКа.^{2}$ The second laboratory of the LHC, which took part in experiments on the Higgs boson, is abbreviated as CMS (from the English Compact Muon Solenoid). In principle, this laboratory is similar to ATLAS. It is slightly smaller, “more compact”, although it also has impressive dimensions - a cylinder with a length of 22 m and a diameter of 15 m. The magnetic field of this solenoid has a value of 4 Tesla. The laboratory is designed to identify many particles, including the Higgs boson, or more precisely, its decay products - muons and antimuons, which is why the word “muon” is included in its name. For this laboratory, the Kharkov plant in Ukraine grew crystal detectors (sodium and cesium iodides activated by thallium), and the Moscow plant controlled their quality. What is happening at the LHC now? In February 2013, it was stopped in order to carry out preparatory work for launching at full particle energy - 14,000 GeV. The stronger magnetic fields required to keep accelerated particles on their trajectory require greater reliability of all mechanical connections. Hundreds of engineers and technicians are currently working along the entire 27-kilometer ring installing new solenoid fasteners. The work will continue for another year. According to the plan, the LHC will be turned on at full capacity at the end of 2014. And in all scientific institutions that have received the results of experiments already conducted, work is underway to process these results. The enormous merits of the creators of the LHC did not go unrewarded. The Fundamental Physics Prize of the Russian billionaire Yuri Milman awarded this prize (three million euros) to a team of 7 people, project leaders, back in March 2013. Therefore, the Nobel Committee only has to honor theoretical physicists.
