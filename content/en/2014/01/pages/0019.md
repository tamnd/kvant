There is also other terminology associated with fields and virtual particles. When quantum carriers are born from the field, the field energy decreases. It cannot be zero due to the quantum uncertainty relation. The state with the minimum field energy is called a physical vacuum. There are as many types of this vacuum as there are types of “glues”. Note that all “glues” are bosons, and all other particles are fermions. What does this mean? The formal difference is associated with the magnitude of the internal quantum characteristic - the so-called spin (from the English spin - spin, spin). Particles have their own rotational motion. Therefore, for example, all electrically charged particles are small magnets. The intensity of this rotation does not depend on any external causes; it is the particle’s own characteristic. The angular momentum of this rotation (often called angular momentum), or more precisely, the projection of this moment onto the direction of the external influence, like other characteristics of the particle, is fixed (quantized). It is equal to sℏ $, где^{ℏ}$ – Planck’s constant, and s – the so-called spin quantum number. All quarks and leptons have s = 1/2, and carrier particles have s = 1. This difference in spins leads to a radical difference in the behavior of particles. When a certain number of quarks or leptons are in one quantum ensemble (“collective”), subject to the influence of a certain field, for example an electric one, they cannot be exactly the same. Each particle must have its own characteristic of probabilistic behavior - a wave function or a set of quantum numbers. The probability function for particle energy is called the Fermi–Dirac distribution, and such particles are called fermions. Figuratively, we can say that fermions are “individualists.” Electrons belong to fermions, and that is why the electrons of one atom or one molecule must be different (have different sets of quantum numbers). Ultimately, this leads to a huge variety of molecules, substances and biological objects. But the carrier particles in a group can be completely identical (for example, photons in a laser beam). They are “collectivists”. Their probabilistic energy characteristic has a slightly different form than that of fermions and is called the Bose–Einstein distribution. Such particles were given the name bosons. Unfortunately, it is impossible “on the fingers”, i.e. without resorting to the mathematical apparatus of quantum science, explain why such a seemingly insignificant difference in the value of the particle’s own angular momentum leads to a fundamental difference in the behavior of particles. An important role in the Standard Model is played by the principles of symmetry, understood much more broadly than the mirror symmetry or symmetry of crystalline forms that we are familiar with. The essence of these symmetries is the repeatability (immutability) of the “picture” under a certain geometric transformation. In physics, the concept of symmetry extends to any transformation (coordinates, time, internal characteristics of an object). The laws of physics do not change their essence during these transformations, and the corresponding equations do not change their form. It turned out that each type of symmetry corresponds to a certain conservation law (Emmy Noether, 1918). For example, spatial (coordinate) symmetries correspond to the law of conservation of momentum and the law of conservation of angular momentum, time symmetry - the law of conservation of mechanical energy. onic or lepton charge. Special symmetries associated with such internal characteristics of a quantum object have received a special name - “gauge invariance”. This is a special theoretical technique suitable for all types of fields. Gauge invariance makes it possible to predict and calculate the properties of elementary particles - mass, average lifetimes, decay options, etc. All properties predicted by the theory were confirmed in accelerator experiments, all predicted particles were discovered. But the Standard Model had some difficulty. In quantum electrodynamics, i.e. in the section of the Standard Model, where electromagnetic interactions are considered, the experimental fact of the absence of mass in photons - the carriers of this interaction - had a theoretical justification. It was closely related to the internal symmetry of electrodynamics, which leads to the conservation of electric charge. The spin (spin number) of the photon should be equal to 1. A similar picture is observed in quantum chromodynamics - that part of the model that deals with strong interactions of quarks. As mentioned above, the particles that carry this interaction are called gluons. They, like photons, have no mass. But the situation was worse with weak interactions, more precisely with electroweak interactions, since the creation of the Standard Model is usually associated with the development of a unified theory of electrical and weak interactions (Nobel Prize 1979 to S. Glashow, A. Salam and S. Weinberg). No special terms have been invented for the carriers of these interactions. They are called, somewhat informally, vector $W^{\pm}$ -bosons and Z-bosons. According to all the canons of gauge invariance, having a spin equal to 1, they should have been massless. But experiments (Nobel Prize 1984 to K. Rubbia and S. van der Meer) irrefutably showed the presence of masses in these bosons. And not small masses by the standards of the microcosm - but 80–90 times more than that of a proton. This means that some symmetries in the world of elementary particles may be violated. And then the creators of the Standard Model remembered the hypothesis of Engler, Higgs and other physicists about a special field that could break one of the symmetries of gauge invariance so that particles interacting with it should gain mass. This field should not have dedicated directions, i.e. must be scalar, not vector. And this, in turn, means that the quantum of this field - the Higgs boson - should not have a spin. The theory also predicted other properties of the Higgs boson. For the complete triumph of the Standard Model, all that remained was to experimentally detect the presence of this boson in nature and confirm the theoretical predictions of its properties. But this turned out to be a difficult task. The theory predicted such a large mass of the Higgs boson that existing accelerators were not powerful enough to detect it. Some physicists even suggested abandoning the Standard Model and looking for new theories of particles. That’s when the Higgs boson was declared a divine particle that cannot be known. But in the scientific world, faith in the Standard Model prevailed; scientists convinced the governments of several countries that they should not spare money on the construction of new accelerators. Since this was a lot of money, the United States was the first to succeed. A new accelerator, the proton-antiproton collider, launched in 1983 and designed for an energy of 1000 GeV, searched for the Higgs boson. In 2002, it even seemed that he had been discovered. But a thorough check revealed that this was not the case.
