We have just illustrated the mathematical concept of parallel vector translation on a curved surface. Its essence is to move the vector “as parallel to itself as possible”, provided that the vector must always lie in the tangent plane to the surface on which it lives. In other words, if you get so close to a curved surface that it appears flat, the vectors must be parallel in the normal sense of the word. This is what we see when we unfold the cone on a plane. At a point lying on the parallel - on the circle along which the cone touches the sphere - the cone and the sphere have a common tangent plane. This means that the parallel transfer of a vector along a given circle on a sphere can be studied using a cone. But, it would seem, what is the connection between the vectors on the cone and the Foucault pendulum? To answer, you need to remember about angular momentum and the law of its conservation. Let's say we have a gyroscope in a gimbal that allows it to freely change the direction of its axis. Let's direct its axis to some distant star (the North Star is not suitable for us) and spin the disk. Then the angular momentum vector, perpendicular to the plane of the disk, will always be directed towards this star, due to the corresponding conservation law. This means that in a laboratory reference frame associated with the Earth's surface, the gyroscope axis will rotate following the direction of the selected star. Now we apply the law of conservation of angular momentum to the Foucault pendulum. The angular momentum vector will be directed perpendicular to the plane of oscillation of the pendulum. But, unlike a gyroscope, the angular momentum of a pendulum cannot be constantly directed towards one star, since the pendulum oscillates under the influence of gravity directed towards the Earth's center of mass. It turns out that the angular momentum vector should be directed tangentially to the earth’s surface and at the same time move “as parallel to itself as possible,” which is a consequence of the law of conservation of momentum, taking into account the imposed restrictions. Thus, for the angular momentum vector of the Foucault pendulum, transferred by the rotation of the Earth along the surface of the sphere, the condition of parallel transfer is satisfied. Indeed, according to the law of conservation of angular momentum, the pendulum “wants” to oscillate all the time in one plane. However, due to the fact that the force of gravity is directed towards the center of the Earth, the normal vector to the plane of oscillation must always be directed tangentially to the earth's surface. In other words, we are talking about parallel vector transfer on a sphere. It follows from this that a paper cone with drawn vectors is not a “simplified model” or a “fit to answer”: the curvature of the Earth’s surface is the direct cause of the rotation of the plane in which the Foucault pendulum oscillates. Topology (How to determine the shape of a planet)

Do you know the fundamental difference between a bun and a bagel? When you eat a bagel, there is nothing left, and when you eat a bagel, there is a hole left. This kind of difference is what topology deals with. Speaking a little more strictly, topology is a part of mathematics that studies the properties of geometric objects that do not change under smooth deformations. In other words, the object can be bent, crushed, bent, but it cannot be cut and glued different parts. It would seem that if you take any geometric drawing and allow yourself to mentally bend all these segments, circles, straight lines - what will remain of the geometry? The circle will cease to be a circle, the straight line will become a curve, and all possible theorems will be violated. However, there are properties of shapes that will survive these destructive transformations. And the joke about the donut hole is only part of the joke. To verify this, you will need an imaginary balloon. Imagine that you are squeezing it from diametrically opposite points, squeezing it with two fingers (Fig.2). At some point

When you open your eyes again, you will find that now you are no longer holding a balloon, but an excellent bicycle tube. From a topological point of view, a balloon is a sphere, and the surface of a donut is a torus. We have just performed a “glue-break” transformation, which led to a change in the topological invariant - the number of holes: a sphere has no holes, but a torus has one. What is the number of holes in the intermediate figure resulting from gluing two opposite points of the sphere, topology cannot say. The maximum that can be done is to give our figure some name. To visually distinguish a sphere from a torus, you need to be at some distance from them.
