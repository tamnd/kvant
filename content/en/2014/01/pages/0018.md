Theorist Prize for Experiment

L. BELOPUKHOV

ON THE 8TH OF OCTOBER 2013, THE NOBEL COMMITTEE announced the award of the Nobel Prize in Physics to Professor François Englert of the Free University of Brussels and Professor Peter Higgs of the University of Edinburgh “for the theoretical discovery of a mechanism that has contributed to our understanding of the origin of the mass of subatomic particles and which has recently been confirmed

François Englert and Peter Higgs with the discovery of a predicted fundamental particle by the ATLAS and CMS experiments at the Large Hadron Collider at CERN." The formulation is unusually long - 31 words, but if you open three abbreviations, then even 43 words, although the average length of such formulations (in physics) is 11 words, and there is even a formulation of 3 words: “for the discovery of the neutron”. Another feature of the current prize is that it is awarded not to experimenters, but to theorists who developed and published their work 49 (!) years ago. In fairness, it should be noted that in those years, not only Engler and Higgs put forward a new hypothesis. Several other theoretical physicists worked in the same direction (and almost all independently of each other). However, the Nobel Prize is awarded only to those scientists who live to see the award being discussed. (The only exception is the 2001 Prize in Physiology or Medicine, awarded to Ralph Stayman, whose death the Nobel Committee did not have time to learn about a week before the discussion and the decision was not reversed.) The work of Engler, Higgs and other scientists in that time, already distant by the standards of the development of science, seemed semi-fantastic. Physics journals have been reluctant to publish them for some time. But after about 15 years, theoretical physicists became somehow uncomfortable without these works, and they began to look forward to experimental confirmation of the theoretical prediction. For very significant reasons, this prediction received the conventional name “Higgs boson.” For a long time, this phrase was known only to those who studied particle physics. But a few years ago, many people learned about the Higgs boson. This happened when the Large Hadron Collider (LHC) was launched, one of the main goals of which was declared to be the discovery of this hypothetical particle. In the media, using the careless statements of some scientists, the Higgs boson began to be called the devil particle, the divine particle and even the God particle. So what is this mysterious object? The magazine «Kvant» talked about it in an excellent article by academician V. Rubakov “Towards the discovery of the Higgs boson” («Kvant» No. 5–6, 2012). This article successfully combines scientific accuracy with popular presentation. Let us dwell on two points - on deciphering the meaning of the theoretical discovery and on its confirmation. By the end of the last century, the more than 50-year history of particle physics was marked by the creation of a theory of “almost everything,” as the theoretical physicists themselves who created it wrote. The most basic (fundamental) “building blocks” of which atomic nuclei, atoms, molecules, substances, biological objects, people, planets, stars and galaxies are composed were identified and classified, and the laws of interaction between these “building blocks” were determined. The theory was called the Standard Model. It is possible that the origin of such a (“clumsy”, as V. Rubakov writes) name is due to the fact that it, like technological standards, includes all the ingredients necessary to create an object, and all the production operations performed. In our case, the ingredients are the “bricks”, production operations are the laws of interaction, and the object is the entire Universe. The ingredients of the Standard Model are 6 quarks, 6 leptons and 5 particles that carry the interactions of quarks and leptons between $собой.^{1}$ A puzzling question may arise - where are our usual protons, neutrons and electrons? All of them are in the Standard Model: both the proton and the neutron consist of three quarks of two types, and the electron is one of the leptons. Other leptons “walk” in huge numbers throughout the Universe. These are neutrinos (and antineutrinos), which very rarely interact with other particles, but they carry the most valuable information about the processes inside the stars where they are born. And quarks in suitable conditions, when grouped, can create not only protons and neutrons, but also many (several hundred) different short-lived particles. There was even the term “zoo” of particles. With the advent of quark concepts, all of them turned out to be not fundamental, not the main “building blocks” of the world. The five carrier particles are sometimes called "glues". One of them is directly called - gluon (from the English glue - glue). The most famous “glue” is the photon. Using his example, it is clear that carrier particles can be spoken of as field quanta. A field is an object containing possible (virtual) carrier particles. Under any influence on the field, such a particle is, as it were, born from it, becoming a real particle. For example, the process of the appearance of a photon occurs during the transition of an atomic electron from one energy state to another.

1 All quarks and leptons can have antiparticles that differ in the opposite sign of charge - electric, baryon or lepton.
