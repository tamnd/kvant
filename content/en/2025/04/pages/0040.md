We conclude: the temperature dependences of the power of heat losses associated with thermal radiation, proportional to $T^{4}$, and the power of heat losses associated with the evaporation of matter, proportional to exp L RT, are indeed different. The obtained differences in the shares of the total heat loss power at different temperatures for these two heat loss mechanisms qualitatively correspond to reality. In other words, as temperature increases, the power of losses associated with evaporation increases faster than the power of heat losses due to radiation. These powers are compared for a thick ice floe at a temperature of about 256 K 17 q C. If the ice floe turns out to be at a distance from the Sun that is two times less than the distance from the Earth to the Sun, then in this case, with the same absorption coefficient of radiation incident on the ice floe (25%), the temperature established on the illuminated surface will become equal to 272 K and the ice will begin to melt.
