REVIEWS, BIBLIOGRAPHY

Another door to mathematics

MATHEMATICS IS A COMPLEX SCIENCE and the path to mathematics is not easy. Ben Orlin, in his book Math Games with Stupid $рисунками»^{1}$, opens the door for readers to the world of mathematics through games. The author conventionally divides the games presented in the book into five categories: geometric games, number games, combinatorial games, risk games and information games. At the same time, he does not set himself the goal of systematically presenting any branches of mathematics. Orlin doesn't so much explain mathematics as entice you into it. He

demonstrates how to approach various mathematical topics and problems through play. The author shows that mathematics literally surrounds us, and mathematical games can be found almost everywhere if you adjust your perception correctly. The author examines some games in detail, opening up new horizons for the reader, while others are described only in general terms, leaving room for independent research. The illustrations in the book were made by the author and deserve special attention. If you are already familiar with Orlin's other books, such as Math with Wacky Pictures or The Time of Variables, you will recognize his style. Fun figure explain playing positions to help you better understand the rules, and sometimes serve to explain complex ideas. The narrative is presented in a light style, and the author's gentle humor and paradoxical comments not only attract attention, but also encourage thought. You can work with the book at different levels. You can simply spend time having fun, or you can immerse yourself in this or that game headlong: study its connections, history, strategies, come up with new variations or compare it with other games. The book is suitable for readers from 9 to 99 years old, but it will be especially useful for high school students. Exciting games can be the beginning of independent research - both serious and not so serious. Schoolchildren, especially during competitions and exams, often feel that it is important to solve problems quickly. But real, “adult” mathematics requires a different approach: slow, thoughtful, immersed in details and studying the connections between problems and different areas of mathematics. This is how mathematical thinking is formed.
