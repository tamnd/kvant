On the table in front of the lecturer there was an electric battery (voltaic pole), copper rods, clamps for connecting them to the battery and other objects, among which was a magnetic needle (like a large compass). The rods connected to the battery heated up and it was possible to use them to heat and even boil water - a prototype of an electric stove. There would be nothing interesting about such an experience today. But this happened when the only source of rapid heating was fire - the Joule-Lenz law was still far away. By chance, the magnetic needle ended up close to one of the rods. And one of the students noticed that when the current was turned on, the needle trembled and deviated slightly from its “legal” direction to the north. Oersted himself did not notice this at first; he spoke about the thermal effect of the current. But when the student told about his

observation, Ørsted became interested. At first he decided that the arrow was affected by the mechanical effect of the switch when pressing on it. The table shook, and this was transmitted to the magnetic needle. However, when she was placed on a separate table, the phenomenon repeated. Oersted abandoned all the experiments he had been involved in and began to study this completely new and unexpected phenomenon. Oersted ordered a more powerful battery and rods made of purer copper. In fact, he received a much stronger current in the rods, and the magnetic needle began to respond to the current much more strongly. When the current was turned on, it turned almost 90 degrees and pointed not to the north, but to the east or west. It was located tangentially to imaginary circles, the centers of which were on the rod with current. The effect of the current on the arrow was, as it were, lateral. Oersted understood that he had discovered a phenomenon unknown to science. According to the prevailing views of that time, electricity and magnetism are different phenomena. Magnetism only occurs on substances containing iron, while electrical forces operate between objects made from a wide variety of materials. But experiments showed a connection between electricity and magnetism. This connection turned out to be difficult, and Oersted was unable to describe it with mathematical formulas. He only presented the drawings and figure phenomenon of turning the arrow. And on July 21, 1820, a four-page pamphlet in Latin was published in Copenhagen, entitled “Experiments Concerning the Effect of Electric Conflict on the Magnetic Needle” by Oersted. Realizing the importance of the discovery, Ørsted sent out a brochure that same day to all European scientific institutions and scientific journals. After publishing his works, Oersted traveled to the scientific centers of Europe. He was greeted with great enthusiasm and was accepted as an honorary member of many academies of sciences.
