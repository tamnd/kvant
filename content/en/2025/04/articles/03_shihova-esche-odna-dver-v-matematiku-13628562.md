Another door to mathematics

MATHEMATICS IS A COMPLEX SCIENCE and the path to mathematics is not easy. Ben Orlin, in his book Math Games with Stupid $рисунками»^{1}$, opens the door for readers to the world of mathematics through games. The author conventionally divides the games presented in the book into five categories: geometric games, number games, combinatorial games, risk games and information games. At the same time, he does not set himself the goal of systematically presenting any branches of mathematics. Orlin doesn't so much explain mathematics as entice you into it. He

demonstrates how to approach various mathematical topics and problems through play. The author shows that mathematics literally surrounds us, and mathematical games can be found almost everywhere if you adjust your perception correctly. The author examines some games in detail, opening up new horizons for the reader, while others are described only in general terms, leaving room for independent research. The illustrations in the book were made by the author and deserve special attention. If you are already familiar with Orlin's other books, such as Math with Wacky Pictures or The Time of Variables, you will recognize his style. Fun figure explain playing positions to help you better understand the rules, and sometimes serve to explain complex ideas. The narrative is presented in a light style, and the author's gentle humor and paradoxical comments not only attract attention, but also encourage thought. You can work with the book at different levels. You can simply spend time having fun, or you can immerse yourself in this or that game headlong: study its connections, history, strategies, come up with new variations or compare it with other games. The book is suitable for readers from 9 to 99 years old, but it will be especially useful for high school students. Exciting games can be the beginning of independent research - both serious and not so serious. Schoolchildren, especially during competitions and exams, often feel that it is important to solve problems quickly. But real, “adult” mathematics requires a different approach: slow, thoughtful, immersed in details and studying the connections between problems and different areas of mathematics. This is how mathematical thinking is formed. And games provide an excellent opportunity for this: they are exciting, you can play them over and over again, reaching new levels, thinking, coming up with new strategies. At the same time, we try

Game "Domination"

In this game, two players take turns placing dominoes on a rectangular board. One places the dominoes vertically, the other horizontally. If a player cannot make a move, he loses. The field sizes are not specified, which immediately raises questions. How big should the field be so that the game is neither too easy nor too difficult? Are there winning strategies? Which? Do they change depending on the size of the field? It's a good idea to just play a few games first to get a feel for the game. You should start with small fields, for example 2 u 2, 2 u 3 or 3 u 3. You should pay attention to the strategies and tactics used, monitor the number of moves, the features of the game at the beginning and end of the game. It is also worth considering

Game "Pig"

This game can be played by two to eight people. During your turn, you can roll a pair of dice as many times as you like, adding the total to your score each time. You can stop at any time. The first one to score 100 points wins. There are several bonuses: if both dice roll the same number, the sum is doubled (for example, 5 + 5 is worth 20 points), and two ones are worth 25 points. But be careful: if one die shows a one and the other doesn’t (the probability of this outcome is about 28%), then all points scored during the turn are reset to zero (points scored in previous turns are saved) and the turn goes to the opponent.
