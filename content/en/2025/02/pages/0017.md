SUPERCONDUCTIVITY.

Two electrons and a shrinking crystal lattice of a flowing electron. For a pair of bound electrons, their spins turn out to be antiparallel and their total value becomes zero: h 2 h 2 0. These two electrons are, as physicists say, a quasiparticle formed when electrons interact with ions of the crystal lattice. Or rather, with phonons. This interaction is called electron-phonon interaction. A pair of electrons “feels” each other through phonons. In this case, the distance between the electrons of a pair can be very large, on the order of tens of nanometers, while the average distance between ions and free electrons is on the order of one nanometer. Between the electrons of the pair, other “unpaired” electrons can “flicker” and ions “sway”, but the electrons of the pair do not care about them, they do not notice them. There is an analogy: the electrons of such a couple are two dancers of the opposite sex (back), who dance in the same rhythm, sometimes at a great distance from each other, without losing sight of their partner, as if feeling him, although other disco participants can dance between them and do not disturb them. And there are many such couples in the hall. Quantum statistics shows that particles with half-integer spin (for example, electrons) obey the Pauli principle and in the same ensemble (crystal) must have different energies. However, for particles (and quasiparticles) with integer or zero spin, different quantum statistics apply. It is called Bose–Einstein statistics. Its main difference is that the particles that obey it can be in exactly the same quantum state and have the same energy. The main example of such particles is photons, whose spin number is equal to one. In a powerful laser beam, all photons can have the same energy (monochromatic laser light). Particles that obey Bose–Einstein statistics are called bosons. A quasiparticle consisting of a pair of bound electrons and having zero spin is a boson. It was called a Cooper pair (Leon Cooper was the first to consider the formation of such a pair in 1956). The next task of the researchers was to consider the energy behavior of an ensemble of Cooper pairs. Since all pairs have the same energy, there are no other allowed energies left in the energy spectrum. Both energies, greater vapor energies, and lesser energies are prohibited. These are forbidden zones, as these energy intervals are called in statistics. Theorists call the energy of Cooper pairs an energy gap. But this means that a boson (Cooper pair) does not change its energy when passing through a superconductor. There is no resistance to his movement. And there are no losses due to heating of the conductor, no matter how many Cooper pairs are formed. And their number determines the current strength; it can be very large.
