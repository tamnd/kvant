To lower the gas temperature below the critical one, only the first method is often sufficient - cooling by evaporation of the liquid. Organoelement fluorine-containing hydrocarbons, called freons or freons (about 40 of them are known), are best suited for this purpose. In the hollow walls of the refrigerator (or freezer) chamber, liquid evaporates due to the heat removed from the contents of the chamber. Freon vapor enters another chamber, where it is compressed by a compressor. When condensing, they release heat into the surrounding space, part of which is the heat received from the refrigerator compartment (the back wall of a working kitchen refrigerator is always warm). This means that heat is transferred from a body with a low temperature (the contents of the refrigerator) to a body with a higher temperature (the space surrounding the refrigerator). But this is in no way a violation of the second law of thermodynamics, which prohibits such spontaneous processes. And for this, the refrigerator needs an external influence - the work of the compressor (and the corresponding payment for this work). In a kitchen refrigerator, the operating temperature in the freezer is 18 q C, in food industry refrigerators 35 q C. For the purpose of liquefying some gases, more expensive refrigerants are used, which make it possible to obtain lower temperatures. But they are still not low enough to liquefy gases such as methane, oxygen, nitrogen, hydrogen and helium. A simple way to lower the temperature is the adiabatic expansion of the gas with the performance of work. In cooling units (expanders), gas work is the energy of piston movement (piston expanders) or turbine rotation (turboexpanders). Since the cooled gas needs to be compressed again, a compressor is needed. This reduces the efficiency of the installation and increases the cost of liquefied gas production. In the 30s of the last century, the famous physicist, future Nobel laureate, academician P.L. Kapitsa proposed a new design of a turboexpander with a common axis of rotation of the turbine and compressor. The cooling gas “worked” to pressurize the next portion of gas. This dramatically increased the efficiency of the installation, increased its productivity and reduced the cost of production. But a lot of work was required to select the appropriate types of turbines and compressors. And it is no coincidence that in 1943 it was P.L. Kapitsa who was appointed head of the Main Directorate of the Council of People's Commissars for Oxygen. The country was in dire need of increasing the use of liquid oxygen in the metallurgical industry, and Kapitsa's decisive participation in this matter was a rare case of the active participation of a scientist involved in fundamental physics in a practical matter. In a short time, the production of liquid oxygen in the USSR increased significantly. And today, all over the world, Kapitsa turboexpanders are an integral part of gas liquefaction plants. These installations also use another method of lowering the temperature - using the Joule-Thomson effect. This effect was established in 1853–54 by two English physicists, the then already famous James Joule (1818–1889) and his younger friend and colleague in science William Thomson (1824–1907), no less famous under the name of Lord Kelvin. They experimentally established that during adiabatic expansion of a gas without performing work, its temperature changes. According to the ideal gas model, which neglects the forces of interaction between molecules, this should not be the case. After all, according to the first law of thermodynamics, the change in internal energy during such an expansion is zero. This means that there should be no change in temperature. But it exists.
