SUPERCONDUCTIVITY.

Johannes Diederik van der Waals received the Nobel Prize with the wording “For his work on the equation of state of gases and liquids.” By this time, the concept of critical temperature had already become firmly established in the practice of gas liquefaction. The liquefaction temperature must be below critical. The liquefaction temperature can greatly depend on pressure. Therefore, tables usually give boiling temperatures at atmospheric pressure, i.e. temperatures at which a “gas liquid” boils. For propane it is 231 K (–42 1 q C), for methane 112 K (161 q C), for oxygen 90 K (183 q C), for nitrogen 77 K (196 q C), for hydrogen 20.1 K, for helium 4.2 K. And where can you get such low temperatures? The lowest temperature on Earth was recorded at the Antarctic Vostok station on July 21, 1983, this is 89.2 q C. In space, of course, there are lower temperatures. On the bodies of the Solar System, the lowest temperature is 33 K (– 240 q C). And strangely enough, it is observed not on the planets most distant from the Sun, but very close to the Earth - at the bottom of some very deep lunar craters, where the Sun never looks. And the lowest temperature in the Universe was recorded in the protoplanetary Boomerang nebula at a distance of 5000 light years from Earth - this is 1 K. This is lower than the temperature of the cosmic microwave background radiation. But still not absolute zero.

⟦column⟧

On Earth, low and ultra-low (cryogenic) temperatures have long been obtained in laboratory and technical conditions. At first it was the desire to obtain a temperature of several tens of kelvins, then - several kelvins. And today there is a competition between scientific laboratories - to get as close as possible to absolute zero. And amazing successes have been achieved along this path. Today the “world record” is 3.8 $10^{11}$ K, or thirty-eight picokelvins (Germany, research center of the University of Bremen). Why is all this needed at all - to obtain low and especially ultra-low temperatures? Why are liquefied gases needed? Let's look at several reasons. Firstly, it is the satisfaction of scientific curiosity and the inevitable excitement of research. Well? This is generally an important reason for the development of science. As Enrico Fermi said when he created and launched the world's first nuclear reactor on December 2, 1942, designed to produce plutonium for an atomic bomb: “This is, first of all, very interesting physics.” The second reason is the study of the properties of substances and their behavior at low temperatures. They can differ greatly from their properties and behavior at normal temperatures. Of great scientific importance is the study of chemical reactions under conditions when the speeds of chaotic movement of particles become completely insignificant and the forces of interaction between molecules remain more significant for the reaction. In chemical laboratories you can often see cylinders with liquid nitrogen and even liquid helium. Knowledge of the properties of substances at low temperatures is also necessary in many human affairs, for example, in Arctic and especially Antarctic expeditions. Thus, insufficient knowledge of the properties of tin at low temperatures was one of the reasons for the tragic end of Robert Scott's expedition to the South Pole in 1912. On this expedition, tin was the main element for soldering seams in kerosene cans.
