In ordinary matter, the magnitude of this counter-field is very small, it is only $10^{6}$ of the magnitude of the external magnetic field. All substances have diamagnetism. Only it is often masked by another effect that enhances the external field and is called paramagnetic. If there is no paramagnetic effect, the substance is called diamagnetic. The magnitude of the force pushing a diamagnetic material out of the region of a strong inhomogeneous field is very small and is usually not taken into account in electrical designs. But it turned out that in superconductors the counter-field is equal in magnitude to the external one, but there is no magnetic field inside the substance. The consequence of this is, in particular, a large force that pushes the superconductor out of the inhomogeneous magnetic field. These studies were carried out by R. Meisner and his colleagues. Russian physicist A. Abrikosov developed a theoretical mechanism for this phenomenon. For some superconductors, metals and their alloys, the magnetic field induction decreases to zero already at a distance of several tens of nanometers from the surface. Abrikosov called them superconductors of the first kind. But he also admitted the existence of other superconductors, in which the external field partially penetrates deep into the substance. Abrikosov called them type II superconductors. Subsequently it turned out that there are a majority of such superconductors. And their superconducting transition temperature turned out to be higher than that of type I superconductors. Later this became of great practical importance. Using methods based on the Meissner effect and the Abrikosov hypothesis, hundreds of superconducting substances of the first and second kind were discovered.

Particularly important was the experimental discovery of type II superconductors niobium-tin $Nb_{3} Sn$ and niobium-germanium $Nb_{3} Ge$. For the first, the critical temperature (the temperature of transition to the superconducting state) turned out to be 18.5 K, and the critical induction of the magnetic field that destroys superconductivity was 28 Tesla. The second has 23.9 K and 10 Tesla, respectively. In addition, these materials are flexible enough to make wires. This makes it possible to have a current of tens of thousands of amperes in the superconducting windings of the solenoid, and on the axis of such a solenoid the magnetic field induction exceeds 10 Tesla. Superconducting magnets for cranes and powerful small electric motors were created. Not fantastic, but real engineering projects for transmitting electricity via superconducting cables have appeared. Even now, compound $Nb_{3} Sn$ remains one of the most important for creating powerful superconducting electromagnets. But the theory of superconductivity did not appear for a long time. There was no answer to the main question - why, at temperatures below the critical temperature, the electrical resistance completely disappeared abruptly, and did not just gradually decrease. What was the change in the internal structure of matter during this phase transition? In 1957, the article “The Theory of Superconductivity” appeared in the most prestigious physics journal “Physical Review”. The authors of the article were American physicists John Bardeen and his young students Leon Cooper and Robert Schrieffer. John Bardeen was already a world-famous physicist; in 1956 he became a Nobel laureate for his work in a group of physicists who gave the world the transistor and thereby ushered in the “transistor age” in which we have lived for eighty years now. It must be assumed that among the three authors of the article it was Bardin who was the leader. And he generously gave his ideas to young scientists.
