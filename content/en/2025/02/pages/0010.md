KVANT 2025/№2

James Dewar

You may say, isn’t this an ordinary thermos? It's very similar! Yes, indeed. A cold storage invention can easily be converted into a heat storage device. Dewar did not have a practical streak and did not start working on “thermoses”. The idea was picked up by an American company, which came up with this word to distinguish its production from what Dewar did. Household thermoses have a simpler design and their vacuum requirements are not as stringent. And most importantly, people need them immeasurably more than scientific vessels for storing cold. Dewar did not bother to patent his invention and was left with nothing, despite repeated attempts to sue the now-rich Thermos company. Isn't it a paradox - the invention for storing cosmic cold has become a symbol of everyday warmth and comfort! But Dewar’s name has not disappeared - all chemists know him. Naturally, after the liquefaction of hydrogen, Dewar began to try to obtain liquid helium. But he dealt with too many issues in both physics and chemistry; as we would say, he was too scattered in his scientific interests. Perhaps he was distracted from science by the struggle for priority in the invention of a vacuum vessel. And not only. One way or another, Dewar lost the main bet in cryogenic research - obtaining liquid helium. And he lost it to the most outstanding scientist in this field, the Dutch physicist Heike Kamerlingh Onnes (1853–1926).

In 1882, Kamerlingh Onnes became a professor at the University of Leiden, where in 1894 he created a cryogenic laboratory and became its permanent director. He proclaimed the laboratory’s motto: “Through measurement to knowledge.” A few years later, this laboratory became a recognized scientific center for work on low and ultra-low temperatures. It differed from other laboratories in its powerful technological base, unique equipment and well-trained support staff. Here, liquid gases were produced not in drops, but in liters (in 1904, the laboratory’s installations produced 4 liters of liquid hydrogen per hour). In the laboratory, the properties of substances at temperatures below 20 K were studied - absorption spectra, phosphorescence, viscosity of liquefied gases, their magnetic and electrical properties. But Kamerlingh Onnes considered the main goal of the laboratory’s work to be the liquefaction of the only remaining “unconquered” gas - helium. An installation was built with cascade use of the Joule–Thomson effect in gas cooled below 20 K. But no one knew to what temperature helium had to be cooled to become a liquid. From calculations using the van der Waals equation, it only followed that it was below 20 K. And so this experiment was scheduled for July 10, 1908. At 8 a.m., the liquid hydrogen production plant began operating. By 2 o'clock in the afternoon the required amount had been produced - 20 liters. In the presence of many physicists from different countries, at 5 o'clock the circulation of cooled helium vapor began through pipes passing through liquid hydrogen cooling due to evaporation. All eyes were focused on the glass flask in the center of the installation, where the helium liquid was supposed to collect. For a long time the flask seemed empty. Evening came.
