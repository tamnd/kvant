SUPERCONDUCTIVITY.

Georg Bednorz and Karl Müller explore the boiling of liquid nitrogen at atmospheric pressure. Indeed, superconductivity at such temperatures is a breakthrough, bringing its widespread use closer. After all, liquid nitrogen is almost 100 times cheaper than liquid helium and boils away much more slowly. This experimental breakthrough in physics can only be compared with the discoveries of X-rays, radioactivity and the transistor. The leader of this pair of physicists was the Swiss physicist Carl Alexander Müller (1927–2003), a professor at the University of Zurich, and since 1982, the head of the physics department in the European scientific division of the world famous IBM corporation. He began, on instructions from IBM, to study the electrical properties of minerals such as perovskite as promising semiconductor materials and discovered an anomalous behavior of the resistance of this substance with decreasing temperature. What is perovskite? This is a rather rare mineral with the chemical formula $CaTiO_{3}$, which is more correctly considered not a salt of titanic acid, but a compound of CaO oxides $TiO_{2}$. It received a name recognized throughout the world when it was first found in the Urals in 1839 in honor of the Russian senator Lev Perovsky, one of the few Decembrists forgiven by Nicholas I, a sponsor of many geological expeditions, who collected a rich collection of crystals, which he transferred to the Hermitage. In 1984, Müller invited Johannes Georg Bednorz (b. 1950), whom he remembered as his talented student, to collaborate with. Together, they began to methodically study substances similar to perovskite, each time engaging in their chemical synthesis, alternately replacing one metal oxide with another. Their guiding star was the search for the superconducting transition. And after two years of hard work, success came - high-temperature superconductivity in ceramic materials was discovered. Many laboratories around the world have begun searching for new high-temperature superconductors. Over 30 years, about a thousand such substances of different chemical classes have been discovered. Some of them became superconductors only at pressures of hundreds of thousands and millions of atmospheres (hundreds of gigopascals). Most were classified as type II superconductors (with partial penetration of the magnetic field inside), and the destructive magnetic field in them amounted to tens of teslas. The record temperature was discovered for the first time by Russian physicists (MSU) in 2003 at atmospheric pressure, the temperature of transition to the superconducting state of 135 K (138 q C) for cuprate - a substance with a complex composition $HgBa_{2} Ca_{2} CuO_{10}$. And at a huge pressure of 70 GPa in 2020, a critical temperature of 260 K (13 q C) was discovered for lanthanum superhydride $LaH_{6}$. In this competition (as in modern sports), many results were annulled after some time. Errors were discovered in the experiments, and repeated studies did not confirm the stated result.
