Moreover, if the barrier has a finite extent, it is called the barrier width, then behind the barrier the particle enters a region where again its total energy is greater than the potential and the kinetic energy is positive. The particle is realized again and continues its movement. In relation to the flow of particles, this means that part of the flow was not reflected, but passed through the barrier - as if through an imaginary tunnel. Hence the names “tunnel effect”, “tunneling”. And in relation to an individual particle, mathematical formalism allows us to introduce the probability of overcoming the barrier as the ratio of the number of particles ' N that passed the barrier to the number of particles N that approached the barrier: W ' N N. This probability can be called the barrier transparency coefficient. There is no experimental way to follow the movement of an individual particle in the barrier region, for example, its trajectory. the tunnel effect simply does not exist in classical physics. Explanation refers to the creation and discussion of a visual model of a phenomenon. But the phenomenon itself exists! It was noticed and began to be studied independently of the birth of quantum physics. At the beginning of the twentieth century, due to the rapid development of radio electronics, physicists paid a lot of attention to the detailed study of gas discharges. And in 1901, the young scientist Robert Earhart in the laboratory of J. J. Thomson in Cambridge obtained unexpected results in the behavior of ions during a gas discharge between two closely spaced electrodes. Thomson suggested that this was not an experimental error, but some new phenomenon. Practical work on creating radio tubes distracted physicists from studying an incomprehensible phenomenon. These studies were continued only in 1911 by Thomson's graduate student Franz Roser. In 1914, he discovered the release of electrons (emission) from the cathode when emission could not occur due to the presence of another negatively charged electrode nearby (under high vacuum conditions). The phenomenon was called field emission, and no explanation was given for it at that time. Physicists at the Cavendish Laboratory became interested in creating a planetary model of the atom and the first nuclear reactions. But there were also inexplicable phenomena in nuclear physics. One of them is the probabilistic nature of radioactive transformations of nuclei. A theoretical explanation for these incomprehensible phenomena associated with the presence of a potential barrier appeared only after the creation of quantum mechanics in 1925. German physicist Friedrich Hund, Russian physicists Leonid Mandelstam, Mikhail Leontovich and Georgiy Gamow, American physicists Ronald Henry and Edward Condon almost simultaneously and independently applied wave quantum mechanics, then just created by Edwin Schrödinger, to problems with a potential barrier.
