Louis de Broglie

The harbinger of quantum mechanics was Louis de Broglie's hypothesis about the wave properties of matter, expressed by him in 1923. By this time, Einstein’s hypothesis about the quantum properties of radiation, formulated by him back in 1905, had already gained universal recognition, especially after its brilliant application by A. Compton to explain the interaction of electromagnetic radiation with free electrons. According to this hypothesis, an electromagnetic radiation wave can also be considered as an object that has corpuscular (quantum) mechanical characteristics - momentum p and energy E, related to the wavelength O by the relations $p h O,^{E} hc O$, where h is Planck’s constant, c is the speed of light in vacuum. Thus, electromagnetic radiation has a dual nature (dualism). In some phenomena, wave properties are more significant, in others – corpuscular (quantum) properties. But the fact that such dualism is characteristic not only of electromagnetic radiation, but of every

to a material object, was not immediately recognized by physicists. And this is the essence of de Broglie’s hypothesis. Any object with momentum p, at the same time, has certain wave properties characterized by the wavelength O h p. Indeed, soon after de Broglie’s article was published and he defended his dissertation on this topic in 1924, the wave phenomenon of electron diffraction on crystals was discovered in the experiments of American physicists Joseph Davisson and Lester Germer in 1925. In 1930, this phenomenon was studied in more detail by the English physicist George Thomson, the son of the famous J. J. Thomson, who “discovered” the electron in 1897. The era of electron microscopy has begun. Joseph Davisson and George Thomson became Nobel laureates in 1937. Note that J.J. Thomson Sr. was not only a wonderful physicist, but also a great teacher. A Nobel laureate himself (1906), he raised seven Nobel laureates, including his son. But in 1924, few physicists were interested in the problem of dualism. One of those interested was Edwin Schrödinger, professor of theoretical physics at the University of Zurich. He found interesting the idea of ​​de Broglie, expressed in his dissertation, that the trajectory of an electron in a stationary atomic orbit can be represented as a kind of standing wave. Using the stationary condition of a standing wave along the length of the electron's orbit and his postulate about the wave properties of the electron, de Broglie obtained for the hydrogen atom the same discrete values ​​of electron energies that Niels Bohr had derived 10 years earlier in his theory and which were so well confirmed by experience. Schrödinger began to look for an equation whose solution would be this standing wave. This was not an easy task. At first he failed in his attempt to represent this standing wave as a spatial wave-like distribution of the electron's charge. In the fall of 1925, Schrödinger's tuberculosis worsened, and he left to treat it
