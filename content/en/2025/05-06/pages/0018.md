The most important reactions in nature are the synthesis of helium nuclei from the nuclei of hydrogen isotopes. These reactions are the essence of stars as energy sources. In stars, however, other nuclear reactions occur with the formation of other elements of the periodic table with the release of energy. (When synthesizing heavier nuclei, the reactions are already endothermic, i.e., they require energy expenditure.) In the first years after the Second World War, an event of particular importance occurred on earth - on November 1, 1952, on the Enewetak Atoll (Marshall Islands of the Pacific Ocean), a stellar reaction was carried out for the synthesis of helium from hydrogen isotopes - deuterium and tritium. It was an explosion - a prototype of future hydrogen bombs. This device, however, could not be used for military purposes - it had a mass of 82 tons and was the size of a two-story house. The bulk of the device was thermal protection, or rather, “cold protection” of liquid hydrogen isotopes from boiling using liquid nitrogen. The main designer of this device was the outstanding American scientist and engineer Edward Teller. Less than a year later, on August 12, 1953, a device suitable for military use, weighing only 7 tons, was exploded at the Semipalatinsk test site in Kazakhstan (USSR). And the energy of this explosion was equivalent to 400 kilotons of TNT. This is not the place to discuss how this was achieved. Let us only note that the birth of this device (bomb) was entirely due to the scientific and engineering talent of Academician A.N. Sakharov. An arms race has begun. The test explosion of the most terrible bomb on October 30, 1961 (nicknamed “Tsar Bomb” or “Kuzka’s Mother”) was equivalent in energy to the explosion of 58 megatons of TNT (a train with

And although today there are still one and a half thousand hydrogen bombs in the nuclear arsenals of the United States and Russia, the likelihood of their terrible use is small and they continue to be slowly dismantled. Over 30 years, nuclear arsenals have decreased by more than 10 times. Even before the creation of bombs, in 1950-51, A.D. Sakharov, together with I.E. Tamm, developed the principle of a stationary nuclear fusion reaction as an almost inexhaustible source of “peaceful” energy. It was called a thermonuclear reaction. Why? It would be more correct to call it thermonuclear. In order for two nuclei of hydrogen isotopes (for example, a deuteron, i.e., a deuterium nucleus, with a triton, i.e., a tritium nucleus) to combine, they need to approach at a distance of 2 fm (2 $10^{15}$ m). Only then will the powerful nuclear forces “turn on” and, with their powerful attraction, give the particles a speed of the order of 5 $10^{6}$ m s. When there are many particles and they have such an average speed, this means that the temperature of the entire mass of particles will be on the order of hundreds of millions and even billions of kelvins. Apparently, this is exactly the temperature of the inner layers of the Sun. But on the path of the collision of particles, two deuterium nuclei or a deuterium nucleus with a tritium nucleus, there is a potential barrier of Coulomb repulsive forces. Calculations have shown that if the nuclei of hydrogen isotopes have an average energy corresponding to a temperature of 20 million degrees, then this barrier can be partially overcome. The probability of the tunnel effect will already be sufficient for the particles to come closer to 2 fm and the fusion of nuclei to occur with the appearance of a helium nucleus and a neutron. But their average energy will be much higher - it will correspond to a billion kelvins.
