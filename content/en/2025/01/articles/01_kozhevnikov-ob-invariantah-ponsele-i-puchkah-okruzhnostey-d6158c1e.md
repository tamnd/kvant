Around Poncelet's theorem

Poncelet's Theorem is one of the geometric theorems that you can admire endlessly! Let us present one of the “classical” versions of its formulation (of course, variations and generalizations are known). Poncelet's theorem. Let two circles be given on a plane, and the second circle lies inside the first (Fig. 1).

Suppose there is a closed n-link broken line, all of whose vertices lie on the first circle, and all of whose links touch the second. Then there are infinitely many such closed n-link broken lines and any point on the first circle can be the vertex of such a broken line. Thus, the construction of a polyline can be started from any point of the first circle, drawing a tangent to the second circle and obtaining a new intersection point on the first circle. The step-by-step construction of such a broken line is called the Poncelet process for a pair of given circles, and the broken line itself (inscribed in the first circle and circumscribed around the second) is called the Poncelet broken line or Poncelet trajectory. Let's look at another example. Let there be some multiset (a set in which elements can be repeated) of integers M and some number S. It is necessary to check whether there is a subset of numbers in M ​​that add up to S. There are no fast algorithms for finding the required subset yet. Thus, it is not known whether this problem lies in P. However, we can definitely say that the problem lies in NP. If we are given a certain set of numbers, then we just need to make sure that we were not deceived and were really given a subset M, and also that the sum of the numbers given to us is equal to S. Since the solution can be quickly checked, the problem lies in NP. In addition, there is a class of NP-complete problems, i.e. the class of the most “difficult” NP problems. If you find a “fast” algorithm for solving at least some of them, this will automatically mean that for any NP problem there is a “fast” algorithm. Movement of the sound source and receiver

If a sound source moves relative to a gas, then the spatial distribution of areas with high and low pressure (pressure maxima and minima in the wave) depends on the direction in which the sound wave propagates and on the speed vector of the sound source (both the direction and the magnitude of the source speed are taken into account). This phenomenon is called the Doppler effect, named after the physicist who first explained this effect. For example, a person standing motionless (in calm weather) and listening to the sound of a car (train or flying helicopter) passing by him at a speed less than the speed of sound hears a higher vibration frequency of the sound as the source approaches him in comparison with the frequency of the sound coming to the listener from the same sound source moving away from him. The movement of the listener himself relative to the air also leads to a dependence of the height of the audible sound on the direction of propagation of sound waves and on the magnitude and direction of the listener’s speed of movement.

Supersonic aircraft

If a sound source moves relative to a gaseous medium at a speed exceeding the speed of sound (some modern aircraft can fly in the air at a speed greater than the speed of sound), then in this case all space is divided into two regions: one region is not yet occupied by sound waves, and in the other region sound waves already exist. If such a sound source moves rectilinearly and uniformly, then a cone-shaped surface separates these two regions. They call it the Mach cone, named after the physicist who first described it. Sound waves in a heterogeneous medium

It is clear that the media in which sound waves propagate can be inhomogeneous. For example, it is known that in air (even in stationary air) both temperature and density can change with height above a selected initial horizontal level. Consequently, waves in such an inhomogeneous medium can change the direction of their propagation if they move from an area with one set of environmental characteristics to an area with another set. In this case, certain relationships are satisfied between the wave incident on the interface between the sections, reflected from this boundary, or the wave passing through this interface. The corresponding laws of reflection and refraction apply. If we assume that there are no losses, that the interface is flat and its geometric dimensions are significantly larger than the wavelength, then these laws coincide with the corresponding laws in geometric optics and are based on conservation laws in mechanics. Namely: on the laws of conservation of momentum and energy. Of course, there are situations in which the wavelengths are comparable

Surface waves

If the gaseous medium is heterogeneous and a sharp interface is observed, for example, separating from each other areas with different densities of the same gaseous substance (at different temperatures, but the same pressure) or areas with different compositions of gas mixtures, which also have different densities, then in the gravitational field the substances are located so that the less dense component is located above the more dense one. In such a situation, the formation and propagation of so-called surface waves along this interface is possible. (An analogue is gravitational waves on the surface of water.) The speed of their propagation depends on the densities above $U_{в}$ and below $U_{н}$ of the interface, as well as on the magnitude of the acceleration of gravity g. And, most interestingly, the speed of propagation of such waves depends on both the wavelength and amplitude. Of course, in the atmosphere at different altitudes, air densities differ and such surface waves are sometimes observed in the form of a specific form of clouds, but the frequency of vibration in such waves is so small that they do not fall into the frequency range that we can hear. In other words, such waves cannot be called sound waves.
