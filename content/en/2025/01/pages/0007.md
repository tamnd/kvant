ELECTROVACUUM

I come across a similar problem - why, when cooked, sausages burst lengthwise and not across. True, there is pressure not from outside, but from within, but the task is still similar. When an electron tube operates, its glass container heats up, and in some malfunctions the heating can be so strong that atmospheric pressure can deform the container (Fig. 3).

But this is a rare and, one might say, criminal case. In order for the lamp to work, voltage must be applied to the cathode, anode and grid (or several grids). In other words, the cylinder must be either dielectric (glass, ceramics) or metal, but then the inputs must be insulated. It would be possible to isolate all but one, but in practice they isolate everything - it is inconvenient to have a metal cylinder under the potential of one of the electrodes. Accordingly, both technological (how to do) and physical problems arise. Vacuum-tight, i.e. without gaps, joining metal and glass (or ceramics) is a well-known design and technological problem. Under what conditions hot glass sticks to metal is no longer a simple and not very well studied question. But what happens next? The connection itself is carried out at high temperature, then the object must be cooled, but what do substances do when the temperature changes? In particular, they change their dimensions, and if they are connected and the resulting mechanical stresses exceed the strength of the connected materials or the strength of the connection itself (these are three different strengths), then the connection will fail. In practice, they try to use materials with the same expansion as the cylinder material as inputs. But these are alloys that usually have higher resistance, and we were going to pass currents through the inputs. Therefore, the first physical problem is that the inputs must have a sufficient cross-section so that they are not heated by the flowing current, and the second problem is that the distance between the inputs must be large enough to prevent breakdown along the surface of the cylinder (breakdown along the surface occurs at lower voltages than breakdown through the volume).
