Let's look at another example. Let there be some multiset (a set in which elements can be repeated) of integers M and some number S. It is necessary to check whether there is a subset of numbers in M ​​that add up to S. There are no fast algorithms for finding the required subset yet. Thus, it is not known whether this problem lies in P. However, we can definitely say that the problem lies in NP. If we are given a certain set of numbers, then we just need to make sure that we were not deceived and were really given a subset M, and also that the sum of the numbers given to us is equal to S. Since the solution can be quickly checked, the problem lies in NP. In addition, there is a class of NP-complete problems, i.e. the class of the most “difficult” NP problems. If you find a “fast” algorithm for solving at least some of them, this will automatically mean that for any NP problem there is a “fast” algorithm. And the problem we considered is precisely NP-complete. Note that the task of finding a subset M with a sum S is essentially a special case of the game that artificial intelligence came up with. It’s just that in this case the game is played on a board, one of the sides of which is equal to 1. Thus, if a good algorithm exists, then it will most likely be difficult for a person to guess it, and even more so for modern artificial intelligence. All that remains is to somehow sort out the options. Such problems are often NP-complete. For example, Sudoku is also among them. So you can be sure that when a new level is generated, the numbers on the board appear randomly and the “solution” is also chosen randomly. Therefore, it is likely that some of the levels will not have the only correct combination, and this, of course, is not very good. After all, then when making a decision you will have to act at random at some point. Let us summarize our reasoning with the remark that artificial intelligence must be used with (human) intelligence, and not hope for its perfection.
