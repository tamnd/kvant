But besides them, everyone else consumes energy and power. What do they give out? Lamps operating in the high frequency range produce an electromagnetic wave, i.e. energy and power. About lamps operating in the region of relatively low frequencies, it is impossible to say “they produce”, we can say “they can produce”. Because to do this, a load must be connected to them, and without a load, they have voltage at the output (or current in the anode circuit), but no power. True, there are subtleties here. For example, there are high-frequency lamps that are harmful to operate without load. Thus, microwave ovens (microwave ovens) should not be turned on without load. But now we are discussing simpler situations. So, one way or another, efficiency is never 100%. Accordingly, two questions arise: how can efficiency be increased and where does the “rest” go? And the rest turns mainly into heat - electrons fly to the anode with energy and almost all of it is converted into heat (about 1% into x-rays). This heat causes a lot of troubles, for example, the anode material can evaporate or melt. Cooling occurs from the outside, and the anode is heated from the inside. When heated, the anode material expands, but the outer layers do not allow it to do this, and mechanical stress arises in the material. As a result, the anode material cracks. These processes are difficult to calculate, but experimental data and operational experience are available. At the very beginning of the article, it was briefly said about average power and its difference from pulsed power. Let's imagine that the lamp operates in continuous mode; part of the energy it consumes is somehow converted into heat and transferred to the electrodes. As a result, all this heat is released into the environment. Cooling - natural or artificial, water or air - is not so important, the main thing is that the heat is removed into the environment.
