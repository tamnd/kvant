The most well-known application is the imaging of internal organs using NMR.

How is internal organs visualized using NMR?

Until now we have implicitly assumed that, neglecting the influence of weak electron currents in the coils, the magnetic field in which the nuclei are placed is uniform, i.e. has the same value at all points. In 1973, Paul Latterbourg proposed conducting NMR studies by placing a sample in a magnetic field that varies from point to point. It is clear that in this case the resonant frequency for the nuclei under study changes from point to point, which makes it possible to judge their spatial location. And since the intensity of the signal from a certain region of space is proportional to the number of hydrogen atoms in this region, we obtain information about the distribution of the density of matter in space. Actually, this is the principle of the NMR research technique. As you can see, the principle is simple, although in order to obtain real images of internal organs in practice, it was necessary to obtain powerful computers for controlling radio frequency pulses and for a long time to improve the methodology for creating the necessary magnetic field profiles and processing NMR signals obtained from the coils. Let's imagine that along the x-axis there are small spheres filled with water (Fig.3). If the magnetic field does not depend on x, then a single signal appears (see Fig.3,a). Let us further assume that by means of additional coils (relative to the one that creates the main magnetic field directed along the z axis), we create an additional magnetic field $B_{0}$, varying along the x axis, and its value increases from left to right. It is clear that for spheres with different coordinates, the NMR signal will now correspond to different frequencies and the measured spectrum will contain five characteristic peaks (see Fig.3, b). The height of these peaks will be proportional to the number of spheres (i.e. the mass of water) having the corresponding coordinate, and thus, in the case under consideration, the intensities of the peaks will be in the ratio 3:1:3:1:1. Knowing the magnitude of the magnetic field gradient (i.e., the rate of its change along the x axis), we can represent the measured frequency spectrum as a dependence of the density of hydrogen atoms on the x coordinate. In this case, it will be possible to say that where the peaks are higher, the number of hydrogen atoms is greater: in our example, the numbers of hydrogen atoms corresponding to the positions of the spheres actually correlate as 3:1:3:1:1. By measuring radiofrequency NMR spectra and knowing the magnitudes of magnetic field gradients along the coordinates, it is possible to create a three-dimensional map of the distribution of spheres (and, consequently, hydrogen density) in the configuration under study. This is much more difficult to do than in the one-dimensional case discussed above, but it is intuitively clear what this process involves. An image restoration technique similar to the one we described is carried out in NMR tomography. Having finished accumulating data, the computer, through very fast algorithms, begins “processing” the signals and establishes a relationship between the intensity of the measured signals at a certain frequency and the density of resonating atoms at a given point in the body. At the end of this procedure, the computer visualizes a two-dimensional (or even three-dimensional) “image” of a specific organ or part of the patient’s body on its screen.

Striking "images"

In order to fully appreciate the results of an NMR study of human internal organs (for example, various sections of the brain, which a physicist today can obtain without touching the skull!), one should first of all understand that we are talking about a computer reconstruction of “images”, and not about real shadows that appear on photosensitive film when X-rays are absorbed in the process of obtaining an X-ray image.
