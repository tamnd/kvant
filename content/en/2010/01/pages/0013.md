WHAT

SUCH

NMR TOMOGRAPHY?

⟦figure⟧

Fig.4. Images of the skull and spinal column that show white or gray brain tissue, spine and cerebrospinal fluid with excellent anatomical accuracy depending on contrast

The human eye is a sensitive sensor of electromagnetic radiation in the visible range. Fortunately or unfortunately, radiation originating from internal organs does not reach our eyes - we see human bodies only from the outside. At the same time, as we just discussed, under certain conditions, the atomic nuclei of the internal organs of the human body can emit electromagnetic waves in the radio frequency range (i.e., frequencies much lower than for visible light), and the frequency varies slightly depending on the point of emission. It cannot be seen with the eye, so such radiation is recorded using complex equipment and then collected into a single image using special computer processing. And yet, we are talking about a completely real vision of the inside of an object or a human body. Humanity has achieved such amazing success thanks to a number of fundamental achievements of scientific thought: quantum mechanics with its theory of the magnetic moment, the theory of the interaction of radiation with matter, digital electronics, mathematical algorithms for signal conversion, and computer technology. The advantages of NMR imaging compared to other diagnostic methods are numerous and significant. The operator can easily select which sections of the patient's body to scan, and can also examine several sections of the selected organ simultaneously. In particular, by selecting magnetic field gradients appropriately, it is possible to obtain vertical cross-sectional images of the insides of our skull. This may be a central section or sections offset to the right or left. (Such studies are virtually impossible with X-ray radiography.) The operator can “narrow” the field of view by visualizing NMR signals originating from only one selected organ or only one part of it, thereby increasing the resolution of the image. An important advantage of NMR tomography is also the possibility

the ability to directly measure the local viscosity and direction of flow of blood, lymph and other fluids inside the human body. By selecting the necessary ratio between the relevant parameters, for example, the duration and frequency of pulses, for each pathology the operator can achieve optimal characteristics of the resulting image, say, increase its contrast (Fig.4). To summarize, we can say that for each image point (pixel) corresponding to a tiny volume of the object under study, it is possible to extract various useful information, in some cases including the distribution of concentrations of certain chemical elements in the body. To increase the sensitivity of measurements, i.e. increasing the signal intensity to noise ratio, a large number of signals should be accumulated and summed. In this case, it is possible to obtain a high-quality image that adequately conveys reality. That is why the time required for NMR tomography is quite long - the patient must remain relatively motionless in the chamber for several tens of minutes. In 1977, English physicist Peter Mansfield came up with a combination of magnetic field gradients that, while not providing particularly good image quality, nevertheless allows it to be obtained extremely quickly: a single signal is enough for the appropriate construction (in practice, this takes approximately 50 milliseconds). With the help of such a technique, called planar echo, it is now possible to monitor the pulsations of the heart in real time: in such a film, its contractions and expansions alternate on the screen. Was it possible to imagine at the dawn of the creation of quantum mechanics that a hundred years later the development of science would lead to the possibility of such miracles? It should be noted that in 2003, Paul Lauterbur and Peter Mansfield were awarded the Nobel Prize in Medicine “for the invention of magnetic resonance imaging.”
