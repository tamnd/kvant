Fortunately, there is another alternative approach involving so-called optical nanostructured materials - media whose optical properties (for example, refractive index) vary in space on a scale of less than a few hundred nanometers. A striking example of such materials are photonic crystals. Photonic crystals are periodic dielectric or metal-dielectric materials that affect the light waves propagating through them in the same way that the periodic potential in crystals affects the movement of electrons, leading to the formation of allowed and forbidden energy bands. Since the idea of ​​a photonic crystal is based on the phenomena of diffraction and interference, the period of the photonic crystal structure should be on the order of the wavelength of electromagnetic radiation in the substance, i.e. about 300 nm for operation in the visible light range. An example of one-dimensional photonic crystals is a multilayer structure of alternating layers of transparent substances with two different refractive indices (Fig.2,a). A system of parallel holes in the dielectric layer forms a two-dimensional photonic crystal (Fig.2,b), and densely packed nanospheres

quartz are a three-dimensional photonic crystal (Fig.2,c). What is so special about nanostructured materials and, in particular, photonic crystals? The fact that their optical properties - the direction, intensity and polarization of reflected and transmitted light - are determined not only and not so much by the refractive indices of the substances from which they are made, but by their structure. The specially selected structure of the substance leads to the phenomena of interference and diffraction, which significantly changes the conditions for the passage of light through the material. Thus, in photonic crystals, band gaps appear - regions of light frequencies in which light cannot penetrate inside the photonic crystal and is completely reflected from it. The emergence of nanostructured materials actually opens up a new direction in the creation of optical media. The required optical properties of the material are now achieved not by selecting the optimal chemical composition (as was the case in the old approach), but by creating a geometric or phase structure with a characteristic size not exceeding several hundred nanometers. Since nanostructured materials are created artificially, they are often called metamaterials.

Magnetized photonic crystals If a nanostructured material contains magnetic substances, then one can expect that it will exhibit magneto-optical effects similar to those that arise in ordinary homogeneous media, but perhaps slightly modified. The idea of ​​using magnetic substances to control light in a photonic crystal was first proposed in the late 90s of the last century by Japanese scientists. They examined the Faraday effect in one-dimensional photonic crystals, which are multilayer films of randomly alternating layers of bismuth-substituted yttrium ferrite garnet and quartz. For certain radiation frequencies with optimally selected structure parameters, an increase in the Faraday effect by more than 300 times was found compared to a similar homogeneous medium. Using the one-dimensional case as an example, several types of magnetic photonic crystals can be distinguished. First of all, these are standard systems consisting of alternating quarter-wave (thickness equal to one-fourth the wavelength of light in the substance) magnetic (for example, cerium-substituted yttrium ferrite garnet) and non-magnetic (for example, gadolinium gallium garnet) layers. Such photonic crystals have a band gap centered at the design wavelength, i.e. do not transmit light with a wavelength in a certain area around a given one. By design wavelength we mean the wavelength of light outside the crystal at which one-fourth of the wavelength fits in each of its layers. figure 3a and b show the transmittance and Faraday angle for a one-dimensional photonic crystal tuned to the near-infrared range (design wavelength 1.55 μm). The crystal consists of 30 pairs of magnetic and non-magnetic layers. The enhancement of the Faraday effect occurs at the boundary of the band gap, i.e. in the region of wavelengths 1.49 µm and 1.61 µm. It turns out that it is at these wavelengths that the group speed of light increases sharply. This leads to an increase in the effective time of interaction of the wave with the magnetization of the material, which means the Faraday effect increases. An important feature of resonances at boundary frequencies is that the transmission and Faraday rotation maxima practically coincide. This makes it possible to use photonic crystals as miniature elements that rotate the plane of polarization at large angles. The optimal selection of magnetic materials, their geometric dimensions and location will make it possible to create a new generation of optical devices controlled by magnetic fields. In this case, you need to keep in mind not only the infrared, but also the visible range of light.
