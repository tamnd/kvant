From the point of view of modern ideas about aviation, the achievements of the circle seem naive: flights of several tens of meters, launches in the summer with the help of a horse, a harness, a bicycle, and in the winter with the help of a sled. But, let us note, this was the time of the birth of aviation, and the activities of the circle were of a pioneering nature. The work of the circle influenced the development of the domestic aircraft industry. One of the members of the circle was Igor Sikorsky, who subsequently, having received the necessary funds, built a huge 4-engine aircraft at that time, famous in the history of Russian aircraft construction under the name “Ilya Muromets”. After the October Revolution, I. Sikorsky emigrated to the USA, where over time he became a famous helicopter designer. One of Sikorsky's helicopters made a transatlantic flight. Let us also note another interesting episode related to the circle. Before discussing the place that mathematics occupied in the life of Boris Delaunay in his childhood, it is worth noting another hobby that lasted throughout his life. This hobby - mountaineering - first appeared in Boris Nikolaevich at the age of 12-15 in the Swiss Alps, where the Delaunay family went on summer holidays in the early 1900s. Delaunay later spoke about his ascents and hikes in the vicinity of the Monte Rosa and Matterhorn peaks above Zermat in the Welsh Alps. Delaunay's mathematical talent manifested itself very early. At the age of 12 he was familiar with the basics of mathematics.

The father, for example, in 1904 took his son to the International Congress of Mathematicians in Heidelberg, where 14-year-old Boris saw Hilbert and Minkowski and attended their lectures. It turned out to be important for the future scientific work of Boris Nikolaevich that in the early 1900s his father worked as a professor at Varshavsky $университета^{2}$ simultaneously with the outstanding mathematician Georgiy Feodosievich Voron. G.F. Voronoi (1868–1908) was one of the most brilliant representatives of the famous St. Petersburg school of number theory. Nikolai Borisovich became friends with the outstanding mathematician, and Voronoi became a frequent guest in Delaunay’s house. Boris Nikolaevich recalled that when conversations between his father and Voronoi dragged on until late, he, a boy, already “being in bed in his room, listened to their conversation through the half-closed door leading into the hall.” By the way, G.F. Voronoi also took part in the work of the congress in Heidelberg and communicated a lot with Minkowski. The result of the communication between the two creators of a new direction in mathematics - the geometry of numbers - was the grandiose program that Voronoi outlined for himself for the following years. And although, unfortunately, fate gave him only four years, Georgiy Feodosievich managed to write several fundamental works on the geometry of positive quadratic forms and the theory of parallelohedrons, in which fundamental problems from the theory of coverings, packings and partitions were solved. These works determined the direction of research for a century to come.

Although Voronoi’s work subsequently had a serious influence on Delaunay’s research, Boris the high school student had no direct scientific contacts with the venerable scientist. Voronoi died unexpectedly at the age of 40 in the same year 1908, when Boris Delaunay entered the Faculty of Physics and Mathematics of Kyiv University. At about the same time, several exceptionally talented and ambitious young people entered this faculty to study, among them Otto Schmidt and Nikolai Chebotarev. Nikolai Grigoryevich Chebotarev (1894–1947) became an outstanding world-class algebraist, corresponding member of the USSR Academy of Sciences. Academician Otto Yulievich Schmidt (1891–1956) is a multifaceted talented person of incredible energy. In the history of Russian mathematics, Schmidt is known not only as a major algebraist, the author of the well-known book “Abstract Group Theory,” but also as the founder of the famous Moscow algebraic school.
