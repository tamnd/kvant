Some kind of higher stubbornness speaks in favor of the Unified State Examination. I'm so semi-optimistic here. In 1943, Stalin came up with the idea of ​​separating boys and girls in schools. In 1953, as soon as Stalin died, this was immediately canceled. – That is, This is not something natural, this is a whim of some people, and perhaps it will pass. - It's a whim. Maybe it is connected with money - someone is earning something there, but at least it is a whim. – I wanted to ask about mathematics and physics. Sometimes they are opposed, someone says - no, mathematics is a part of physics, someone says that it is only a tool for physics. As a person who knows both mathematics and physics, what could you say about this? – Firstly, I don’t know physics. I don't know math either. Well, of course, I touched a little on both. Of course they are different. For example, there is the Huygens-Fresnel principle, which is how to calculate an interference pattern. The fact that there are two names, and one of them is Huygens, suggests that he appeared in the 17th century. It has not yet been proven, but no one cares. Now, if a dog determines the truth with the help of its nose, a person - more with the help of the eye, then mathematicians and physicists - they perceive with different organs. Mathematicians believe that everything must be proven. Physicists believe that it is necessary to find an explanation for the phenomenon. For them, the phenomenon that is observed is the starting position. We need to find an explanation for him. For example, the phenomenon of helium superfluidity was described mathematically by Landau. This is considered his major achievement. But this is an approximate calculation, and there is no estimate of the error. However, physicists believe they now understand why this happens. – But, on the other hand, mathematicians also want to understand some phenomena, some theorems, situations. They don't just juggle formulas. - Certainly. But partly it is. Well, for example, many consequences of the axiom of choice - I know physicists who say that these are mathematical phantoms. This is, of course, a logical consequence of the axioms they accepted, but it has no relation to actual reality. Mathematical phantoms, consequences of unsuccessfully invented axioms. And now mathematicians are developing the idea that they need to limit themselves to the countable axiom of choice, and then many phantoms disappear. For example, unmeasurable sets disappear. Well, who knows. Pavel Sergeevich Alexandrov said that this is a mathematical reality. What is real is that such consequences are obtained from these axioms. This is a mathematical reality, but it is not a fact that there is some kind of physical reality behind it. How does physics work? She creates a mathematical model. It also happens that a verbal model exists first, that is, some verbal conversations about how the phenomenon occurs. But this is not yet a mathematical model. Then a mathematical model is created, which includes many things from mathematics. For example, kinematics. Ibid.

Each point is assumed to have a coordinate. The distance can always be divided in half. And in real physics, the smaller the distance, the more difficult it is to divide it in half, and this becomes completely unrealistic when we have no way to measure such a small distance. But, of course, with the help of models, understanding still occurs. This is all a mystery, no one here understands what is really happening, but I purely emotionally like Spinoza’s statement. He says: “Freedom is achieved by knowledge, and knowledge is achieved by spiritual unity with nature.” But he identified the word “nature” with the word “god”. In spiritual union with God. I think this truth is quite correct and deep. For example, Faraday. Why did he know that there was a transition from a magnetic field to an electric one? Nobody was watching. Why did he know that he existed? I understand this as the result of spiritual unity with nature. - How would you hear the music of angels? - Well, something like that. He had already made several major discoveries, he had a well-trained intuition, and therefore he could, like a god, see the truth directly, not through clever reasoning, but directly. – We once discussed that a direct vision of the truth is more important than the ability to prove theorems. - Well, yes, of course. – This, by the way, is also an interesting question. How important is, say, mathematical rigor in school, when learning. If you prove everything very strictly, it can be... - I think there is a compromise here. If in some place I referred to the obvious, perhaps I didn’t even refer to it, but kept silent - kept silent, realizing that the students would perceive it as something natural and not causing objections - then this may be honest, or it may be dishonest. Honest - if I can, without destroying this structure, add an accurate consideration, and dishonest - if I need to destroy this structure in order to make it accurate. For example, I want to prove that the set of points on a segment is uncountable. Let's assume that it is countable. We take a segment of length one, renumber these points, and cover the points with intervals - the first point with an interval of one-tenth length, the second with one-hundredth, and so on. It turns out that we have covered all the points of a segment of length one with intervals, the sum of the lengths of which is certainly no more than one-ninth. This is a very clear reasoning. For beginners, it's amazingly compelling, even if it's not rigorous. We must also prove that the sum of the lengths of these intervals covering the segment must be greater than the length of the segment itself. But this inaccuracy is honest, because it can be proven. You can prove this theorem separately, in one lesson, and everything turns out fine. It’s unfair play if you refer to the obvious, but you can’t replace it with accurate reasoning. Therefore, Kiselev’s textbook is successful. There, for example, the existence of a perpendicular is proven by bending a sheet of paper. – Visually, understandably, obvious to everyone. - Yes. And still, there is correct evidence there later. – In this sense, I am very surprised, for example, in Pogorelov’s school textbook on geometry - there is no such axiom that the sum of two sides of a triangle is greater than the third. This is proven from some other axioms, although for me it is a clearer and simpler axiom - that a segment is the shortest distance. – And the approach with the axiom of the shortest distance is successful pedagogically, because everyone seems to know in advance that this is so.
