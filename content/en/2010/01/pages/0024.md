– And Serezha Markelov’s problem about a parallelepiped:

Given a box (rectangular parallelepiped), on the surface (but not inside) of which an ant crawls. Initially, the ant sits in the corner. Is it true that among all points on the surface, the opposite corner is located at the greatest distance from the ant? (The distance between points, from the ant’s point of view, is the length of the shortest path between these points passing along the surface of the parallelepiped.)

- It's great, yes. Markelov has several such tasks. Here is another beautiful task:

There are 6 pine trees growing on the shore of the round lake. It is known that if you take two triangles such that the vertices of one coincide with three of the pines, and the vertices of the other with three others, then in the middle of the segment connecting the points of intersection of the heights of these triangles, there is a treasure at the bottom of the lake. The only thing that is not known is how to divide these six points into two triplets. How many times will you have to dive to the bottom of the lake to be sure to find the treasure?

In the form of its presentation, it seems to be a combinatorics problem, but in fact it is a geometry problem. – An excellent problem was about card tricks. - Yes, definitely:

a) Two people perform a card trick. The first one removes five cards from a deck containing 52 cards (previously shuffled by someone from the audience), looks at them and then lays them out in a row from left to right, placing one of the cards face down and the rest face up. The second participant in the trick guesses the face-down card. Prove that they can agree in such a way that the second one will always guess the card. b) The second trick differs from the first in that the first participant lays out four cards from left to right, face up, and does not lay out one. In this case, can the participants in the trick agree so that the second one always guesses the unplayed card?

This problem is remarkable in that it was published in a weakened form (point a) by Martin Gardner, and Grisha Halperin came up with a strengthening, very natural (point b). Of course, the whole point is that the tasks should be bright. - Beautiful. Or so that they are unexpected. Or, as we discussed, it’s very cool when teaching to give schoolchildren problems in which an obvious answer suggests itself, but it is incorrect. – Obvious, but incorrect, yes. Each of the N passengers bought a ticket for an N-seat plane. The crazy old lady came in first and sat down in a random place. Further, each new entrant takes his place, if it is free; otherwise it takes random. What is the probability that the last passenger will take his seat?

- Yes, there is an unexpected answer. Let our readers solve these problems. – The problem about climbers – one of the Americans who came to us already put it in his book as a classic problem, with an unknown author. I said that I seem to be the author...

There is a mountain in the middle of the flat steppe. There are two paths leading to the top (we consider them graphs of continuous functions), which do not go below the level of the steppe. Two climbers simultaneously began their climb (along different paths), observing the condition: at each moment of time to be at the same height. Can climbers reach the top by moving continuously if a) the trails consist of a finite number of ascents and descents; b) in general?
