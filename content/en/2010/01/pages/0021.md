MATHEMATICAL WORLD

INTERVIEW WITH N.N. KONSTANTINOV

Nikolai Nikolaevich Konstantinov is an outstanding organizer of mathematical education, one of the creators of the system of mathematical classes in Moscow. The permanent leader of the only international mathematical competition of its kind - the Tournament of Cities, in which schoolchildren from all over the world take part. Full member of the Moscow Society of Natural Scientists, Moscow Mathematical Society. Represents the World Federation of National Mathematical Competitions in Europe. He teaches at Moscow school No. 179. Awarded the Paul Erdős International Mathematical Medal. Recently received the Russian Government Prize in the field of education for creating the Lomonosov Tournament. We asked him to tell us about himself, his work and his views on mathematics education. Nikolai Nikolaevich willingly responded to our request. – At school you were more interested in biology... – I had a passion for biology even before school. When I was seven years old, I planted strawberries for the first time and tried to make strawberries out of them. I didn't succeed. I convinced myself that the berries became a little larger when I watered them a lot. I asked in the library, already in evacuation, i.e. in 1941, - I was in the third grade, - a book about travel, and they gave me Darwin, “A Naturalist's Voyage Around the World on the Beagle.” In the preface of Professor S.L. Sobol it was written that acquired characteristics are not inherited. I still say: it’s one thing to learn something from a book, and quite another to experience it emotionally. I experienced this at a very susceptible age. It’s just funny to me when people start discussing something where they haven’t felt anything and come to pre-planned conclusions. Then I studied at the young naturalists station in Sokolniki, in the Botanical Garden of Moscow State University. There was a botanist, associate professor Potapov, who supervised me. And in the ninth grade I went to the best club, the strongest, we were taken to the departments of the Faculty of Biology. But this was the 1947/48 academic year, and in the summer of 1948 a disaster broke out with biology, Lysenko appeared, and so on. After that, it was useless to go to the biology department. They did not give books: it was forbidden to issue textbooks. Besides, I became interested in mathematics, and for me this was no longer a loss. So my passion for mathematics is kind of secondary, and I see some advantage in this. I always feel that the loss of mathematics for me is not the loss of everything. – What is the loss of everything? – Well, if biology, physics, and everything else had died for me, then it would have been a loss of everything. – That is, mathematics is one of several important things. - Yes, one of them. Let's say nothing works out in mathematics. Well, what to do, this is not a reason for suicide. – Why did you enter the physics department of Moscow State University? – Of course, I became interested in physics – precisely in the 10th grade, my senior year. Probably because mathematics seemed to me then a frivolous science. There are some bunnies jumping on the circles, red, green and white, and you also have to prove that there are an even number of red ones. All these tasks seemed like toys to me. It's strange to do this. Then I realized that these were, of course, exercises. But I think that people who abuse such children's stories do not fully understand psychology. Children like their activities to look grown-up. – Have you ever taken part in the Olympics? - In the seventh grade, a mathematics teacher - we had a very popular teacher, when he was replaced, the whole class was on strike - he recommended that I go to the Olympiad, but the recommendation looked like that, so I didn’t go. They fight and compete to see who can write this test better. I thought: “What idiots, they don’t have enough tests…” And I went there a year later because my neighbor on my desk went to a lecture at the university. It was a Sunday lecture on the topic of four-dimensional space. He returned from it completely crazy, his eyes were somewhere far away, and he kept telling me something that I couldn’t understand. We went to the next lecture together. This was a lecture by Isaac Moiseevich Yaglom on induction in geometry. It made an amazing impression on me because there were a lot of unexpected things there. Yaglom talks to those schoolchildren who are sitting directly in front of him and asks: is there such and such a theorem? - he must be a university professor, I think - and he doesn’t know what theorems are. Where did I end up?.. The schoolchildren who were sitting in front of him did not give him an intelligible answer. “Okay,” says Yaglom, “what’s the difference. Let's prove it." This completely shocked me. He doesn't know what theorems are, and he doesn't need to know. A completely unexpected thing for a student who is used to: completing the task - getting an A, and at the same time learning nothing. – And after the physics department you had the audacity to teach mathematicians? – Not only after the physics department - in my fifth year at the physics department, I announced a seminar on the theory of functions of a real variable at the Faculty of Mechanics and Mathematics. I’ve always had enough nerve, and I still have enough now. Classes at the university then began at 10 o'clock. Naturally: there was no metro. Getting to the Lenin Mountains was more difficult. Buses, long lines... And since we still had to find an audience, we scheduled classes for 8 am. There were about 15 first-year students studying, who always arrived at eight. Some of them later became very strong mathematicians. Among them were Kolya Rozov; Zhenya Golod, the strongest; Sasha Wentzel; Ira Vinogradova; Lenya Bokut. There were also people whose last names I forgot. The seminar was quite strong, it lasted one semester and ended with me reaching the limit of my knowledge. Then I had to dig something up myself.
