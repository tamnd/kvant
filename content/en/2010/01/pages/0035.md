It's interesting that...

...they learned to make durable sabers and blades from Damascus steel back in the Middle Ages. But the secret of making such steel was revealed quite recently, when an analysis of its structure made it possible to discover carbon nanotubes in it - giant cylindrical molecules made of carbon atoms, giving the materials unique elasticity and hardness. ...unlike natural diamonds, artificially created nanodiamonds have a number of special properties. Thus, their nanoparticles have significantly higher surface activity and reactivity (they have a larger specific surface area). Therefore, nanodiamonds are now used both in biology and medicine as adsorbents of toxic compounds and carriers of drugs for medicinal purposes. ... they learned long ago how to flatten gold into sheets 100 nanometers thick, and by chemical processing bring it to a thickness of 10 nanometers. However, it was impossible to get to the size of the atoms themselves in this way. And only with the advent of an electron microscope with a resolution of 0.12 nanometers was it possible, with a magnification of seven million times, to “see” rows of atoms in a gold crystal located at a distance of 0.235 nanometers from each other. ...the history of preparing colloidal solutions of gold, changing color from orange to green, or adding gold compounds to molten glass, giving it a bright red color (“golden ruby”), dates back to the distant past. The famous 16th century physician Paracelsus also mentioned the use of “soluble gold” in medicine. However, only in 1857 did Faraday prove that the bright color of colloidal solutions of gold is due to its small suspended particles. This was nanogold, completely different in its properties from ordinary gold.

...perhaps the sharpest object created by nature is the sting of a wasp - the radius of curvature of its tip does not exceed 10 nanometers. ...in his famous experiment on determining the size of molecules, Lord Rayleigh did it in a surprisingly simple way. Dripping oil into a basin of water, Rayleigh threw camphor pollen into it. Knowing that on the surface of the water the camphor particles move randomly, but on the surface of the oil they are calm, he selected the mass of the oil drop so that it covered the entire surface of the water and stopped the “dancing” of pollen. This was evidence that the oil spread into a monomolecular layer, the thickness of which, and therefore the size of the oil molecules, did not exceed 1.7 nanometers. ...superinvar - an alloy of iron and nickel with the addition of chromium - has an unusually low coefficient of thermal expansion. Thus, with an increase in temperature by one degree, a meter-long superinvar rod lengthens by only 30 nanometers. ...the electrical capacity of electrolytic capacitors can be very large due to the small distance between their plates, determined by a chemical film several nanometers thick. ...membrane technologies used to produce semi-permeable films have recently been supplemented with another method. Accelerated heavy nuclei irradiate synthetic films and turn them into “sieves” with neat holes, the size of which can be adjusted in the nanoscale. ...the so-called living molecular machines - bacterial flagella or elementary muscle units that ensure targeted cell movement and the transfer of molecules through membranes - have characteristic sizes of units or tens of nanometers. The diameter of the double helix of a DNA molecule is about two nanometers, and its length along the contour is about 400 nanometers. ...the prefix “nano” became so widespread that it began to be used to describe objects on a macroscopic scale. Thus, in accordance with the European classification of small satellites, devices weighing from one to ten kilograms are called “nanosatellites”.
