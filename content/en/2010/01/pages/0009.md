Life in Kyiv, moving to St. Petersburg (Leningrad)

Success was the result, as Delaunay said, of several thousand hours of intensive work. Boris Nikolaevich liked to add that solving a difficult mathematical problem differs from solving an Olympiad problem in that “an Olympiad problem requires 5 hours, and a problem requires 5 thousand hours.” It should be noted that he managed to achieve major success in the theory of Diophantine equations, despite the extremely difficult political and living conditions that existed in Kyiv at that time. During the First World War, Kyiv was occupied by German troops, and together with many other university professors, the Delaunay family traveled to Saratov in 1915–1916. Nikolai Chebotarev, who was four years younger, also came there from Kyiv. It was in Saratov that his communication with B.N. Delone turned out to be decisive for Chebotarev. When the Delaunays returned to Kyiv, a revolution and a bloody civil war began. Kyiv found itself at the center of these brutal events. One government replaced another continuously. German troops were driven out of Kyiv by the Red Army. The Reds, in turn, ceded the city to the Whites, the Whites were forced out again by the Reds, the Reds by the Yellow Poles, those by the Greens, the White Poles, etc. The political carousel in Kyiv, accompanied by terror, continued for several years. Delaunay often spoke, not without humor, about one episode from his life in those years. His younger brother Alexander - his family called him Alik - was an officer in Denikin's army. Once, when the Whites hastily surrendered the city to the Reds, Alik, dressed in civilian clothes, was forced to flee Kyiv. But the time was cruel and merciless, execution, as they say, on the spot was commonplace. At that time, Boris Nikolaevich worked as a mathematics teacher in a gymnasium and an assistant professor at the Kiev Polytechnic Institute. In 1920, he wrote a large work on cubic Diophantine equations and sent a beautifully designed manuscript as a doctoral dissertation to St. Petersburg, then Petrograd University. The commission under the leadership of $А.А.Маркова^{4}$ highly appreciated the dissertation, and in 1922 B.N. Delaunay was invited to Petrograd University as a professor. This was indeed very honorable, since the university was the “home” of the famous St. Petersburg school of number theory, the seeds of which were sown by Euler and which flourished in the second half of the 19th century under the leadership of P.L. Chebyshev. During the time of Chebyshev, the core of this school was A.A. Markov, A.N. Korkin, E.I. Zolotarev, A.A. Lyapunov. At that time, in accordance with the traditions of the famous number-theoretic school, its outstanding representative Georgy Voronoi “dressed” his exclusively geometric work on parallelohedrons in “analytical clothes”. In contrast to this tradition, Delaunay, with an undoubted gift for geometrics, spent many years geometrizing several important algebraic works, including the Voronoi algorithm for calculating the fundamental unit in a ring corresponding to the cubic form of the negative discriminant. In this and other works he interpreted the ring of cubic irrationalities as an integer lattice with a natural multiplication rule. Here we complete the review of studies of algebraic questions using geometric methods in order to talk about one simple but important theory that Boris Nikolaevich began in the early 1920s - the so-called empty ball method and the associated partition of space into special polyhedra. Somewhere in the 1980s, after the death of B.N. Delaunay, these partitions were called “Delaunay triangulations”.
