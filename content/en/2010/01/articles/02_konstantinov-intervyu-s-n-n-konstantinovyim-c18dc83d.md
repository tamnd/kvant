INTERVIEW WITH N.N. KONSTANTINOV

Nikolai Nikolaevich Konstantinov is an outstanding organizer of mathematical education, one of the creators of the system of mathematical classes in Moscow. The permanent leader of the only international mathematical competition of its kind - the Tournament of Cities, in which schoolchildren from all over the world take part. Full member of the Moscow Society of Natural Scientists, Moscow Mathematical Society. Represents the World Federation of National Mathematical Competitions in Europe. He teaches at Moscow school No. 179. Awarded the Paul Erdős International Mathematical Medal. Recently received the Russian Government Prize in the field of education for creating the Lomonosov Tournament. We asked him to tell us about himself, his work and his views on mathematics education. Nikolai Nikolaevich willingly responded to our request. – At school you were more interested in biology... – I had a passion for biology even before school. When I was seven years old, I planted strawberries for the first time and tried to make strawberries out of them. I didn't succeed. I convinced myself that the berries became a little larger when I watered them a lot. I asked in the library, already in evacuation, i.e. in 1941, - I was in the third grade, - a book about travel, and they gave me Darwin, “A Naturalist's Voyage Around the World on the Beagle.” In the preface of Professor S.L. Sobol it was written that acquired characteristics are not inherited. I still say: it’s one thing to learn something from a book, and quite another to experience it emotionally. I experienced this at a very susceptible age. It’s just funny to me when people start discussing something where they haven’t felt anything and come to pre-planned conclusions. Then I studied at the young naturalists station in Sokolniki, in the Botanical Garden of Moscow State University. There was a botanist, associate professor Potapov, who supervised me. And in the ninth grade I went to the best club, the strongest, we were taken to the departments of the Faculty of Biology. But this was the 1947/48 academic year, and in the summer of 1948 a disaster broke out with biology, Lysenko appeared, and so on. After that, it was useless to go to the biology department. They did not give books: it was forbidden to issue textbooks. Besides, I became interested in mathematics, and for me this was no longer a loss. So my passion for mathematics is kind of secondary, and I see some advantage in this. I always feel that the loss of mathematics for me is not the loss of everything. – What is the loss of everything? – Well, if biology, physics, and everything else had died for me, then it would have been a loss of everything. – That is, mathematics is one of several important things. - Yes, one of them. Let's say nothing works out in mathematics. Well, what to do, this is not a reason for suicide. – Why did you enter the physics department of Moscow State University? – Of course, I became interested in physics – precisely in the 10th grade, my senior year. Probably because mathematics seemed to me then a frivolous science. There are some bunnies jumping on the circles, red, green and white, and you also have to prove that there are an even number of red ones. All these tasks seemed like toys to me. It's strange to do this. Then I realized that these were, of course, exercises. But I think that people who abuse such children's stories do not fully understand psychology. Children like their activities to look grown-up. – Have you ever taken part in the Olympics? - In the seventh grade, a mathematics teacher - we had a very popular teacher, when he was replaced, the whole class was on strike - he recommended that I go to the Olympiad, but the recommendation looked like that, so I didn’t go. For example, I want to prove that the set of points on a segment is uncountable. Let's assume that it is countable. We take a segment of length one, renumber these points, and cover the points with intervals - the first point with an interval of one-tenth length, the second with one-hundredth, and so on. It turns out that we have covered all the points of a segment of length one with intervals, the sum of the lengths of which is certainly no more than one-ninth. This is a very clear reasoning. For beginners, it's amazingly compelling, even if it's not rigorous. We must also prove that the sum of the lengths of these intervals covering the segment must be greater than the length of the segment itself. But this inaccuracy is honest, because it can be proven. You can prove this theorem separately, in one lesson, and everything turns out fine. It’s unfair play if you refer to the obvious, but you can’t replace it with accurate reasoning.
