Diophantine equations of the third degree

At the same time, B.N. Delone began research on the theory of Diophantine equations of the third degree with two unknowns. The series of works on the theory of cubic Diophantine equations turned out to be, by Boris Nikolaevich’s own admission, the best achievement in his entire mathematical career. The Diophantine equation is an equation of the form

$p ( x_{1}$, … $, x_{n} ) = 0$,

(1) where $p ( x_{1}$, … $, x_{n} )$ is a polynomial with integer coefficients, for which it is necessary to find integer, sometimes rational, solutions. Thus, the well-known equation $x^{2} + y^{2} = z^{2}$, in which we need to find all integer solutions, is an example of a 2nd degree equation with three unknowns. It has infinitely many integer solutions, well known as Pythagorean triples because they correspond to right triangles with integer sides: (3,4,5), (5,12,13) ​​and $т.д.^{3}$ In one of the 23 famous problems, namely the 10th problem, Hilbert posed the question: is there an algorithm that allows one to determine from the coefficients of equation (1) whether a solution to this equation exists or not? Now, after the work of Yu.V. Matiyasevich, who solved Hilbert’s 10th problem (1970), it is known for sure that such an algorithm does not exist. It must be said that specialists in Diophantine equations, who knew very well how difficult every step in this field was, always doubted the existence of such an algorithm. Life in Kyiv, moving to St. Petersburg (Leningrad)

Success was the result, as Delaunay said, of several thousand hours of intensive work. How is internal organs visualized using NMR?

Until now we have implicitly assumed that, neglecting the influence of weak electron currents in the coils, the magnetic field in which the nuclei are placed is uniform, i.e. has the same value at all points. In 1973, Paul Latterbourg proposed conducting NMR studies by placing a sample in a magnetic field that varies from point to point. It is clear that in this case the resonant frequency for the nuclei under study changes from point to point, which makes it possible to judge their spatial location. And since the intensity of the signal from a certain region of space is proportional to the number of hydrogen atoms in this region, we obtain information about the distribution of the density of matter in space. Actually, this is the principle of the NMR research technique. As you can see, the principle is simple, although in order to obtain real images of internal organs in practice, it was necessary to obtain powerful computers for controlling radio frequency pulses and for a long time to improve the methodology for creating the necessary magnetic field profiles and processing NMR signals obtained from the coils. Let's imagine that along the x-axis there are small spheres filled with water (Fig.3). If the magnetic field does not depend on x, then a single signal appears (see Fig.3,a). Let us further assume that by means of additional coils (relative to the one that creates the main magnetic field directed along the z axis), we create an additional magnetic field $B_{0}$, varying along the x axis, and its value increases from left to right. It is clear that for spheres with different coordinates, the NMR signal will now correspond to different frequencies and the measured spectrum will contain five characteristic peaks (see Fig.3, b). The height of these peaks will be proportional to the number of spheres (i.e. the mass of water) having the corresponding coordinate, and thus, in the case under consideration, the intensities of the peaks will be in the ratio 3:1:3:1:1. Knowing the magnitude of the magnetic field gradient (i.e., the rate of its change along the x axis), we can represent the measured frequency spectrum as a dependence of the density of hydrogen atoms on the x coordinate. In this case, it will be possible to say that where the peaks are higher, the number of hydrogen atoms is greater: in our example, the numbers of hydrogen atoms corresponding to the positions of the spheres actually correlate as 3:1:3:1:1. The human eye is a sensitive sensor of electromagnetic radiation in the visible range. Fortunately or unfortunately, radiation originating from internal organs does not reach our eyes - we see human bodies only from the outside. At the same time, as we just discussed, under certain conditions, the atomic nuclei of the internal organs of the human body can emit electromagnetic waves in the radio frequency range (i.e., frequencies much lower than for visible light), and the frequency varies slightly depending on the point of emission. It cannot be seen with the eye, so such radiation is recorded using complex equipment and then collected into a single image using special computer processing. And yet, we are talking about a completely real vision of the inside of an object or a human body. Humanity has achieved such amazing success thanks to a number of fundamental achievements of scientific thought: quantum mechanics with its theory of the magnetic moment, the theory of the interaction of radiation with matter, digital electronics, mathematical algorithms for signal conversion, and computer technology. The advantages of NMR imaging compared to other diagnostic methods are numerous and significant. The operator can easily select which sections of the patient's body to scan, and can also examine several sections of the selected organ simultaneously. In particular, by selecting magnetic field gradients appropriately, it is possible to obtain vertical cross-sectional images of the insides of our skull. This may be a central section or sections offset to the right or left. (Such studies are virtually impossible with X-ray radiography.) The operator can “narrow” the field of view by visualizing NMR signals originating from only one selected organ or only one part of it, thereby increasing the resolution of the image. Thus, in photonic crystals, band gaps appear - regions of light frequencies in which light cannot penetrate inside the photonic crystal and is completely reflected from it. The emergence of nanostructured materials actually opens up a new direction in the creation of optical media. The required optical properties of the material are now achieved not by selecting the optimal chemical composition (as was the case in the old approach), but by creating a geometric or phase structure with a characteristic size not exceeding several hundred nanometers. Since nanostructured materials are created artificially, they are often called metamaterials.

Magnetized photonic crystals If a nanostructured material contains magnetic substances, then one can expect that it will exhibit magneto-optical effects similar to those that arise in ordinary homogeneous media, but perhaps slightly modified. The idea of ​​using magnetic substances to control light in a photonic crystal was first proposed in the late 90s of the last century by Japanese scientists. They examined the Faraday effect in one-dimensional photonic crystals, which are multilayer films of randomly alternating layers of bismuth-substituted yttrium ferrite garnet and quartz. For certain radiation frequencies with optimally selected structure parameters, an increase in the Faraday effect by more than 300 times was found compared to a similar homogeneous medium. Using the one-dimensional case as an example, several types of magnetic photonic crystals can be distinguished. First of all, these are standard systems consisting of alternating quarter-wave (thickness equal to one-fourth the wavelength of light in the substance) magnetic (for example, cerium-substituted yttrium ferrite garnet) and non-magnetic (for example, gadolinium gallium garnet) layers. Such photonic crystals have a band gap centered at the design wavelength, i.e. do not transmit light with a wavelength in a certain area around a given one. By design wavelength we mean the wavelength of light outside the crystal at which one-fourth of the wavelength fits in each of its layers. figure 3a and b show the transmittance and Faraday angle for a one-dimensional photonic crystal tuned to the near-infrared range (design wavelength 1.55 μm). The crystal consists of 30 pairs of magnetic and non-magnetic layers. It turns out that it is at these wavelengths that the group speed of light increases sharply. This leads to an increase in the effective time of interaction of the wave with the magnetization of the material, which means the Faraday effect increases. An important feature of resonances at boundary frequencies is that the transmission and Faraday rotation maxima practically coincide. This makes it possible to use photonic crystals as miniature elements that rotate the plane of polarization at large angles.
