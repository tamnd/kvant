Selected Tournament Challenges

1 (6–8). The product of two consecutive natural numbers can end in 1000. For example, $1000 \cdot 1001 = 1001000$ or $8999 \cdot 9000 = 8999 \cdot 9 \cdot 1000 = =...1000.$ a) Can the product of four consecutive natural numbers end in 1000? b) Can the product of six consecutive natural numbers end in 1000?

D. Shnol

2 (6–7). Draw a 6-gon and draw a line through its two vertices, which divides it into two pentagons.

V. Hurowitz

G. Galperin

4. a) (6–8) There were coins in 10 wallets. The denominations of any two coins from the same wallet differed by no more than 1 berendyk. The coins were mixed and placed in an opaque bag. Sasha doesn’t know anything about the coins in advance. He takes one coin out of the bag, looks at it and places it in one of the 19 available boxes. Once you put a coin in a box, it can no longer be moved. Prove that Sasha can act in such a way that in each box the denominations of any two coins differ by no more than 1 berendyk. b) (7–8) There were coins in 10 wallets in such a way that the weights of any two coins from one wallet differed by no more than 1 g (the masses of the coins may not be whole). The coins were mixed and placed in an opaque bag. Sasha knows nothing in advance about the masses of coins. He takes one coin out of the bag, weighs it, then puts the coin into one of the available 20 boxes, takes out the next coin, and so on. (Having put a coin in a box, it can no longer be moved.) Prove that Sasha can act in such a way that in each box the masses of any two coins differ by no more than 1 g. c) The condition of the problem is the same as in part b), but there are 19 boxes.

A. Shapovalov

5 (6–7). For two eight-digit numbers, the products of the digits are positive and equal. In each of the numbers, all the digits are different. Prove that these numbers have equal sums of digits.

A. Shapovalov

6 (8–9). a) For what smallest n is there an n-gon that can be cut into 13 equal parts?
