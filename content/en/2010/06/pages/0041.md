The cleanliness of the surface of the electrodes plays an important role in this case, therefore, before carrying out experiments, it is necessary to carefully remove the oxide film from the surface of the electrodes using fine sandpaper. Removing the oxide film will also reduce the so-called polarization effect, which reduces the actual emf of the source. From Ohm's law for a complete circuit it follows that the current strength is maximum in the source short circuit mode, when the external load resistance tends to zero. A milliammeter has a negligible internal resistance, and when the device is connected directly to the terminals of a fruit battery, one can speak with great accuracy about a short circuit. In this case, the current strength must be measured as quickly as possible, since the source quickly discharges. The lack of current inside a disconnected fruit battery is explained by the peculiarities of redox chemical reactions, due to which voltage appears on the electrodes. As a result of a chemical reaction under the influence of fruit juice, zinc ions leave the surface of the zinc electrode, and the zinc electrode acquires a negative potential relative to the solution. The chemical reaction does not depend on the presence of another electrode in the juice. A similar reaction occurs with a copper electrode, but, according to the electrochemical series, with less intensity. Thus, a potential difference is established between the zinc and copper electrodes. When the source terminals are shorted, electrons flow through the external circuit from the zinc to the copper. That is why in all cases the copper electrode was the anode, and the zinc electrode was the cathode.

The removal of ions of the corresponding chemical element from the metal during a chemical reaction inevitably leads to the destruction of the electrodes, which in this case cannot be reversible. This circumstance makes it impossible to recharge fruit batteries and, thus, the impossibility of manufacturing batteries based on them. The maximum power of a fruit battery is small and amounts to tenths of a milliwatt. Therefore, the fruit battery can be used to power only low-power devices, such as an electronic watch, calculator, low-power LED, etc. Take, for example, a calculator and your battery. If the calculator has a built-in solar battery, it must be covered with opaque paper. Connect two sources from a lemon or an apple in series into a battery and connect a calculator to it (observing the polarity). You will see that the calculator is working. figure 3 shows a photograph of an electronic thermometer powered by a battery of current sources made from two apples. To power more powerful devices, it will be necessary to connect fruit current sources to a battery. Think about how many fruit batteries are needed and how they need to be connected to power an Aspire One netbook (with specifications of 19 V and 1.58 A) or a cell phone and whether you can save on the number of fruits by connecting them into a battery. You can continue experimenting with fruit batteries yourself. Try, say, to establish the dependence of the voltage at the terminals and the power of the element on the surface area of ​​​​the electrodes, or try to plot the discharge characteristics of various fruit sources.
