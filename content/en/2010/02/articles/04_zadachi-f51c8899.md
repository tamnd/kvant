Ice patterns on glass

Ice on the glass. In winter, bus windows are often covered with frost or painted with frosty patterns on the inside. Their appearance depends on the temperature and humidity in the cabin. At low humidity and gradual cooling from 0 to –6 $\infty^{C}$, a uniform layer of opaque loose ice is deposited on the surface of the glass. In this case, the entire glass is below the dew point, and moisture from the air simultaneously condenses across the entire surface of the glass, covering it layer by layer and forming an amorphous, opaque state of ice. If the glass is cooled at high air humidity, and during the cooling process the dew point is passed, then the water condensing on the glass crystallizes in the form of frosty patterns, similar to spruce paws or branches of strange plants, while taking away heat from nearby areas of the glass. These patterns are called dendrites. With further cooling of the glass, a thin layer of frost is deposited between the dendrites, and then on the dendrites themselves. Sometimes the temperature and humidity are such that the wiped glass freezes right before your eyes, becoming covered with ice crystals. Somehow, after a thaw, it suddenly became colder, and patterns of larger dendrites covered the lower part of the window glass, while at the top the crystals were noticeably smaller. Why? It's not difficult to explain. Crystals are formed from water condensed on glass, and due to gravity, it flows and accumulates below. In winter, cold air can enter the bus interior through frame cracks and open windows. The warm and humid air of the cabin comes into contact with it, which at the same time cools, losing excess moisture. Why don’t windows fog up and freeze where outside air penetrates? In this case, two processes occur inside the cabin: not only the warm air is cooled, but also the cold air, entering the cabin, heats up and absorbs water, as its moisture capacity increases. This is easy to confirm experimentally.
