It is possible, with a small amount of imagination, to “recognize” the person depicted (familiar to us from portraits). Against a light background of fog, the entire figure of a person and his face naturally look dark. The maximum relative brightness of spots on the face is determined by the light reflectance coefficient and is about 0.5. There are also darker areas of the face image on the retina - up to a contrast of 1.0, which is defined as the ratio of the difference between the maximum and minimum brightness of the spots to the maximum brightness. You can express this value as a percentage, then the maximum contrast will be 100%. The range from 0.5 to 0.1 in brightness (or from 0 to 80% in contrast) can be divided into several parts. Moreover, for reliable recognition of details, it is necessary that at the boundaries of the spots the brightness changes abruptly by a noticeable amount. If the change in brightness is 20%, then only four levels are enough to “fill” the entire range (80/20 = 4 = 2 bits). As a result, the minimum amount of information (in bits) required to recognize a person's face is approximately $30 \times 30 \times 2 = 1,8 \times 10^{3}$. Then, in the absence of fog and in normal lighting, a person with a characteristic head size of 20 cm can be recognized by his face from a distance equal to 0.3 m $\times ( 20$ cm 4 cm $) \times \times ( 800 30 ) = 40$ m. Here 0.3 m is the distance of best vision, the multiplier (20 cm/4 cm) ensures the “placement” of the image of the person’s face on the central fovea, and the multiplier (800/30) “adjusts” the size cone images up to magnitude $30 \times 30$. In the problem statement, the indicated distance – 10 steps or 7 m – is much less than 40 m, i.e. It is not this circumstance (reducing the size of the image on the retina) that prevents you from recognizing a person in the fog. The lesion on the retina decreases with distance from the object of observation, gradually decreasing to zero. This is due to two processes occurring simultaneously. Firstly, as you move away from the source, the light gradually dissipates, but this is not the most important thing. And the main thing is that (secondly) as the thickness of the fog layer between the light source and the eye increases, the so-called backlight increases. The share of light going directly into the observer's eyes from the light source (photograph) decreases, and the share of light from other sources, scattered by drops of fog and also falling into the eyes, increases. In the series of photographs (Fig.3) given to illustrate this phenomenon (backlighting), it is clearly visible that the brightness of the surroundings does not change, the light spots in the pictures become even lighter, and the dark spots gradually brighten until both are comparable in brightness to the surroundings. The transition from the first pair of photographs (on the left) to the second and so on (on the right) corresponds to an increase in the “density” of the fog by a certain number of times. Gradually, the photographs become so light that it is difficult to recognize anyone by their faces, but the captions to the pictures are still legible. This suggests that sharp, dark lines are important for recognizing images, particularly people's faces. Such lines characterize the shape of the eyes, eyebrows, mouth, and nose. It is by these lines that we recognize those depicted in figure or in the photograph. Let's return to our task. When they talk about recognition at a distance, they usually assume that “one glance” is enough for this operation. Therefore, we will make the task easier by specifying 20% ​​as the minimum contrast. Fog droplets with a diameter of 10 microns are significantly larger than the lengths of all light waves visible to the eye, so we can assume that the scattering of light does not depend on the wavelength, i.e. Light of all colors is scattered equally by such balls and is practically not absorbed. Since light falls on the droplets between two people from all directions, each droplet is a spherical light source with a certain brightness or luminous intensity. A white wall of fog is a solid wall of such balls, since no matter in which direction we look, the line of sight at some distance will definitely “hit” one of the balls. If on the way to the observation object 80% of the image area is covered with balls, then in this case the contrast of the visible image will not exceed 20%. Thus, it will be possible to find a connection between the sizes of droplet balls $D_{к}$, their concentration in the air $n_{к}$ and the distance L at which it is impossible to recognize a person in the fog.
