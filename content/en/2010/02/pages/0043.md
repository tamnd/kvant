Ice patterns on glass

Ice on the glass. In winter, bus windows are often covered with frost or painted with frosty patterns on the inside. Their appearance depends on the temperature and humidity in the cabin. At low humidity and gradual cooling from 0 to –6 $\infty^{C}$, a uniform layer of opaque loose ice is deposited on the surface of the glass. In this case, the entire glass is below the dew point, and moisture from the air simultaneously condenses across the entire surface of the glass, covering it layer by layer and forming an amorphous, opaque state of ice. If the glass is cooled at high air humidity, and during the cooling process the dew point is passed, then the water condensing on the glass crystallizes in the form of frosty patterns, similar to spruce paws or branches of strange plants, while taking away heat from nearby areas of the glass. These patterns are called dendrites. With further cooling of the glass, a thin layer of frost is deposited between the dendrites, and then on the dendrites themselves. Sometimes the temperature and humidity are such that the wiped glass freezes right before your eyes, becoming covered with ice crystals. Somehow, after a thaw, it suddenly became colder, and patterns of larger dendrites covered the lower part of the window glass, while at the top the crystals were noticeably smaller. Why? It's not difficult to explain. Crystals are formed from water condensed on glass, and due to gravity, it flows and accumulates below. In winter, cold air can enter the bus interior through frame cracks and open windows. The warm and humid air of the cabin comes into contact with it, which at the same time cools, losing excess moisture. Why don’t windows fog up and freeze where outside air penetrates? In this case, two processes occur inside the cabin: not only the warm air is cooled, but also the cold air, entering the cabin, heats up and absorbs water, as its moisture capacity increases. This is easy to confirm experimentally.

⟦column⟧

If you wear glasses, you know (though everyone knows this) that in winter, when you enter a warm room from the street, they fog up. But few people paid attention to the following: as soon as you go out into the cold again without wiping your glasses, the moisture will completely evaporate from them. Do this experiment with a piece of glass, which is best held not in your hand, but by some kind of frame. Let me give you a few more observations. Once I breathed on an area wiped in a frozen window, and it quickly became covered with fluffy frost, with longer needles than on the rest of the glass. Another time, at an outside temperature of -25 $\infty^{C}$, a thicker layer of frost grew on the wiped area in 5–7 minutes than on the unwiped glass, and this time there was no need to breathe on the glass. Apparently, the process of building up a layer of frost on the glass depends on a combination of certain conditions: temperature and humidity in the cabin and outside the window. Ice crystals appeared on the glass, growing before our eyes, in the form of ice fibers (trichites) or ice patterns in the form of spruce paws (dendrites). Interestingly, during growth they were surrounded by a small frost-free area of ​​glass. As expected earlier, the released energy of crystallizing water affects the conditions in the space surrounding the dendrite, preventing the formation of other crystals near and around the growing ones. According to scientists' measurements, the deposition of ice on the surface is accompanied by temperature fluctuations near it of up to +5 $\infty^{C}$, which influences the existence in this area of ​​noticeable heat release and its transfer by convection. This is confirmed by the following observation of an electric train standing in the depot. The train's window glass was double, the outside air temperature was about –12 $\infty^{C}$. On the outer surface of the outer glass there were grains of ice - from frozen rare drops of water. The inner glass on the passenger side was covered with a layer of white frost with round spots of clean glass, the centers of which coincided with the location of frozen drops outside.
