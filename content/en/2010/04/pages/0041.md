IN “QUANTUM” No. 3 FOR 2000, A SMALL note by I. Akulich “Walks of the King” WAS PRINTED. In it, the author posed several interesting open questions, including this one: What is the greatest possible length of a closed path for a king to go around the chessboard $8 \times 8$? More precisely, let the king walk around the chessboard, visiting each square once, and with his last move return to the original square. If you connect the centers of the fields that he sequentially passed through segments, you will get a closed broken line (most likely, self-intersecting). What is the greatest possible length of this broken line? It is clear that the broken line consists of segments of two types - short (length 1) and long (length 2). Therefore, it is enough to find the minimum possible number of short moves for the king. This is what we will do. In fact, we will solve the problem in the general case - for boards $2 n \times 2 n$ (the problem for boards with an odd side length is solved, as we will soon see, quite simply). Consider the chessboard $2 n \times 2 n$ and construct an auxiliary board called the Welch board. To do this, remove all the cells of the same color from the original board, and replace each cell of a different color with a square with a side length 2 times larger, which is rotated by $45^\circ$ around the center of the cell (Fig.1).

If the original chessboard has dimensions $( 2 n + 1 ) \times ( 2 n + 1 )$, then on one of the colored Welch boards there will be more cells of one color than the other, and by no less than 2n + 1. This means that it will take at least 2n + 1 continuous pieces of path to visit all the cells of one board. Therefore, there will be at least 4n + 2 jumps from one board to another (two jumps for each piece of the path). So, if the chessboard has an odd size, then the king, going around the board along a closed path, will make at least 4n + 2 short moves. This is a large enough number that constructing a corresponding example path is not very difficult.

If the chessboard is of even size, then each of the Welch boards contains equal numbers of white and black squares. But their location will give us the key to the solution. Let the rook, when moving along the Welch board, leave a trace - a line connecting the centers of the squares that the rook successively visits. When the rook jumps (to another board), the line is interrupted, but then a new one will begin. If the rook has visited all the squares, then several such lines “cover” the entire board. Let us mentally cut the board shown in figure 2 into two identical parts along the segment AB. This segment divides some lines into parts. Note that the bottom half of the board contains n more black cells than white ones. This means that there are at least n lines or parts thereof that start and end in a black cell. Similarly, looking at the top half, we find that there are at least n lines or parts thereof that start and end in a white square. In total there are at least 2n lines or their parts. But gluing parts of lines along segment AB will reduce their number by no more than n (after all, there are only n possible gluing places).
