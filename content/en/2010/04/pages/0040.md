Then you just need to increase the density of the water by a little more than 0.02%, and the stick will turn over to a horizontal position. Recall that the density of sea water is 1.03 g $см^{3}$, which is 3% greater than the density of fresh water. This means that achieving an increase in the density of water by just a few hundredths of a percent does not seem to be a difficult task - you just need to salt the water! This effect is reliably observed, and you can easily repeat it. Now let's describe the experiment itself. We use a half-liter glass jar as a vessel, and tap water as a liquid. We have already made a stick; its average density is as close as possible to the density of fresh water. The center of gravity of the stick is shifted to one of the ends so that its equilibrium position in fresh water is vertical. Now carefully pour a tablespoon of fine table salt into the water along the wall of the vessel. After about a minute, a white cloud begins to slowly rise from the bottom of the vessel. Halfway through

minutes it reaches the surface of the water and after a few seconds dissipates without a trace. After another 1–3 minutes, the stick begins to rotate from its vertical position and after 10–20 seconds it takes a horizontal position. Attention! An artifact is possible (from the Latin artefactum - artificially made). The fact is that the cause of the stick turning over may be a white cloud - it contains many air bubbles, which, sticking to the stick and thus making it lighter, will lead to the turning effect. This side effect is reliably observed if a lot of salt is poured into water at once. It is very easy to verify this. It is enough to knock down the bubbles stuck to the stick by rinsing it in the same water, and it will float vertically again. But after some time, when, due to diffusion, the density of the upper layers of water reaches a critical value, the stick will definitely turn over.

Wet water spots

To answer the question posed, let's conduct a simple experiment. Place a dry paper napkin on the book, and then wet part of the napkin with clean water (Fig.1). We will see that the wet napkin will become translucent and through it it will be possible to read the previously invisible text on the cover of the book and examine the pattern of the fabric on which the book lies. You can explain what you saw, for example, as follows. Water penetrates the napkin and saturates it using capillary forces. In this case, the fibers that make up the napkin are lengthened (as happens, for example,

This is why water spots will appear darker in reflected light. The same dark spots can be left by other colorless transparent liquids, such as glycerin.
