The simple statistical hypothesis of horizontal homogeneity and isotropy has been used in meteorology for about half a century. However, the accumulation of archives of meteorological measurements, the growth of computing capabilities and the development of new computational algorithms have led to its revision. It turned out that anisotropy in horizontal variables, for example, temperatures, is significantly manifested in the tropical zone (Fig.11). The statistical interpolation algorithm, which takes into account the anisotropy of the interpolated field, is somewhat more complicated than the isotropic version.

In turn, knowledge of the fields of average and correlation functions of meteorological fields makes it possible to debug numerical climate models, which have been quite popular in recent decades. Until the correlation functions generated by these models are close to the true correlation functions estimated from observational archives, such models should not be trusted. This article was not able to explain exactly how daily observational data $^{24}$ is used for weather forecasting: what evolutionary differential equations of hydrodynamic type are solved, what physical processes they describe, what computational algorithms are used. This requires a separate book and good mathematical preparation of the reader. The first numerical weather forecast was made by the Scot L. Richardson about a century ago. The computer staff was armed with slide rules. The computational work on the 6 o'clock weather forecast for Europe lasted several years and resulted in a monstrously erroneous forecast. Another quarter of a century required “work on Richardson’s mistakes,” in which both mathematicians (R. Courant, K. Friedrichs, G. Levy) and “mathematicians with a meteorological bias” (G. Rossby, I. A. Kibel) took part. When scientists (and their computers) had some free time at Los Alamos after the production of the atomic bomb, they took up the task of weather forecasting. Over the past sixty years, the quality of forecasts has steadily improved. Humanity has begun to enjoy the fruits of its centuries-long investment in meteorology. But that's another story...
