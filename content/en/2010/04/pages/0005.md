The follower turned out to be third-year student Arnold. And in April 1957, a student’s notebook in a cage lay on Kolmogorov’s desk, on which it was written that this was the course work of a third-year student V. Arnold. In this course work, Hilbert's thirteenth problem was solved. This was Arnold's first work, which made his name known throughout the mathematical world. And then a series of discoveries began, the listing of which would take several pages. In his thesis, Arnold further developed one Kolmogorov method in the theory of dynamical systems. This method was further developed in the works of the outstanding mathematician Jürgen Moser. The theory constructed by these three mathematicians was called the “KAM theory” - the Kolmogorov–Arnold–Moser theory. It has found numerous applications in mathematics, mechanics, cosmology and physics. Arnold transformed entire mathematical fields. For example, the “theory of singularities.” In philosophy, for a long time, the idea has been expressed that “with the transition from quantity to quality,” evolutionary processes often make leaps. The “theory of revolutions” was largely built on this. Jump processes began to be intensively studied in the first third of the twentieth century. One of the founders of the new direction, the French mathematician Rene Thom, proposed the name “catastrophe theory” for it. In the works of Arnold, this theory received outstanding development. Always avoiding unjustified advertising, V.I. Arnold calls this direction “the theory of singularities.” Arnold far advanced a special branch of modern geometry, the so-called symplectic geometry, and laid the foundation for a new direction in topology, which received fundamental development - symplectic topology. Arnold's contribution to algebraic geometry was significant. He established connections between Hilbert's sixteenth problem on ovals of real algebraic curves and four-dimensional topology, as well as connections between algebraic geometry and braid theory. Vladimir Igorevich was an outstanding geometer, he studied algebra and number theory, combinatorics, logic and the foundations of mathematics, in a word, his work covered almost all areas of modern mathematics. His achievements in natural science are great - in hydrodynamics, cosmology, potential theory. He had fruitful contacts with the greatest physicists of his time - N.N. Bogolyubov, V.L. Ginzburg, Ya.B. Zeldovich. With passion and conviction, V.I. Arnold developed and propagated Poincaré’s ideas that mathematics is a part of natural science. Arnold served his profession and education in every possible field. He founded an outstanding mathematical scientific school, wrote many wonderful textbooks (his textbook on classical mechanics is compared to the greatest work of scientific literature - Newton's "Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy", monographs and review articles devoted to problems of mathematics. Beginning with the leadership of the famous school mathematical circle in the fifties (you can read about what happened in the section of this circle he headed in Arnold’s article published in this issue of the magazine), V.I. Arnold paid a lot of attention to direct work with schoolchildren. In 1963, he participated in the first summer mathematics school, and for the last ten years he spent every year at

youth schools in Dubna. The course given by Vladimir Igorevich for the first graduates of the Physics and Mechanics School (now the A.N. Kolmogorov Scientific Research Center) became a pedagogical masterpiece. On the initiative of V.I. Arnold, the Moscow Center for Continuing Mathematical Education and the Independent Moscow University were created. The influence of V.I. Arnold on the entire mathematical world was enormous. V.I. Arnold was awarded many titles, doctorates and awards. Among the prizes are the Moscow Mathematical Society Prize (1958, of which Vladimir Igorevich was especially proud), the Lenin Prize (1965, together with A.N. Kolmogorov), the Crafoord Prize of the Swedish Academy of Sciences (1982), the Technion Harvey Prize (1994), the Wolf Prize (2001), the Rhun Prize Zhunshou (2005) (it is called the Nobel Prize of the East), State Prize of the Russian Federation (2008). The general talent of his personality was manifested in his most diverse reactions and hobbies. He was an unusually flamboyant guide to Paris. He went on long (about a hundred kilometers) bicycle rides in the Moscow region and in the outskirts of Paris. At the same time, he also conducted topographical surveys here, recording places where there were clearings with mushrooms, sorrel or strawberries, or bushes with raspberries. And when the time came, he set off forty kilometers from home to his once chosen places with a backpack, which was filled with “gifts of the forest.” In winter, Arnold went skiing, sometimes up to a hundred kilometers. He also loved long voyages and went hiking, including in the mountains. He read unusually a lot and remembered a lot of what he read in detail, which made him an unusually interesting interlocutor, and among his interlocutors were the greatest scientists of our time. Along the way, he also made humanitarian discoveries (his discovery of the connection between the epigraph to Pushkin’s “Eugene Onegin” and the text from “Dangerous Liaisons” by Charles de Laclos caused general delight). Arnold left many remarkable autobiographical notes, which, I think, constitute only a small fraction of what could have been said to him. He wrote wonderful memoirs about Kolmogorov, Zeldovich, Bogolyubov. Arnold’s teacher, Andrei Nikolaevich Kolmogorov, gave a wonderful, timeless description of Arnold in his greeting to his student on his fiftieth birthday. At a time when Arnold did not have major academic titles, Kolmogorov expressed “his conviction that the first Soviet mathematician is being honored not only by the strength of the results obtained, but also by the temperament of the individual, by the ability to perceive new things and courage in overcoming obstacles.” The message from the teacher to the student surprisingly characterizes both: Vladimir Igorevich, whose personality and work received such an enthusiastic and insightful assessment, and Andrei Nikolaevich himself, who was on the verge of death, but retained a reserve of spiritual generosity and the ability to admire. This message is not taken as a farewell. This is a blessing! Vladimir Igorevich Arnold began his journey to immortality. Everything connected with him - extraordinary personal talent, creativity, service to humanity - makes his image unforgettable for everyone who was lucky enough to come into contact with him on their life's path. V. Tikhomirov
