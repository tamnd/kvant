Well-tempered clavier

I.GELFAND, A.SHEN

EVERYONE KNOWS THAT THE SAME MELODY CAN BE PLAYED IN DIFFERENT keys. But what do the words “the same” mean? To answer this question, let's start with another: what is a melody? Formally speaking, a melody is sounds of different pitches played one after another. What is pitch? From a physics point of view, sound is air vibrations. The pitch of the sound is determined by the vibration frequency, i.e. number of vibrations per second. A tuning fork vibrating 440 times per second (physicists say “with a frequency of 440 hertz”; the unit of frequency is named after the German physicist Heinrich Hertz) produces the A note of the first octave. To the ear, higher frequencies correspond to higher notes. Very low and very high sounds become inaudible. It is believed that a person can hear sounds in the range from 20 hertz to 20 kilohertz (although in fact these limits may vary from person to person; the range of audible frequencies decreases with age). Low-frequency sound that we cannot hear is sometimes called “infrasound,” and high-frequency sound is “ultrasound.” Dolphins talk to each other using ultrasounds. The frequency of the current in the electrical network is 50 hertz, i.e. 50 vibrations per second (this applies to Europe, in America - 60 hertz). If, due to a malfunction, the network background penetrates into the audio path of the tape recorder, a low hum is heard. Task 1. By pulling the string harder, we change the frequency of vibration. Do you think the frequency is increasing or decreasing? Try this with a taut thread. Task 2. By clamping a string (for example, a guitar) with a finger, we seem to reduce its length, almost without changing the tension. What do you think happens to the pitch of the sound? Problem 3. A 33-rpm record was set at 45; How will the frequency of all sounds recorded on it change? Problem 4. Where are the lower notes in the piano - on the left or on the right? How does this relate to the shape of the piano? Problem 5. The mosquito itches almost like a tuning fork. How many beats per second do its wings make? Task 6. Who do you think makes a higher sound - a mosquito or a big fly?

Chapter from the book: I. Gelfand, A. Shen. Algebra. – M.: MTsNMO, 2009.

⟦column⟧

It turns out that the ratio of the frequencies of neighboring sounds of a melody is primarily perceived by ear, and not the frequencies themselves. Only a few people with “perfect pitch” (not all musicians have it) can distinguish the note A from the note G played on the piano. However, almost every person, after a little practice, can easily distinguish the interval D-A (fifth, frequency ratio D: A = 2: 3) from D-S (quart, frequency ratio D: Sol = 3: 4). Task 7. Find the ratio of the frequencies of adjacent notes G and A using these data. Now we can tell which melodies sound the same (differing only in “key”) - these are those in which the frequency ratios are the same. Problem 8. The melody A-E-A (descending) consists of three notes with frequencies of 440, 330 and 220 hertz. What will the frequencies be if you play the same melody (with the same frequency ratios), starting with the note E (its frequency is 330 hertz)? ⊳ According to what has been said, it is necessary that

440: 330: 220 = 330: x: y.

Since

440: 330: 220 = 4: 3: 2, we get $y = 330 \cdot ( 1 2 ) = 165, x = 330 \cdot ( 3 4 ) = 247,5$. The corresponding notes are called mi-si-mi. ⊲ Looking at the piano keyboard, it is easy to notice that it is “periodic”: the same combinations of white and black keys are repeated - as they say, in “different octaves”. Notes separated by a period (an octave) have the same name and differ in frequency by exactly 2 times. Thus, the notes A in different octaves have frequencies

…55, 110, 220, 440, 880, 1760, …

forming a geometric progression with denominator 2. Problem 9. How many octaves can a piano have if all of them must be audible? (Consider that sounds are heard in the range from 20 to 20,000 hertz.) A geometric progression is formed not only by the notes A, but also by other notes of the same name: the notes G form another geometric progression (also with a denominator of 2), the notes F form a third, and so on. Problem 10. Looking at the piano keyboard (Fig.1), count how many progressions there are (how many notes in one octave). Answer. 12 (7 white keys and 5 black).
