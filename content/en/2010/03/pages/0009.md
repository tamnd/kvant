This equipment is expensive, but each individual measurement costs much less than launching a radiosonde. On figure you can see how the wind direction changes over time - at different heights, not at the same time. A typical front inclination angle is on the order of $1 1 ^\circ$. The direction of the arrow indicates the direction of the wind. If she looks up, then the wind is south, and if she looks to the right, it is west. Plumage describes the strength of the wind. A long feather corresponds to a speed of 5 m/s, and a short feather corresponds to half that speed

What if we abandon the uniform distribution of weather stations? Let’s place a dozen of them in the Moscow area, and then we’ll give an accurate forecast for Moscow. It won't work. The equations themselves that describe the dynamics of the atmosphere make it possible to estimate the speed of influence propagation from one point to another - about 300 m/s. And a wind speed of 50 m/s at an altitude of several kilometers is quite common. Even if we focus on this lower speed, we must admit that air particles can travel 180 km in an hour. Consequently, even with a forecast for one day, the area of ​​influence on the weather in Moscow significantly exceeds the Moscow region, and therefore it is necessary to have information from outside the region. So, when forecasting weather for more than a day, even in such a large country as Russia, data from abroad is needed. It has already been mentioned that the Hydrometeorological Center of Russia calculates the forecast once or twice a day (to assimilate the data, it is calculated for a short period of time, but more often: four times a day). Fig.6. You can try to separate the average wind and the local wind. The upper square $25 \times 25$ km with a resolution of 0.5 km shows the field of wind deviation from the average for the same square at an altitude of 1 km (where the wind is weakly influenced by the features of the earth’s orography, houses, etc.). Measurements were taken jointly by two radars in Oklahoma at 14:38 GMT on July 27, 1977. The vector of the average flow (more precisely, 1/2 $v_{ср} )$ is shown in the upper right corner and is 14.8 m/s. It can be seen that the deviations are less than the average flow by an order of magnitude (but no more!). And they have their own repeating structure - vortices with a characteristic size of 4 km. The lower figure shows wind measurements taken under the same conditions as the upper one, but after about 3.5 minutes. The average wind has remained virtually unchanged, and the small-scale structure of deviations from it has changed. The main change (although not everything comes down to it) is the transfer along the main flow in the easterly direction. It can be estimated that the entire initial picture will “leave” from the square in question in about 28 minutes - quite enough for the landing or takeoff of the aircraft.
