But if you play the notes one after the other, or even both at once, you can hear whether they sound good together. The violinist, having tuned one of the strings with a tuning fork (in practice, usually with an accompanist's piano or an oboe in an orchestra), then tunes the second one like this: by moving the bow along both strings, he adjusts the tension of the second string until they sound good (“clean”) together. When do two notes form a harmonic interval? It turns out that this happens when their frequencies are related to each other as small integers. We won’t say why this happens - for this you need to know a little trigonometry. Instead, we list some intervals and their names (see Table 1).

Until the 18th century, pianos (more precisely, harpsichords and organs - the modern piano appeared later) were tuned, trying to make some intervals clear. At the same time, some keys sounded beautiful, while others (usually with a large number of black keys) sounded terrible, and composers tried to avoid them, believing that a normal organist would not be able to play them anyway. The tradition of dividing keys into “good” and “bad” and writing music only in good ones was shaken by the great Bach, who wrote “The Well-Tempered Clavier” - a collection of preludes and fugues. It consists of two parts. Each movement contains 24 preludes and fugues, one in each major and minor key. It is not known exactly how Bach tuned his harpsichord - whether it was uniform temperament, when the chromatic scale forms a geometric progression, or some kind of not entirely uniform. But modern performers play it on evenly tempered pianos, on which all intervals (with the exception of the octave) do not sound entirely pure - but the same in all keys. Problem 20. It is remarkable that musicians used the 12-tone system without knowing anything about geometric progressions and without making any calculations - just as bees made neat hexagonal honeycombs long before people discovered that this was the optimal shape.
