Starting from moment $( t_{0}$, give a forecast for a short period $\delta t$, then, using the obtained result as a first approximation, “pump up” the data related to the time interval $( t_{0}, t_{0} + \delta t )$, then use the updated forecast for time $t_{0} + \delta t$ as the initial condition for the forecast for period $( t_{0} + \delta t, t_{0} + 2 \delta t )$, etc. Since practice requires a weather forecast with a lead time of $T^{≫} \delta t$, from time to time it is necessary to calculate the forecast for the period T, but at the same time, the initial data for such a forecast also includes earlier data from the interval $( t_{0} - k \delta t, t_{0} )$, where k is the number of “swappings” of information. It is as if we are taking a jump from a continuously moving escalator belt, and not from the stationary Earth. After a new forecast has been calculated, you can use it as a first approximation and introduce corrections so that the next forecast is even closer to the observations. Of course, to implement such a very complicated procedure of “continuous”, also called four-dimensional, data assimilation, you need to perform more calculations than to solve a dynamic problem with a fixed initial field, and therefore, have a more powerful computer. wandering. Weather dynamics are a trajectory in this space. And its assessment constitutes the task of weather forecasting. A real computer has limited memory and speed and cannot calculate the dynamics of a system with an infinite number of degrees of freedom. Modern discrete weather forecast models have degrees of freedom in the range $N = = 10^{7} - 10^{8}$. Instead of calculating the “true trajectory,” a polyline is built in increments of several minutes. The model needs a starting point in this N-dimensional space. The measurement carries information about one of the coordinates of the trajectory in N-dimensional space at the moment of measurement. If there are more than N units of information during the period of assimilation (and in practice this is what happens), then a trajectory that exactly corresponds to all measurements does not exist. The model itself is approximate, and the measurements are made with some errors. Let us reformulate the problem statement: we will find a trajectory such that the sum of squared deviations of the trajectory from the measured values ​​will be minimal. The simplest analogue of such a problem (solvable by the least squares method) is when there are m points on the plane and you need to draw a straight line so that the sum of the squares of the m deviations of the points from the straight line is minimal. Our task has three differences: everything happens not on a two-dimensional plane, but in N-dimensional space; the trajectories are not straight, but rather complex curves; we do not know all the points, but only some of their coordinates.

What is foam?

(See the beginning on the 4th page of the cover)

Why is liquid foam white? Chaotically oriented bubbles reflect the incident light in different directions, i.e. scatter it. And a thin film of any liquid (shampoos, carbonated drinks, etc.) surrounding gas bubbles is practically colorless when exposed to light and has almost no effect on the color of the foam. Therefore, the color of the foam of most different liquids is the same - the color of the light falling on it. To see foam, for example, red in color, it is enough to hold a glass of foam at night to the red brake light of a car. And to see black foam, you can place it on black paper and illuminate it with a beam of parallel rays of light, for example passing through a narrow slit. If you look at the illuminated foam through a magnifying glass, it will appear black except for a few cells that give glare towards the observer. Foam on earth and in space. Natural materials often have a foam structure. This is wood, cork, sponge or coral. Many artificial materials also resemble foam - for example, whipped cream, bread, polystyrene foam, polyurethane foam. Foam can also be made from molten metal by passing gas bubbles through it. As it cools, the metal foam turns into a solid porous body, the properties of which depend on the percentage of gas in the foam. For example, the density of aluminum foam, containing 94% air (by volume), is 6 times less than the density of water, and the thermal conductivity is 100 times less than that of ordinary aluminum. By varying the percentage of air, foam with different physical properties can be obtained. The production of materials with specified properties opens up broad opportunities in biomedicine. Thus, in the treatment of fractures, implants are often used - artificial elements that replace lost parts of the bone. However, if the Young's moduli of the bone and the implant differ, then the latter will eventually simply be rejected. But hard titanium foam with an elastic modulus equal to the elastic modulus of bone tissue is an ideal material for an implant in this case. Gravity interferes with obtaining a stable liquid foam with identical characteristics throughout the entire volume of the sample. Over time, small gas bubbles merge into large ones, and liquid accumulates at the bottom of the vessel. This process is called foam aging. If the aging of foam occurs quickly, then it is very difficult to study it. Therefore, it is best to study foam formation in zero gravity. This topic is indeed included in the scientific program of interplanetary space stations. K. Bogdanov
