Aerological information, to use meteorological jargon, “illuminates” the atmosphere above the planet - a radiosonde flies and reports information about meteorological fields to an altitude of 30 km or more. Measurements made by a radiosonde in flight are often attributed to a single point in time - called the standard observation period. There is an international agreement on what terms should be considered standard, so that these measurements can be carried out simultaneously in all countries (we will discuss below why such simultaneity is convenient for calculations). However, the probe does not rise to its maximum height instantly - it usually takes about one and a half $часов.^{14}$ Therefore, neglecting this time leads to an error in the interpretation of measurements. In order to estimate this error, it is necessary to multiply the characteristic derivative $\partial_{t} f$ of the measured quantity f (temperature, pressure, humidity, wind) with respect to time t by $\Delta t \approx 1,5$ hours. In a crude model such an error $\delta_{f} = \Delta t \cdot \partial_{t} f$ can be neglected, but in a good modern model it is better to make proper corrections. It is worth taking into account the horizontal displacement of the probe that increases with the time of ascent. The southern hemisphere of the Earth is “illuminated” by data much worse than the northern hemisphere. Many meteorological centers located far north of the equator once ignored this poorly lit area and limited their forecast area to the northern hemisphere, and sometimes even part of it. Another reason for this limitation was insufficient computer performance. Of course, atmospheric processes occurring in the southern hemisphere affect the state of the atmosphere in the northern, and such a forced limitation leads to an error that increases over time and spreads from the equator to the north. The “mitigating circumstance” was the relatively small values ​​of the $меридиональной^{15}$ wind component. Synoptic and especially aerological data “illuminate” the northern (and even more so the southern) hemisphere unevenly. More than half of its area is ocean, and only a few island weather stations and a few anchored weather ships launch radiosondes there. The densest network of stations is in Europe, East Asia and North America.
