Dark Energy in the Universe

V. RUBAKOV

PHYSICISTS LOVE THE RED WORD. IN THEIR ENVIRONMENT, for some time now it has been customary to give “unscientific” names to newly discovered entities. Take, for example, “strange” or “charm” quarks. So dark energy is not a synonym for dark forces, but a term coined to designate an unusual property of our Universe. The discovery of dark energy was made using astronomical methods and came as a complete surprise to most physicists. Dark energy is perhaps the main mystery of modern natural science. It is likely that its solution will become the most important event in physics of the 21st century, comparable in scale to the largest discoveries of the recent past, such as the discovery of the phenomenon of the expansion of the Universe. It is even possible that such a radical development of the theory will occur that it will be on a par with the creation of the general theory of relativity, the discovery of the curvature of space-time and the connection of this curvature with gravitational forces. We are now at the beginning of the journey, and talking about dark energy is an opportunity to look into the “laboratory” of physicists at a time when their work is in full swing.

A little history

"It won't be enough"

The fact that “something is wrong” in our Universe became clear to cosmologists by the beginning of the 90s of the last century. To explain what we are talking about, let's start by recalling the expansion of our Universe. Galaxies that are distant from each other scatter, and the further away a galaxy is, the faster it moves away from us. Quantitatively, the rate of expansion is characterized by the Hubble parameter (constant) - the coefficient of proportionality between the speed of removal and distance. The value of the Hubble parameter in the modern Universe is quite good

The Hubble parameter depends on time; Thus, in the distant past, the Universe expanded much faster than it does now, and, accordingly, the Hubble parameter was much larger.

By the beginning of the 90s, the energy density of “normal” matter in the modern Universe was also estimated with good accuracy. Dark matter apparently consists of new elementary particles that have not yet been discovered in terrestrial experiments. Unlike many known particles, they do not carry an electrical charge and therefore do not emit light; the matter consisting of them is truly dark. The similarity with ordinary matter is that the forces of gravitational attraction cause dark matter to gather into clumps - galaxies and clusters of galaxies. And what happened? It turned out that there is clearly not enough normal matter to explain the measured rate of expansion of the Universe. Moreover, it was significant: the “shortage” was about 2/3 (according to modern estimates, about 72%). There were two possible explanations for this fact: either three-dimensional space is curved and the missing contribution to the Hubble parameter is associated with the “elasticity” of space, or there is a new form of energy in the Universe, which later became known as dark energy. Another difficulty with the good old cosmological model was the age of the modern Universe. This model gave an age value of about 9 billion years. At the same time, the age of old star clusters was (and is now estimated) to be 13–14 billion years. Problem! It must be said that the model with curved space, in which the age is about 11 billion years, did not fully cope with the problem (in the model with dark energy, it is about 14 billion years, everything is fine). Few, however, took this difficulty seriously, and the majority expected that it would “somehow resolve itself.”

Wherever you throw it...

From a theoretical point of view, both possibilities—non-Euclidean space and dark energy—looked extremely implausible. Let's start with the curvature of three-dimensional space. As the Universe expands, space smoothes out and its curvature decreases. If the curvature is different from zero now, then it was greater in the past than it is today. However, the energy (mass) density of matter decreases even faster as the Universe expands. This means that in the past the relative contribution of curvature to the Hubble parameter was very small, and the main contribution, by a large margin, was the contribution of matter. In order for 2/3 of the expansion of the Universe today to be ensured by curvature, it is necessary to “adjust” the value of the radius of curvature of space in the past with fantastic accuracy - one second after the Big Bang it should have been equal to one billionth of the then size of the observable Universe, no more and no less! Without such a fit, the curvature today would be either many orders of magnitude greater or many orders of magnitude less than needed to explain the observations. Even if we ignore the hypothesis that the missing contribution to the modern Hubble parameter is provided by the curvature of space, the problem of curvature still remains: in any case, the curvature must be extremely small in the early stages, otherwise it would be too large today. This problem was one of the main considerations that led to the idea of ​​the inflationary stage of the evolution of the Universe. According to the inflationary theory (proposed by A. Starobinsky and independently by A. Gut and formed thanks to the work of A. Linde, A. Albrecht and P. Steinhardt), the Universe at the very early stage of its evolution went through a stage of extremely rapid, exponential expansion.

Although the inflationary stage probably lasted only a fraction of a second, during this time the Universe expanded so much that its size became much larger than the size of the part we see today. It is important for us that as a result of the inflationary stretching of space, its radius of curvature dropped to almost zero. This, of course, goes against the hypothesis that the Universe is expanding today by 2/3 due to curvature. With dark energy the situation is similar, only worse. We will discuss various hypotheses about the nature of dark energy below, but here we will limit ourselves to the following remark. Regardless of what dark energy is, its density can be characterized by a single energy dimension parameter. To be consistent with observational data, the value of this dark energy scale parameter should be approximately equal to 0.002 electronvolts. At the same time, the known fundamental interactions - strong, weak, electromagnetic and gravitational - are characterized by their energy scales. The smallest of these relates to the strong (nuclear) interactions and is about 200 million electron volts. It turns out to be a 100 billion times inconsistency! The worst thing is that this discrepancy and the generally extremely small amount of dark energy are very difficult to find an explanation. Due to difficulties in interpreting dark energy, the more popular view for quite a long time was that spatial curvature was responsible for the current rate of expansion of the Universe. Many (though by no means all) physicists could not take seriously the possibility that dark energy really exists, and considered spatial curvature to be “the lesser of evils.” The question, as usual, was resolved by experiment.

The universe is expanding at an accelerating rate

The turning point came in 1998–1999, when two groups from the United States (one led by A. Reiss and B. Schmidt, and the other by S. Perlmutter) reported the results of observations of distant type 1a supernovae. From these observations it followed that our Universe is expanding at an accelerating rate. This property is completely consistent with the idea of ​​dark energy, while the non-Euclidean nature of space does not lead to accelerated expansion. Thus, a clear choice was made in favor of dark energy, and the hypothesis of a non-Euclidean three-dimensional space was rejected (we will return to this briefly). A detailed theoretical description of these explosions is still missing, but based on observations of nearby supernovae, empirical patterns have been established that make it possible to determine their absolute luminosity, i.e. the energy they emit during a fairly short flash. In other words, type 1a supernovae are “standard candles”: knowing the absolute luminosity and measuring the apparent brightness (the flow of energy coming to Earth), you can determine the distance to each of them - the greater the distance, the lower the apparent brightness. At the same time, it is possible to establish the speed at which each of the supernovae is moving away from us (using the Doppler effect). Supernovae are very bright objects and can be seen at great distances. In other words, the distant supernovae that we observe now exploded a long time ago, and therefore their escape speed was determined by the rate of expansion of the Universe then, in the distant past. Thus, observations of type 1a supernovae make it possible to determine the expansion rate at relatively early stages of the evolution of the Universe (7 billion years ago and even slightly earlier) and to trace the dependence of this rate on time. This is what made it possible to establish that the Universe is expanding at an accelerating rate. Now there are several more observational results that are in no way related to supernovae and independently indicate the accelerated expansion of the Universe. For example, relatively rapid cosmological expansion prevents the formation of newly formed objects - large clusters of galaxies. Galaxies gather into clusters under the influence of gravitational forces with which they attract each other. The general stretching of space, on the contrary, forces galaxies to move away from each other and thereby prevents them from crowding. It is clear that the higher the stretching rate, the stronger the second effect. Given the known modern expansion rate (the Hubble parameter), the expansion rate in the past was slower in an accelerating Universe compared to a decelerating expansion Universe, and more clusters should have formed. Counting the number of large clusters at different distances from us (and, therefore, formed at different times) shows that the clusters were actually formed very willingly, in full agreement with the idea of ​​​​the accelerated expansion of the Universe and in contradiction with the model of a slowing down Universe without dark energy, but with spatial curvature. Let us also mention the study of features in the distribution of galaxies in space - the so-called baryon acoustic oscillations.

What properties of dark energy are known today? There are few such properties, only three. But what is known may rightly cause astonishment. The first is that, unlike normal matter, dark energy does not cluster and does not gather into objects such as galaxies or their clusters. As far as we know now, dark energy is “spread” evenly throughout the Universe. This statement, like any statement based on observations or experiments, is true to a certain extent. It cannot be completely ruled out that somewhere in the Universe the density of dark energy is a little higher, and somewhere a little less than the average density, somewhere the dark energy is a little denser, and somewhere a little more rarefied. However, it follows from observations that such deviations from homogeneity, if they exist, should be very small in magnitude. We have already talked about the second property: dark energy causes the Universe to expand with acceleration. In this way, dark energy is also strikingly different from normal matter. For normal matter, the usual idea of ​​how gravitational forces “work” is valid: particles of matter formed, say, as a result of an explosion and scattering from the center, gradually slow down their scattering due to gravitational attraction to the center. If it were not for dark energy, the same would be the case in the Universe:

The two described properties indicate that dark energy, in a certain sense, experiences antigravity; for it there is gravitational repulsion instead of gravitational attraction. Because of this, the expansion of the Universe is accelerating, and because of this, dark energy is distributed evenly in space. Areas with an increased density of normal matter, due to gravitational attraction, collect matter from the surrounding space, these areas themselves are compressed and form dense clumps; This is how the first stars were formed, and then galaxies and clusters of galaxies. For an anti-gravitating substance, the opposite is true: areas with increased density (if any) are stretched due to gravitational repulsion, inhomogeneities are smoothed out, and no clumps are formed. The third property of dark energy is that its density does not depend on time. Also surprising: the Universe is expanding, the volume is growing, but the energy density remains constant. There seems to be a contradiction here with the law of conservation of energy. Over the past 8 billion years, the Universe has expanded twice, its volume has increased 8 times, and the energy in this volume has increased by the same amount. The non-conservation of energy is obvious. Of course, this applies specifically to dark energy: the number of particles of normal matter in the expanding volume has not changed, their total rest energy has not changed either, and the rest energy density has dropped by 8 times. In fact, the increase in energy as the Universe expands does not contradict the laws of physics. Dark energy is designed in such a way that expanding space does work on it, which leads to an increase in the energy of this substance in the expanding volume of space. True, the expansion of space is itself caused by dark energy, so the situation is reminiscent of Baron Munchausen pulling himself out of the swamp by his hair. And yet, there is no contradiction: in a cosmological context it is impossible to introduce the concept of total energy, which includes the energy of the gravitational field itself. So there is also no law of conservation of energy, which prohibits the increase or decrease in the energy of any form of matter. The statement about the constancy of the density of dark energy is also based on astronomical observations, and therefore is also true with a certain accuracy. To characterize this accuracy, let's say that over the past 8 billion years, the density of dark energy has changed by no more than a factor of 1.4, so that the energy in the expanding volume has increased by a factor of 6–11. Today we can say this with confidence. Note that the second and third properties of dark energy - the ability to lead to an accelerated expansion of the Universe and its constancy in time (or, more generally, a very slow dependence on time) - are in fact closely related. Why now?

If in the modern Universe dark energy makes the largest contribution to the total energy density, then in the past this was far from the case. Let's say 8 billion years ago, normal matter was 8 times denser, and the density of dark energy was the same (or almost the same) as it is now. From this it is easy to conclude that at that time the ratio between the rest energy of normal matter and dark energy was in favor of the former: dark energy was about 15%, and not 72%, as it is today. Due to the fact that normal matter played the main role at that time, the expansion of the Universe slowed down. Even earlier, the influence of dark energy on expansion was very weak. It is remarkable that this picture is confirmed by data on type 1a supernovae. The most distant of them exploded more than 8 billion years ago, and measurements of their speeds show that the expansion of the Universe was indeed slowing down then. Other cosmological observations also suggest that dark energy was unimportant in the early stages of the universe's evolution. One series of such results is worth mentioning. In the period from one second to several minutes after the Big Bang, when the temperature in the Universe varied from 10 billion to hundreds of millions of degrees, thermonuclear reactions were actively occurring in the cosmic plasma. As a result, deuterium and isotopes of helium and lithium were formed. In some places in the Universe, this primary composition of matter has remained virtually unchanged, and it has been possible to measure it. On the other hand, it can be reliably calculated, and the result, of course, depends on the rate of expansion of the Universe in that distant era. So, the calculation results are consistent with observations, if we assume that dark energy did not play any role then. More precisely, the contribution of dark energy (as well as other new, hypothetical forms of energy) to the total energy density during thermonuclear reactions is limited to about 15%. The accuracy does not seem to be very high, but we must not forget that we are talking about the first seconds after the Big Bang! So, the influence of dark energy and the acceleration of the expansion of the Universe caused by it are very recent phenomena by cosmological standards: the acceleration began only 6.5 billion years ago. Candidates for the role of carrier of dark energy

Vacuum energy is a cosmological constant

If there were no gravity, the absolute value of energy would have no physical meaning. In all theories that describe nature, with the exception of the theory of gravitational interactions, only the difference in the energies of certain states makes sense. Thus, when speaking about the binding energy of a hydrogen atom, we mean the difference between two quantities: the total rest energy of a free proton and electron, on the one hand, and the rest energy of the atom, on the other. It is this energy difference that is released (transferred to the born photon) when an electron and a proton combine to form an atom. In the same way, by the rest energy of a proton we actually mean the difference between the energies of the state in which there is a proton and the state without a proton - vacuum. If it were not for gravitational interaction, talking about vacuum energy would be pointless; there would simply be nothing to compare it with. The situation is completely different if we are interested specifically in gravitational interactions. Vacuum energy, like any other energy, “weighs” and gravitates. Vacuum is a state with the lowest energy (therefore, by the way, energy cannot be taken away from it), but this energy does not have to be equal to zero; from a theoretical point of view, it can be both positive and negative. Whether it can be calculated “from first principles” is a big question. But in any case, vacuum energy, if it is positive, has exactly the properties that dark energy should have. Indeed, the vacuum is the same everywhere (at least in the visible part of the Universe). Assuming otherwise would lead to contradictions, for example with observations

A vacuum cannot be “dense” somewhere and “thinner” somewhere, otherwise it would not be a vacuum. Further, with a relatively slow expansion of the Universe (and this is exactly how things are now and have been in the foreseeable past), the vacuum remains the same. Thus, the vacuum is indeed a suitable candidate for the role of dark energy carrier. We emphasize that the absence of dependence of energy density on position in space and on time is an exact, not approximate, property of the vacuum, which distinguishes it from other candidates for the role of dark energy. The vacuum energy density is a universal constant (at least in the part of the Universe that we observe). Why does the idea of ​​dark energy as vacuum energy not satisfy many physicists? This is due to the absurdly small value of the vacuum energy density, which is necessary for agreement between theory and observations. In a vacuum, virtual particles are born and die all the time, there are field condensates in it - in general, a vacuum is more like a complex medium than an absolute void. This is not just speculation: the features of the vacuum are reflected in the properties of elementary particles and their interactions and are ultimately determined, albeit indirectly, from numerous experiments. The energy of the vacuum, in principle, should “know” how it is structured, what its structure is and what the values ​​of the parameters characterizing it are (for example, field condensates). Now imagine a theorist who has studied particle physics but has not heard anything about the Universe. Let's ask this theorist to predict the vacuum energy density. Based on the energy scales characteristic of fundamental interactions and the corresponding length scales, he will make his estimate - and be wrong by an unimaginable number of times. We have already talked about this: the energy scale of fundamental interactions is at least 200 million electronvolts, and the scale required from observations corresponding to vacuum energy (if dark energy is vacuum energy) is 0.002 electronvolts.

If dark energy is vacuum energy, then trying to understand why it has such a small value can be done by following, say, the following logic. Let us imagine that the Universe is extremely large, that it is many times larger than the part we observe. Let us further assume that in different very vast parts of the Universe, a variety of vacuum states with very different energy densities can be realized. This possibility, by the way, is not theoretically excluded. Regions of the Universe where the vacuum energy density is too high in absolute value look completely different from our region: where the vacuum energy is large and positive, space expands so quickly that stars and galaxies simply do not have time to form; in regions with large negative vacuum energy, the expansion of space quickly gives way to compression, and these regions collapse long before the formation of stars. In both cases, cosmological evolution is incompatible with the existence of observers like us. And vice versa, we could only appear where the vacuum energy density is very close to zero - and that’s where we appeared. Here an analogy arises with another, quite obvious fact: we exist on planet Earth, more or less suitable for life, and not in an arbitrary place in the Universe where there are no conditions for life at all. This, as they say, anthropic view on the problem of vacuum energy was expressed more than 20 years ago (in the works of A. Linde and S. Weinberg). Now it is popular among a significant part of theoretical physicists. Another part perceives it as a way to avoid the question of what physical reasons actually determine such a small observed vacuum energy and whether the nature of dark energy is completely different. The most balanced approach is probably not to rule out the anthropic explanation as a possible final answer, but to still try to find an alternative solution to the problems of vacuum energy and dark energy.

Easy field

An alternative to vacuum as a carrier of dark energy can be some new field “spread” in the Universe. In this version, the energy of the new field is dark energy. This field should be new because the presence of known fields (for example, electromagnetic fields) everywhere in the Universe would influence the behavior of matter too much and lead to effects that would have been discovered long ago. In addition, the known fields are such that their energy does not have the properties of dark energy listed above. The hypothetical new field should have an energy scale of 0.002 electronvolts. Although this is a very small scale, in terms of known interactions, it does not seem completely implausible. Indeed, we already know that the scale of different interactions varies greatly. The new field, generally speaking, changes during the evolution of the Universe. Its energy density also changes. A light field with extremely low mass is a long-range force similar to gravity. To avoid any contradiction with experiments testing the general theory of relativity, the interaction of this field with ordinary matter should be very weak, weaker than gravitational one. All these properties do not look attractive to the theorist, but they can be tolerated. It is important that the hypothesis of a new field, at least in principle, allows for experimental verification. First, more accurate measurements of the rate of expansion of the Universe at the present stage and in the past may reveal that the density of dark energy changes over time. This will definitely reject the hypothesis about the vacuum nature of dark energy and, on the contrary, will serve as an indication of the existence of a new light field in the Universe. Secondly, in the future we can hope to discover the heterogeneity of the distribution of dark energy in space. This would be definitive proof that dark energy is new field energy and not anything else. On the other hand, today there are no visible ways to register a new light field in laboratory experiments (at accelerators, etc.). The reason is the extremely weak interaction of this field with matter. Such pessimism, however, may be temporary: we know too little about the new field to completely exclude the possibility of its direct experimental study in the future. Never say never. Physicists are discussing different types of hypothetical light fields whose energy could act as dark energy. In the simplest, from a theoretical point of view, version, the energy density of the new field decreases with time. For a field of this type, the term “quintessence” is used (sometimes the term “cosmon” is also used). However, the opposite possibility cannot be excluded, when the energy density increases with time; a field of this type is called a “phantom”. Phantom would be a very exotic field; Nothing like this has ever been found in nature. The distinction between quintessence and phantom, as we will discuss below, is important from the point of view of the distant future of the Universe.

New gravity

Finally, another possible explanation for dark energy is that there really is no dark energy. Dark energy has to be invoked to explain the features of the expansion of the Universe in the event that cosmological evolution is described by the general theory of relativity. If this theory is not applicable on modern cosmological length and time scales, then there is no need for dark energy. Of course, this view of dark energy cannot ignore the fact that general relativity has been well tested on smaller distance scales. Therefore, it is necessary to create a new theory of gravity, which would transform into the general theory of relativity at relatively short distances, but would differently describe the evolution of the Universe at later stages, close to ours. This is a difficult task, especially if we take into account the requirement of self-consistency, internal consistency of the theory. However, such attempts are being made, and some of them look quite promising. One possibility is to stop considering Newton's constant of universal gravitation as a constant, and allow it to vary in space and time, obeying certain equations. Unfortunately, the most beautiful versions of the theory that realize this possibility were rejected by experiments testing general relativity. If you don’t pursue beauty, then models that explain the accelerated expansion of the Universe and are consistent with everything that is known about gravity can be built along this path. Such models, as a rule, predict deviations from the general theory of relativity, which, although small, may in the future be experimentally detectable. Let us also note the idea that our space can have more than three dimensions, while additional dimensions do not manifest themselves at ordinary distances. At the same time, at cosmological distances of billions of light years, the gravitational field lines can “spread” into additional dimensions, which is why gravity will no longer be described by Newton’s law. The law of expansion of the Universe may also change. A completely satisfactory theory that explains the accelerated expansion of the Universe in this way has not yet been constructed; In the models proposed to date, this idea is only partially implemented. It is remarkable, however, that these models lead to predictions that are testable by experiment. Among them is the possibility of changing Newton's gravitational law at short distances; small but detectable corrections to the general theory of relativity in the solar system, and so on. So, the recently discovered features of the expansion of the Universe have raised a new question: are they due to the energy of the vacuum, the energy of the new light field, or the new gravity at ultra-long distances? Theoretical study of these possibilities is in full swing.

Dark energy and the future of the Universe

With the discovery of dark energy, ideas about what the distant future of our Universe might be like have changed dramatically. Before this discovery, the question of the future was clearly associated with the question of the curvature of three-dimensional space. If, as many previously believed, the curvature of space by 2/3 determined the current rate of expansion of the Universe, and there was no dark energy, then the Universe would expand without limit, gradually slowing down. Now it is clear that the future is determined by the properties of dark energy. Since we know these properties poorly now, we cannot yet predict the future. You can only consider different options. It is difficult to say what happens in theories with new gravity, but other scenarios can be discussed now. If dark energy is constant over time, as is the case with vacuum energy, then the Universe will always experience accelerated expansion. Most galaxies will eventually move away from ours to an enormous distance, and our Galaxy, along with its few neighbors, will end up as an island in the void. If dark energy is quintessential, then in the distant future the accelerated expansion may stop and even be replaced by compression. In the latter case, the matter in the Universe will return to a hot and dense state, and a “Big Bang in reverse” will occur, back in time. An even more dramatic fate awaits the Universe if dark energy is a phantom, and such that its energy density increases without limit. The expansion of the Universe will become more and more rapid, it will accelerate so much that galaxies will be torn out of clusters, stars from galaxies, planets from the solar system. It will come to the point that electrons will break away from atoms, and atomic nuclei will split into protons and neutrons. There will be, as they say, a big break. Such a scenario, however, does not seem very likely. Most likely, the phantom's energy density will remain limited. But even then, the Universe may face an unusual future. The fact is that in many theories, phantom behavior - an increase in energy density over time - is accompanied by instabilities of the phantom field. In this case, the phantom field in the Universe will become highly inhomogeneous, its energy density in different parts of the Universe will be different, some parts will rapidly expand, and some may experience collapse. The fate of our Galaxy will depend on which region it falls into. All this, however, relates to the future, distant even by cosmological standards. In the next 20 billion years, the Universe will remain almost the same as it is now. We have time to understand the properties of dark energy and thereby more definitely predict the future, and perhaps even influence it.
