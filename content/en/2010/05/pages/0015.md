Candidates for the role of carrier of dark energy

Vacuum energy is a cosmological constant

If there were no gravity, the absolute value of energy would have no physical meaning. In all theories that describe nature, with the exception of the theory of gravitational interactions, only the difference in the energies of certain states makes sense. Thus, when speaking about the binding energy of a hydrogen atom, we mean the difference between two quantities: the total rest energy of a free proton and electron, on the one hand, and the rest energy of the atom, on the other. It is this energy difference that is released (transferred to the born photon) when an electron and a proton combine to form an atom. In the same way, by the rest energy of a proton we actually mean the difference between the energies of the state in which there is a proton and the state without a proton - vacuum. If it were not for gravitational interaction, talking about vacuum energy would be pointless; there would simply be nothing to compare it with. The situation is completely different if we are interested specifically in gravitational interactions. Vacuum energy, like any other energy, “weighs” and gravitates. Vacuum is a state with the lowest energy (therefore, by the way, energy cannot be taken away from it), but this energy does not have to be equal to zero; from a theoretical point of view, it can be both positive and negative. Whether it can be calculated “from first principles” is a big question. But in any case, vacuum energy, if it is positive, has exactly the properties that dark energy should have. Indeed, the vacuum is the same everywhere (at least in the visible part of the Universe). Assuming otherwise would lead to contradictions, for example with observations

changes in the cosmic microwave background radiation: in different vacuums, the properties of cosmic plasma in the era of the last emission of photons would be very different, and the temperatures of the emitted photons would also be very different. There would be other insurmountable contradictions with observations. So, the vacuum is the same everywhere. Its energy density is also the same. A vacuum cannot be “dense” somewhere and “thinner” somewhere, otherwise it would not be a vacuum. Therefore, as required for dark energy, the vacuum energy is distributed evenly throughout the Universe. Further, with a relatively slow expansion of the Universe (and this is exactly how things are now and have been in the foreseeable past), the vacuum remains the same. The properties of vacuum are determined by the physics of ultra-short distances and times, and the slow expansion of the Universe does not affect them. Therefore, again, as required, the vacuum energy density does not depend on time. As we have already said, in the general theory of relativity, the last property automatically means that vacuum energy leads to the accelerated expansion of the Universe. Thus, the vacuum is indeed a suitable candidate for the role of dark energy carrier. We emphasize that the absence of dependence of energy density on position in space and on time is an exact, not approximate, property of the vacuum, which distinguishes it from other candidates for the role of dark energy. The vacuum energy density is a universal constant (at least in the part of the Universe that we observe). Why does the idea of ​​dark energy as vacuum energy not satisfy many physicists? This is due to the absurdly small value of the vacuum energy density, which is necessary for agreement between theory and observations. In a vacuum, virtual particles are born and die all the time, there are field condensates in it - in general, a vacuum is more like a complex medium than an absolute void. This is not just speculation: the features of the vacuum are reflected in the properties of elementary particles and their interactions and are ultimately determined, albeit indirectly, from numerous experiments. The energy of the vacuum, in principle, should “know” how it is structured, what its structure is and what the values ​​of the parameters characterizing it are (for example, field condensates). Now imagine a theorist who has studied particle physics but has not heard anything about the Universe. Let's ask this theorist to predict the vacuum energy density. Based on the energy scales characteristic of fundamental interactions and the corresponding length scales, he will make his estimate - and be wrong by an unimaginable number of times. We have already talked about this: the energy scale of fundamental interactions is at least 200 million electronvolts, and the scale required from observations corresponding to vacuum energy (if dark energy is vacuum energy) is 0.002 electronvolts.
