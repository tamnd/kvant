PHYSICS ELECTIVE

About heat transfer and more

A. KRASNOCHUB

Measure everything that can be measured, and make measurable what could not be measured! G. Galileo

UNTIL RECENT TIMES IN THE INFORMATION WORLD, when it was necessary to present science, the media usually used the following clichés: complex installations with many tubes and wires, displays, oscilloscope screens, etc., and scientists, as a rule, are wise old men with beards and otherworldly looks. In fact, reality, as always, is very different from the journalists' interpretation. Thus, Thomson, the future Lord Kelvin, was 24 years old when he introduced the absolute temperature scale. Newton was the same age when, in two years, he developed ideas that led him to the creation of differential and integral calculus, the invention of the reflecting telescope, and the discovery of the law of universal gravitation. And these are not isolated examples, this is the rule. Science is made by young people with an amazing ability to think clearly and simply. What do these two myths lead to - complexity and old age? Moreover, when a young man graduates from university at the age of 24–25, he is afraid to think independently. Constantly using the “crutches” of solidified knowledge, he tries to extract answers to questions that arise in his work from lectures or textbooks, and during discussions he tries to hide behind an authoritative opinion. This article is an attempt to give schoolchildren the opportunity to feel and understand for themselves what science is and what scientific research is. I don’t want to create my own definition of science for the sake of popularization, so those interested in a strict definition can be recommended to read monographs on the fundamentals and methodology of science. For further narration, we only need that science is, first of all, a system of knowledge based on experience and dealing with reproducible phenomena. At least this applies to the natural sciences. In the strict definitions of science, there is nothing about the thirst for knowledge, undying curiosity and the ability to be surprised and admire the phenomena and their relationships in the world around us. Unfortunately, this part of the work and life of scientists is usually left to biographers or journalists. And only if we are lucky and a man of science leaves behind, in addition to scientific works, diaries and memoirs, we will be able to see the passions and emotions that are usually hidden behind the precise formulations of scientific articles. However, as P.L. Kapitsa said, “the scientific approach is the absence of emotions,” so let’s put aside emotions and get down to business.

What will we consider?

⟦column⟧

As an example, we propose to consider the process of cooling a mug of boiling water. What interest could a “banal” cooling problem have? On the one hand, this is the most suitable and simple example that each of you can repeat and develop on your own. On the other hand, if you think about it, in life you often have to deal with the laws of cooling and heating. It is enough to open the passport for any household appliance (not to mention industrial equipment) - and you can easily find restrictions on operating and storage temperatures. Anyway, sometimes it is very useful to be able to predict something in advance, so why not start with the problem of heat transfer? If you master the approach itself, you can then tackle any issue that interests you. So, we want to know how heated bodies cool down. What does it mean to “know”? From a physics point of view, this means writing an equation for the process; from a technical point of view, it means predicting the temperature of a body placed in specific conditions after a certain period of time. What should we do if we want something, but we don’t know the answer? Probably the first thought is to ask an experienced person. Although, what is an experienced person? Besides, he may not be around. Another idea is to look at the reference book, read books. Yes, this is true, but only if someone has already written this reference book or book. What if not? If you are not going to be second, third, etc. all your life, then it is obvious that sooner or later you will encounter something that will be for the first time. It's a good idea to prepare for this. Let's say that there are no reference books, there is no one to ask, but we really want to figure out how quickly a mug of boiling water will cool down. What should we do? The simplest solution is to take a mug of boiling water, lower a thermometer into it and record the temperature at certain intervals $времени.^{1}$ As a result of this simple experiment, we will obtain a certain dependence of temperature on time (Fig.1).
