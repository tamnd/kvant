So, within the framework of the general theory of relativity, the Hubble parameter determines the sum of the total energy density in the Universe and the contribution associated with the possible non-Euclideanity of three-dimensional space. By the beginning of the 90s, the energy density of “normal” matter in the modern Universe was also estimated with good accuracy. It is normal in the sense that it experiences the same gravitational interactions as ordinary matter. Thus, Newton’s law of universal gravitation is valid for normal matter. The matter, however, is complicated by the fact that most of the normal matter is not the substance known to us (atoms and ions), but the so-called dark matter. Dark matter apparently consists of new elementary particles that have not yet been discovered in terrestrial experiments. Unlike many known particles, they do not carry an electrical charge and therefore do not emit light; the matter consisting of them is truly dark. The similarity with ordinary matter is that the forces of gravitational attraction cause dark matter to gather into clumps - galaxies and clusters of galaxies. It itself attracts matter and light - it was through the effect of gravitational attraction that dark energy was discovered. Moreover, measurements of gravitational forces in galaxy clusters made it possible to determine the mass of dark matter in these clusters, and ultimately in the Universe as a whole. This is how the total energy density of normal matter was found (the famous formula $E = mc^{2} ).$ is valid for it

And what happened? It turned out that there is clearly not enough normal matter to explain the measured rate of expansion of the Universe. Moreover, it was significant: the “shortage” was about 2/3 (according to modern estimates, about 72%). There were two possible explanations for this fact: either three-dimensional space is curved and the missing contribution to the Hubble parameter is associated with the “elasticity” of space, or there is a new form of energy in the Universe, which later became known as dark energy. Another difficulty with the good old cosmological model was the age of the modern Universe. This model gave an age value of about 9 billion years. At the same time, the age of old star clusters was (and is now estimated) to be 13–14 billion years. Problem! It must be said that the model with curved space, in which the age is about 11 billion years, did not fully cope with the problem (in the model with dark energy, it is about 14 billion years, everything is fine). Few, however, took this difficulty seriously, and the majority expected that it would “somehow resolve itself.”

Wherever you throw it...

From a theoretical point of view, both possibilities—non-Euclidean space and dark energy—looked extremely implausible. Let's start with the curvature of three-dimensional space. As the Universe expands, space smoothes out and its curvature decreases. If the curvature is different from zero now, then it was greater in the past than it is today. However, the energy (mass) density of matter decreases even faster as the Universe expands. This means that in the past the relative contribution of curvature to the Hubble parameter was very small, and the main contribution, by a large margin, was the contribution of matter. In order for 2/3 of the expansion of the Universe today to be ensured by curvature, it is necessary to “adjust” the value of the radius of curvature of space in the past with fantastic accuracy - one second after the Big Bang it should have been equal to one billionth of the then size of the observable Universe, no more and no less! Without such a fit, the curvature today would be either many orders of magnitude greater or many orders of magnitude less than needed to explain the observations. Even if we ignore the hypothesis that the missing contribution to the modern Hubble parameter is provided by the curvature of space, the problem of curvature still remains: in any case, the curvature must be extremely small in the early stages, otherwise it would be too large today. This problem was one of the main considerations that led to the idea of ​​the inflationary stage of the evolution of the Universe. According to the inflationary theory (proposed by A. Starobinsky and independently by A. Gut and formed thanks to the work of A. Linde, A. Albrecht and P. Steinhardt), the Universe at the very early stage of its evolution went through a stage of extremely rapid, exponential expansion.
