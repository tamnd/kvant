During the expansion process, it became more rarefied and cooled; The current temperature of the cosmic microwave background radiation is 2.726 kelvin. In the early stages, matter in the hot Universe was in a state of plasma - protons on their own, electrons on their own. Such a medium is opaque to electromagnetic radiation; photons are constantly scattered, absorbed, and emitted by electrons. When the Universe cooled to 3000 degrees, electrons and protons quickly combined into hydrogen atoms, and the substance became transparent to photons. The era of the transition of matter from the plasma to the gaseous state is the era of the last emission of relict photons. At this time, the age of the Universe was 370 thousand years (modern age is 13.7 billion years). Since then, photons have propagated freely throughout the Universe, their wavelength increasing due to the stretching of space, and today these relict photons are radio waves.

What properties of dark energy are known today? There are few such properties, only three. But what is known may rightly cause astonishment. The first is that, unlike normal matter, dark energy does not cluster and does not gather into objects such as galaxies or their clusters. As far as we know now, dark energy is “spread” evenly throughout the Universe. This statement, like any statement based on observations or experiments, is true to a certain extent. It cannot be completely ruled out that somewhere in the Universe the density of dark energy is a little higher, and somewhere a little less than the average density, somewhere the dark energy is a little denser, and somewhere a little more rarefied. However, it follows from observations that such deviations from homogeneity, if they exist, should be very small in magnitude. We have already talked about the second property: dark energy causes the Universe to expand with acceleration. In this way, dark energy is also strikingly different from normal matter. For normal matter, the usual idea of ​​how gravitational forces “work” is valid: particles of matter formed, say, as a result of an explosion and scattering from the center, gradually slow down their scattering due to gravitational attraction to the center.
