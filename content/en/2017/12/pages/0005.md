ASTRONOMY

BACK

The Orion Nebula is a centimeter-scale cloud of interstellar gas. How low densities are achievable in a “space laboratory”? During a solar eclipse, we see the shining corona of the Sun; its density is $10^{8} - 10^{9} см^{-} 3$. On Earth this is an ultra-high vacuum, but in space it is a very tangible environment. Moving away from the Sun, we see how the solar corona, turning into a stream of solar wind, becomes less and less dense. Near the Earth's orbit, the solar wind density decreases to $10 см^{-} 3$. Clouds of interstellar gas have approximately the same density, and between these clouds the interstellar space is even more rarefied - only $1 см^{-} 3$, or even less. This is a billion times less than the density of the highest laboratory vacuum. Atoms under such conditions can remain alone for a long time, without interacting with other atoms. In this case, their properties appear that cannot be studied in the laboratory, for example, excited states with a long lifetime. Transitions from such states to states with lower energy are “forbidden”, i.e. occur extremely rarely, therefore the corresponding lines in the emission spectrum are also called forbidden. In the laboratory, such an excited atom will certainly collide with its neighbor and transfer energy to it without radiation. And in rarefied space, an atom can fly for a long time

walk without collision until it emits a forbidden line. Therefore, it was in the spectra of interstellar clouds that forbidden transitions in atoms were discovered and studied, which significantly advanced atomic physics. But the interstellar medium is not the limit of emptiness. Intergalactic gas in clusters of galaxies has a density of $10^{-} 4 - 10^{-} 2 см^{-} 3$, in the space between clusters of matter it is even less. And the average concentration of atoms in the Universe is about 3 ◊ $10^{-} 7 см^{-} 3$, in other words - one atom (of hydrogen) per cubic meter of space. This is what astronomers call ultra-high vacuum: a million billion times better than in the laboratory! Now let's turn to high densities. It is very important to study matter under strong compression, if only to understand how it behaves in the bowels of the Earth. Of the high-density natural materials, we are familiar with lead ( 11 g $см^{3} ),$ gold ( 19 g $см^{3} ),$ osmium ( 23 g $см^{3} ).$ The maximum densities and pressures achieved in laboratories on presses with diamond anvils are close to those that we have in the Earth’s core. Laboratory installations have not yet reached the conditions prevailing in the bowels of the giant planets. What can we say about the core of the Sun, where the plasma is compressed to a density of 150 g $см^{3}$ and we have the opportunity to study its behavior by recording neutrinos coming from there. And stars older than our Sun, which have already completed their evolution, leave behind cooling cores - white dwarfs. The density of their matter is difficult to fit into our imagination: $10^{5} - 10^{8}$ g $см^{3}$ That’s 100 tons in a thimble! And there are many such objects around us; astronomers have been studying them for the second century. But the remains of the evolution of massive stars are even more amazing - these are neutron stars with a density of $10^{13} - 10^{14}$ g $см^{3}$. Here our imagination finally gives up - after all, this is 100 million tons in a thimble! Never on Earth will we get matter with such a density in macroscopic quantities!
