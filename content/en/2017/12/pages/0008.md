Mathematics

It would seem that the connection between mathematics and astronomy is obvious: the mathematical apparatus is a tool of natural science. Indeed, in most cases in astronomy, mathematics plays a supporting role: an astronomer, combing the sky with a telescope, discovers new objects, and then mathematics helps him calculate the orbit of the discovered body. But it also happens differently. The French mathematician Urbain Le Verrier (1811–1877), analyzing the motion of the planet Uranus, accidentally discovered by William Herschel (1738–1822), realized that there was another massive body in the Solar System, and predicted the existence of a new planet on the periphery of the Solar System. Based on the deviation of Uranus from the calculated trajectory, Le Verrier calculated the position of the unknown planet, and on his instructions, astronomers immediately discovered it: Neptune was discovered on September 23, 1846, as they said then, “at the tip of a pen.” Has such a mathematical miracle been repeated in the history of astronomy? Not yet. Even Le Verrier’s own attempt to predict another planet near the Sun, inside the orbit of Mercury, was unsuccessful: the planet Vulcan (as it was called in advance) does not exist, and the “irregularities” in the movement of Mercury were explained by Ein’s general theory of relativity.

Researchers from the California Institute of Technology Mike Brown and Konstantin Batygin published a paper in 2016 in which they hypothesized the existence of a planet in the solar system with a mass greater than 10 Earth's and approximately the size of Neptune. The new planet, which researchers call Planet Nine, may orbit the Sun every tens of thousands of years. Its existence is indicated by an analysis of the orbits of large asteroids and dwarf planets discovered in recent years on the distant periphery of the Solar system, in the Kuiper belt. Their orbits are not distributed randomly, but in a certain order. Mathematical modeling has shown that their movement is “conducted” by an unknown massive body – the Ninth Planet.

Orbits of the outer bodies of the Solar System and the estimated orbit of the hypothetical Planet Nine

Its orbit is approximately known, but the mathematical model cannot predict the position of the planet in its orbit. Astronomers have been searching for this planet for two years now, but the task is not easy: due to its distance from the Sun, Planet Nine should look very dim. To find it, you need the sharpest telescopes, and they are in short supply. But, as you know, he who seeks will always find.
