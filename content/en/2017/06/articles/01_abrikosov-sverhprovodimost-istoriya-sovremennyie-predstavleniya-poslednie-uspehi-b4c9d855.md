Superconductivity:

history, modern ideas, recent successes

A. ABRIKOSOV

The phenomenon of superconductivity was discovered by the Dutch physicist Kamerlingh Onnes in 1911. Kamerlingh Onnes was the first to obtain liquid helium, and he used it to create cryostats - devices in which the temperature can be maintained at very low temperatures. In particular, he decided to check the correctness of the ideas that existed at that time about the behavior of electrical resistance at low temperatures. Measuring the resistance of mercury, Kamerlingh Onnes discovered that it abruptly vanishes at a temperature of about 4 K. This phenomenon was called superconductivity, and the temperature of transition to the superconducting state was called critical. Currently, many superconductors are known with a wide variety of critical temperatures, from fractions of a degree to about 100 K. But I will talk about the latter later. Subsequent studies of superconductors revealed many of their remarkable properties. Thus, it turned out that superconductivity is destroyed by a magnetic field. The critical field at which this occurs depends on temperature. It was further discovered that superconductivity disappears even when a sufficiently large current is passed through the sample. Finally, the so-called Meissner effect was discovered, the essence of which is as follows. If you place a metal in a not very strong magnetic field and lower the temperature, then when the metal transitions to the superconducting state, the field lines will extrude

After all, the critical field for a thin layer is higher than for a massive superconductor. Partitioning into layers does not occur because in all pure superconductors consisting of a single metal, there is a special surface energy that arises at the boundaries between the normal and superconducting phases. This energy, like surface tension, tends to reduce the surface area of ​​the boundaries. Microscopic theory explained its origin. It turned out that it is related to the finite size of Cooper pairs. If this size is reduced, the surface energy can change sign and become negative. Thus, a natural division of superconductors arises into superconductors of the first kind - with positive surface energy and superconductors of the second kind - with negative surface energy. It should be noted that type II superconductors are much more common than type I superconductors. Moreover, any superconductor of the first kind can be converted to the second kind. To do this, it is enough to introduce a certain amount of impurity atoms into it or otherwise damage the crystal lattice. Electrons begin to scatter on these defects. The pattern of electron motion changes, and the size of the pairs becomes smaller. With a sufficient concentration of defects, a type 1 superconductor necessarily transforms into a type 2 superconductor. Now I will talk a little about the properties of type II superconductors. Since the surface energy in them is negative, nothing prevents the infinite splitting of their volume into normal and superconducting regions.
