After all, the critical field for a thin layer is higher than for a massive superconductor. Partitioning into layers does not occur because in all pure superconductors consisting of a single metal, there is a special surface energy that arises at the boundaries between the normal and superconducting phases. This energy, like surface tension, tends to reduce the surface area of ​​the boundaries. Microscopic theory explained its origin. It turned out that it is related to the finite size of Cooper pairs. If this size is reduced, the surface energy can change sign and become negative. Thus, a natural division of superconductors arises into superconductors of the first kind - with positive surface energy and superconductors of the second kind - with negative surface energy. It should be noted that type II superconductors are much more common than type I superconductors. Moreover, any superconductor of the first kind can be converted to the second kind. To do this, it is enough to introduce a certain amount of impurity atoms into it or otherwise damage the crystal lattice. Electrons begin to scatter on these defects. The pattern of electron motion changes, and the size of the pairs becomes smaller. With a sufficient concentration of defects, a type 1 superconductor necessarily transforms into a type 2 superconductor. Now I will talk a little about the properties of type II superconductors. Since the surface energy in them is negative, nothing prevents the infinite splitting of their volume into normal and superconducting regions. Therefore, superconductivity in them is gradually displaced with increasing external magnetic field, starting from a certain field value $B_{кр1}$. Transition to normal

This state is realized in the upper critical field $B_{кр2}$. The physical meaning of $B_{кр2}$ is as follows. In a magnetic field, electrons, being charged particles, move along spiral trajectories and the radius of the spiral is inversely proportional to B. If the radius of the spiral becomes smaller than the size of the pair, then the pair can no longer exist and falls apart. If the external magnetic field is below $B_{кр2}$, but above $B_{кр1}$, then it partially penetrates into the superconductor. This happens due to the occurrence of eddy currents in the superconductor. It turns out that these vortices are quantum objects. Each of them carries a magnetic flux quantum. As the external field decreases, the vortices diverge and in the field $B_{кр1}$ completely disappear from the superconductor. In fact, the field $B_{кр1}$ is the field at which one vortex can still exist in a superconductor. The region between fields $B_{кр1}$ and $B_{кр2}$ is called the mixed state. In this state, the superconductor is penetrated by vortex filaments - miniature solenoids arranged in the correct order. In cross section they form a triangular lattice. Each vortex has a core whose size is equal to the size of the Cooper pair; this core can be considered a region of normal metal.
