KVANT 2017/№6

2. The hero is smart, i.e. says “I don’t know” only when it is theoretically impossible to obtain an answer based on the information available to him. This condition allows Ilya Muromets to use indirect knowledge about the coins of other heroes. The second condition is not as harmless as it might seem at first glance. To understand this, let’s look, for example, at a chess game and ask ourselves: which player (playing for black or white) has a strategy that will certainly allow him, regardless of the cunning of the opponent, to win or at least reduce the game to a draw? The number of various chess games, although astronomically large, is still not infinite. This means that it is theoretically possible to analyze all possible games and give an answer to this question (in addition, it can be proven that either exactly one of the players has a winning strategy, or both have a strategy that leads to a draw). This answer will logically follow from the rules of the game, i.e., according to our condition, it should be known to anyone who knows these rules. In reality, however, no one knows the answer. Another example, closer to mathematics: since all the theorems of, say, a school geometry course follow logically from the axioms, then according to our condition it would turn out that someone who has learned all the axioms automatically knows all the theorems. In reality, of course, this requires longer training, and no one knows all the possible theorems. This difficulty is usually called the problem of logical omniscience. It arises in cases where the number of possible situations is so large that a person or even a computer is unable to sort through them, or when it is generally infinite. In our examples, this number is small, and we can safely use the second condition. Now let's discuss the classic problem of dirty children. Task 2. Several children returned from a walk, and dad said that some

(at least one) of them has their faces smeared with dirt. Each child sees the faces of other children, but not his own. Dad asks if any of the children know that he himself is grimy. The children answer “no.” Then dad asks the same question again, then again... Will any of the children understand that he himself is grimy? It would seem that nothing changes, and the children will always say “no”. However, each time the child gains knowledge about the ignorance of others, and this information can help him guess whether he himself is grimy. To better understand what is happening, consider the case when there are only three children and two of them - the second and third - are grimy, and the first is clean. We (and dad) know who is dirty and who is clean, but not the children themselves. Therefore, we need to consider all potentially possible situations, and for brevity we will denote a dirty child with the number 1, and a clean child with the number 0: for example, 010 means that the first and third are clean, the second is grimy. Possible cases can be conveniently represented as a cube (Fig. 2). Here two situations are connected by a red $отрез-_{Рис.} 2$ com if they are indistinguishable from the point of view of the first child (he does not know whether he himself is dirty, so he cannot distinguish, for example, 001 from 101). Green segments connect situations indistinguishable by the second child, blue segments connect situations indistinguishable by the third child. One vertex is missing in this cube: since it is said that there are grimy ones, the case 000 is impossible. Let's now see what changes after the first question. In situations where there is exactly one grimy child (for example, 100), this child immediately understands that he is grimy. This means that if everyone answered “no”, then these situations are impossible and, what is especially important for us, all children now know about this!
