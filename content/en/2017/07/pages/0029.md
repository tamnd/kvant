For further reasoning, we need the following fact, which is valid in four-dimensional space: three different hyperplanes that have a common point have a common line. In fact, by Axiom 2, any two of the three hyperplanes have a common plane. Let's take two planes along which one of the three hyperplanes intersects with the other two. Let us now move on to the proof of our statement. If the three planes mentioned in the condition were located in four-dimensional space, then the statement would be obvious. Indeed, each trihedral angle defines a hyperplane. Two hyperplanes intersect in a plane. This plane does not belong to the third hyperplane (by condition, these hyperplanes intersect one of these planes along three straight lines that do not pass through one point) and, therefore, intersects them in a straight line. Any three corresponding faces of trihedral angles lie in the same hyperplane, defined by the two planes on which the corresponding edges are located, and therefore each triple of corresponding faces has a common point. These three points belong to three hyperplanes defined by trihedral angles and have been proven to lie on the same straight line. Now, to complete the proof, it is enough to “see” in this condition of the problem the projection of the corresponding four-dimensional configuration of planes and trihedral angles.

1. Four pedestrians A, B, C, D are walking along four straight planes at constant speeds. It is known that pedestrians A, B and C meet each other, pedestrian D meets A and B. Prove that pedestrians C and D meet. 2. Three disjoint circles are given on a plane. Consider six points, each of which is the point of intersection of common internal or common external tangents to some two of the given circles. Prove that these six points lie on four lines, three on each. 3. Given three parallel lines and three points on a plane. Construct a triangle whose vertices lie on the given lines and whose sides pass through the given points. 4. Three points A, B and C lie on the same line.
