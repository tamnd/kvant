structure. In both typical compounds La – Ba – Cu – O and Y – Ba – Cu – O, it corresponds to the so-called layered perovskites. Their characteristic feature is layering (periods in two directions are about 2.8 E, and in the third 12 E). In copper “layers,” each copper atom is surrounded by an octahedron of oxygen atoms. Calculations show that the main conduction occurs through the copper-oxygen layers as a result of the overlap of the d-shells of copper with the p-shells of oxygen. Rare earth atoms apparently do not play a role: “free” electrons simply do not enter there. Further, both substances have, in principle, two modifications: tetragonal and orthorhombic. In the first, the unit cell has the form of a regular tetrahedral prism, and in the second, a rectangular parallelepiped with arbitrary edge lengths. But the difference from tetragonality is small. It is interesting to note that pure $La_{2} CuO_{4}$ is orthorhombic at low temperatures, but the addition of Ba suppresses this transition and the substance remains tetragonal. On the contrary, the yttrium compound “1 – 2 – 3” is orthorhombic at low temperatures. In principle, it can also be obtained in a tetragonal modification by expelling part of the oxygen from it by heating, but this modification is not a superconductor. As for the behavior in a magnetic field, the new substances are extreme superconductors of the second type, because the lower field $B_{кр1}$ in them is of the order of $10^{2}$ T, and the upper field $B_{кр2}$ at low temperatures is estimated as $10^{2}$ T. It should be noted that these substances are fragile, and it is not so easy to make wire for a superconducting magnet from them. Another negative property is that the critical current density in them is of the order of $10^{2} 10^{3} A см^{2}$. Moreover, this value drops very quickly when substances are placed in an external magnetic field.
