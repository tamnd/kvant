Kepler discovered the law of planetary motion, but the fact that they move in ellipses was proven by Isaac Newton in his book “Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy” (1687), which served as the basis for all modern theoretical physics. He obtained the ellipticity of planetary trajectories as a consequence of the law of universal gravitation. Note that before Newton, this problem was dealt with by his contemporary Robert Hooke. He studied the law of body motion in a gravitational field, believing that attraction is inversely proportional to the square of the distance. Having approximately integrated the equation of motion, Hooke drew the orbits and saw that they looked like ellipses. Scientific honesty did not allow him to call them ellipses, and he could not prove ellipticity. Therefore, Hooke called the trajectory an elliptoid and suggested that Newton prove that Kepler's first law (planets move in ellipses) follows from the inverse square law. Newton, who knew the ancient theory of conic sections well, coped with this task with the help of ingenious elementary geometric constructions. Subsequently, second-order curves began to appear more and more often in natural science research. Why has this model been so fruitful for applications? Why, in particular, does the cone section model describe the motions of the planets? Mysticism. Mystery. There is no answer to this question. We believe in the power of rational science. Newton saw in this proof of the existence of God: “Such a most graceful combination of the Sun, planets and comets could not have happened except by the intention and power of a powerful and wise being... This One rules everything not as the soul of the world, but as the ruler of the Universe, and by His dominion should be called the Lord God Almighty.” Modern space researchers, when designing the launch of artificial satellites, also use the properties of conic sections.
