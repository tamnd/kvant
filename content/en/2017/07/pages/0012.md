The so-called tunnel effect is known from quantum mechanics - the ability of particles to penetrate through a potential barrier, even if the height of this barrier is higher than the energy of the particles. Of course, in reality the width of the barrier should be very small. The tunnel effect was used by I. Giever in 1960 to create a tunnel contact: two metal electrodes are separated by an insulator layer (usually an oxide film on the surface of one of the metals is taken as an insulator). Thanks to the tunnel effect, current can flow through such a contact. If one of these metals is a superconductor, then the electrons in it are combined into pairs. But for a whole couple, penetration through the barrier is very unlikely. Therefore, it is necessary for the electric field to split the pair, and then the electrons will pass through the contact one by one. The minimum energy per electron is equal in this case, and, therefore, at T = 0, the flow of current begins only when the potential difference between the electrodes reaches such a value U that eU. Superconducting tunnel contact. A superconductor film (1) is applied to the glass substrate (3). Then it is oxidized - a dielectric layer (2) with a thickness of about ten angstroms is created on the surface of the superconductor. Another film (1) of superconductor is applied on top. For ease of measurement, superconducting films are “laid” on a substrate in the form of a cross. With some probability, electrons from one film can penetrate into another through the dielectric layer - this is called the tunneling effect. This creates the possibility of not just current flowing through the contact, but superconducting current. This effect was predicted by the English physicist B. Josephson in 1962 and, after experimental confirmation, was named after him. The critical Josephson current is very small, its density is no more than $10^{2} 10^{3}$ A $см^{2}$. This value should be compared with the currents in magnets - of the order of $10^{5} 10^{6}$ A $см^{2}$ or with the “theoretical limit” for pair breakup in a superconductor - of the order of $10^{8}$ A $см^{2}$. However, the Josephson effect has received a new, very promising application. The fact is that the value of the Josephson critical current turned out to be unusually sensitive to the external magnetic field. This made it possible to create special superconducting devices - Josephson interferometers, or SQUIDS, which make it possible to measure magnetic fields up to $10^{14}$ T (the Earth's magnetic field is 0.5 $10^{4}$ T), and then use this field to measure currents up to $10^{14}$ A and potential differences up to $10^{15}$ V. SQUIDS are already used in biology and medicine, for they provide much more accurate data than electrocardiograms or encephalographs, and are superior even to X-ray and NMR tomographs. In addition, Josephson contacts can be used to detect very weak electromagnetic radiation
