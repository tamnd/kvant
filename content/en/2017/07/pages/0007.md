LET'S STUDY

This mathematics course is optional: a third of high school students, for example, do not study algebra. The following example shows what this leads to. The test for 14-year-old American schoolchildren was asked to estimate (not calculate, but only estimate) what would happen to the number 120 if 80% of it was taken. And three answer options were offered: increased; remained the same; decreased. Approximately 30% of respondents placed crosses next to the correct answer. In other words, the students placed crosses at random. Conclusion: no one knows anything. The second feature of the American approach to teaching mathematics is its computerization. Passion for computers in itself does not contribute to the development of thinking. Here's another example from an American test: there are 26 students in a class. You need to take them on a tour in cars. One parent and 4 students can travel in one car. How many parents should you ask to help? Typical answer: 65 parents. The computer outputs: 26: 4 = 6.5. Well, the schoolchild already knows that if the solution must contain integers, then something needs to be done with the decimal point, for example, discarded. Here's an example from the 1992 American undergraduate exam: "Which of the following is most similar to the relationship between angle and degree: a) time and hour, b) milk and quart, c) area and square inch (etc.)." Answer: area and square inch, since a degree is the minimum unit of angle, and a square inch is an area, while an hour is also divided into minutes. The compilers of this problem are clearly trained in American $системе.^{1}$ I'm afraid that we too will reach this level.

1 New York professor Joe Bierman explained to me that for him, as an American, the “right” solution to this problem was quite obvious. “The point is,” he said, “that I can accurately imagine the degree of idiocy of the compilers of these problems.”

MATHEMATICS?

One can only be surprised that there are so many wonderful mathematicians and physicists in the United States (though many of them are immigrants; the best students at American universities today are Chinese). Now our mathematical education is slowly turning from the European system to the American one. As always, we are late, we are 30 years behind Europe, and we must be prepared to save the situation in 30 years and get out of this dead end into which the Americanization of education with its pragmatism, optionality, and widespread computerization will lead us. Our traditional domestic teaching of mathematics was of a higher level and was based on the culture of arithmetic problems. Until two decades ago, families retained the old “merchant” tasks. Now it's lost. The algebraization of the latest mathematics teaching reform turns schoolchildren into automata. Namely, the arithmetic approach demonstrates the meaningfulness of the mathematics we teach. Consider, for example, the following problems: 1. There are 3 apples, 1 was taken. How many are left? 2. How many cuts do you need to make for the log to split into 3 parts? 3. I have 3 more sisters than brothers. How many more sisters than brothers are there in our family? From the point of view of arithmetic, these are all different problems - they have different content. The intellectual effort required to solve these problems is completely different, although the algebraic model is the same: 3 – 1 = 2. What is most striking about mathematics is the amazing universality of its models and their incomprehensible effectiveness in applications. Let us remember V.V. Mayakovsky: “The person who first formulated that “two and two are four” is a great mathematician, even if he received this truth from adding two cigarette butts with two cigarette butts. All subsequent people, even if they added up immeasurably larger things, for example, a steam locomotive with a steam locomotive, are not mathematicians.”
