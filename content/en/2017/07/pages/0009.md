Let's return to Kepler. At first Kepler thought that the orbit of Mars was a circle. However, the Sun turned out to be not in the center, but shifted by about 1 10 parts of the radius. But Kepler did not stop at this (already remarkable) result - because he knew the theory of conic sections. Kepler knew that an ellipse with a small eccentricity is very similar to a circle, and he tested how the small deviation of the orbit from the circle that still remained behaved. Interestingly, this could only be done thanks to the exceptional accuracy of Tycho Brahe’s observations made with the naked eye. In those days, astronomers did not really trust telescopes, and even at the end of the 17th century it was necessary to prove that telescopic observations could achieve just as great accuracy as observations with the naked eye. New physics often begins with a refinement of the last significant digit of a previous theory - if Kepler had been content with his eccentric circular orbit, or if Tycho Brahe's observations had been less accurate, the development of celestial mechanics (and perhaps all theoretical physics) might have been delayed - perhaps even for centuries. The orbit of Mars turned out to be slightly flattened in the direction perpendicular to the diameter on which the Sun lies - by about half a percent, i.e. to $e^{2} 2$. This is how Kepler came to the idea of ​​elliptical orbits of planets. If the theory of conic sections had not been developed in advance by mathematicians, then the fundamental laws of nature would not have been discovered in a timely manner, modern science and technology would not have arisen, and our civilization would have remained at the medieval level - or, at least, the paths of history would have been completely different.
