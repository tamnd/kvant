An example containing 3800 chips turns out, let's say, like this. Let’s select a “border” of width 5 from the 200 ¥ 200 square. This border consists of four corner squares 5 ¥ 5 and four rectangles 5 ¥ 190. Let’s place the chips in these four rectangles: red in the left and top, and blue in the right and bottom. It is easy to see that this example meets all the requirements and has 1900 red and blue chips. It remains to prove that there cannot be more than 3800 chips. Consider an arbitrary arrangement of chips that satisfies the requirements. Let's call a row (row or column) multi-colored if it contains chips of both colors. Let us make two useful remarks at once. Firstly, each chip sees some chip of a different color, so each chip lies in at least one different-colored row. Additionally, since a multi-colored row contains a red tile, it cannot have more than five blue tiles (otherwise the red one will see them all). Similarly, in a multi-colored row there are no more than five red chips, i.e. no more than 10 chips in total. Now it is not difficult to get the required estimate. If there are 191 multi-colored lines, then they contain no more than 191 ◊ 10 = 1910 chips, and the remaining nine lines have no more than 9 ◊ 200 = 1800 chips, for a total of no more than 1910 + 1800 < 3800 chips. The case when there are 191 multi-colored columns is analyzed in a similar way. If there are no more than 190 of each, then they contain no more than 190 ◊ 10 + 190 ◊ 10 = 3800 chips, and all the chips are contained in these rows. The assessment is proven.
