It is difficult to even imagine how many different applications superconductors would have received if not for one sad circumstance. All superconducting devices used so far require cooling with liquid helium. The cost of one liter of this refrigerant is 10 rubles (remember that the article was published in 1988 - editor's note), and this greatly increases the cost of using superconductors. However, recently, so-called superconducting oxides, or ceramics, have appeared, with a critical temperature in the region of 95 K. This is already noticeably higher than the boiling point of liquid nitrogen (77 K), the cost of which is 10 kopecks per liter. It is possible that higher temperature materials will be found. (At the beginning of 1988, it became known about the discovery of superconductors Bi–Sr–Ca–Cu–O and Tl–Ba–Ca–Cu–O with a critical temperature of 105–125 K.) And now I will dwell on the history of the discovery and the properties of such superconducting ceramics. From 1973 until mid-1986, the record for the maximum critical temperature belonged to films of $Nb_{3} Ge$, which retained superconductivity up to 23 K. However, in the fall of 1986, a message appeared from physicists G. Bednorz and A. Muller (Switzerland) about the discovery of superconductivity of the La – Ba – Cu – O compound with a critical temperature in the region of 30 K. These authors did not immediately approach their discovery. The fact is that even before them, the superconducting oxide Ba – Pb – Bi – O with a critical temperature of 14 K was known. The strange thing about this compound was that the density of free electrons carrying current was $10^{21} см^{3}$, which is an order of magnitude lower than that of ordinary metals. At the same time, according to the Bardeen–Cooper–Schrieffer (BCS) theory, the value of the critical temperature increases with the number of free electrons. It is clear that a decrease in the number of electrons should lead to a decrease in $T_{кр}$. And 14 K was a relatively high critical temperature. Next, the attention of Bednorz and Müller was attracted by oxides containing copper in a state with an intermediate valency: partly Cu, and partly Cu. Such oxides were studied by French physicists. They included the rare earth element lanthanum as a structural element. If we take compound $La_{2} CuO_{4}$, then the copper in it is only divalent, and this substance behaves as an insulator. Müller and Bednorz began to replace trivalent lanthanum with divalent elements in order to take away some of the electrons from copper and thereby partially convert copper into the trivalent state. As a result, the first high-temperature superconductor $La_{2} x Ba_{x} CuO_{4}$ was obtained, where x = 0.1–0.2. At first, no one believed Bednorz and Muller, and the leading American physics journal, Physical Review Letters, refused to publish their article. Then they sent it to the German journal Zeitschrift fur Physik, where it was published in the fall of 1986. Initially the article did not arouse interest. But then Japanese experts decided to check the message and were convinced that Muller and Bednorz were not mistaken. After this, these compounds were studied by American physicists, and from the beginning of 1987 a real “superconducting boom” broke out. There are now several thousand articles on this topic. I can't talk about all the details of this race, but I will note the main points. It was natural to try to increase the critical temperature by replacing the elements with their chemical analogues. Replacing Ba with Sr led to $T_{кр} = 45$ K. An American group from Houston, led by P. Chu, subjected these samples to compression and found that the critical temperature increases rapidly during compression, although in ordinary superconductors under similar conditions Tcr, as a rule, decreases slightly.
