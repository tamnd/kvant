True, in films consisting of oriented crystallites, a critical current density of up to $10^{6} A см^{2}$ was obtained at the boiling point of liquid nitrogen (T = 77 K), but the films cannot serve as windings for superconducting magnets. Currently, the efforts of many laboratories around the world are focused on attempts to obtain single crystals of new superconductors. It was possible to make plates with a side of up to 1 cm and a thickness of up to 3 mm. The study of such samples confirms that these substances are layered: the resistance across the layers is tens of times greater than the resistance along them. By the way, I note that in normal condition these are poor conductors: the resistivity is 100 times greater than that of copper. For new superconductors, different measurements give values ​​for this value from 3 to 12. Further, these substances have a number of specific features, but it is not very clear which of them are of fundamental importance. For example: what role does the layering of crystals play; Are lanthanum or yttrium atoms essential to the mechanism of superconductivity, or do they simply play the role of a mechanical truss that holds the crystal lattice together? What is the role of oxygen? It is known that in the yttrium compound there are layers consisting of chains Cu – O – Cu – O, and there are planes in which there are two oxygen atoms per copper atom. When oxygen is removed, it first leaves the chains, and the substance loses superconductivity. But in the lanthanum compound there are no such chains.

So, it’s not very clear what to cling to. It has been experimentally established that in new superconductors, electrons are combined into Cooper pairs. But what is the mechanism of attraction? The mechanism of transfer of phonons - quanta of lattice vibrations - entails an isotopic effect, i.e. change in Tcr with transition to another isotope. Measurements were made on samples with the isotope $O^{16}$ replaced by $O^{18}$. For the lanthanum compound, the effect was observed, although less than predicted by the BCS theory. But in a 90-degree yttrium superconductor, as well as in the same substance with europium instead of yttrium, this effect is practically absent. Hence the conclusion is drawn that in addition to the long-known phonon mechanism, there is another mechanism for transmitting interaction between electrons. In principle, quasiparticles of other types can exist in a substance – for example, those associated with the excitation of electrons remote from conducting layers. They are called plasmons. There is another idea: the substance may be close to the transition to a magnetically ordered state. This state has its own quasiparticles - magnons. But even if there is no real ordering, it can arise in the form of fluctuations and create interaction between electrons. Theories have emerged that take advantage of this circumstance. I cannot list all the theories - there are a lot of them. I will just note the very interesting concept of two-electron centers. It is known that oxygen leaves very easily from new superconductors, while in ordinary oxides it is bound very tightly. There is a concept that two electrons can immediately leave the oxygen atoms for the copper; this makes the oxygen neutral and thus makes it easier to escape from the lattice. And the fact that the electrons are sometimes in a collectivized metallic state, sometimes localized in pairs on oxygen, leads to their attraction.
