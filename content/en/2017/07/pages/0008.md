KVANT 2017/№7

Counting locomotives is the American way of mathematics education. This is doom. The example with the development of physics shows that “locomotive” mathematics at the beginning of our century turned out to be worse than “cigarette-butt” mathematics: applied mathematics did not keep up with physics, but theoretical mathematics found everything that was necessary for the further development of physics. “Locomotive” mathematics cannot keep up with practice: while we are learning to count on an abacus, computers appear. We need to teach how to think, not how to press buttons. True, a mathematical model does not always provide immediate practical results. It happens that it will be useful only after two thousand years. An example of this is conic sections, they were discovered in Ancient Greece and described by Apollonius of Perga (c. 260 - c. 170 BC) in an 8-volume treatise. And Johannes Kepler needed this theory in the 16th century, when he deduced the laws of planetary motion. His teacher Tycho Brahe at the Uraniborg Observatory scrupulously measured the positions of the planets in the solar system for 20 years. After the death of his teacher, Kepler took up the mathematical processing of the results of these observations and discovered that, for example, the trajectory of Mars is an ellipse. An ellipse is a locus of points on a plane, the sum of the distances from which to two given points, called foci, is constant. The fact that the section of a cone by a plane sufficiently inclined to its axis is an ellipse is a remarkable geometric theorem, which, unfortunately, cannot be proven in school. Its proof is very simple (Fig. 1). Spheres inscribed in a cone and touching the plane (at the foci E and F of the ellipse), on which the proof is based, are called Dandelin spheres. To understand Kepler's reasoning, we need some simple facts about the geometry of the ellipse.
