In fact, the field $B_{кр1}$ is the field at which one vortex can still exist in a superconductor. The region between fields $B_{кр1}$ and $B_{кр2}$ is called the mixed state. In this state, the superconductor is penetrated by vortex filaments - miniature solenoids arranged in the correct order. In cross section they form a triangular lattice. Each vortex has a core whose size is equal to the size of the Cooper pair; this core can be considered a region of normal metal. It turns out to be very significant that by increasing the concentration of defects, it is possible to increase the critical field Bcr2, up to which a superconducting current can flow through the sample. You can make a solenoid and short circuit it. This will be a superconducting permanent magnet. The difference from a conventional electromagnet is that in a conventional magnet, the current energy is ultimately spent on heating the winding. Because of this, it is necessary to create a bulky and expensive cooling system. It is this circumstance that limits the capabilities of electromagnets with fields up to 5–6 Tesla. The so-called tunnel effect is known from quantum mechanics - the ability of particles to penetrate through a potential barrier, even if the height of this barrier is higher than the energy of the particles. Of course, in reality the width of the barrier should be very small. The tunnel effect was used by I. Giever in 1960 to create a tunnel contact: two metal electrodes are separated by an insulator layer (usually an oxide film on the surface of one of the metals is taken as an insulator). Thanks to the tunnel effect, current can flow through such a contact. If one of these metals is a superconductor, then the electrons in it are combined into pairs. But for a whole couple, penetration through the barrier is very unlikely. Therefore, it is necessary for the electric field to split the pair, and then the electrons will pass through the contact one by one. It is difficult to even imagine how many different applications superconductors would have received if not for one sad circumstance. All superconducting devices used so far require cooling with liquid helium. The cost of one liter of this refrigerant is 10 rubles (remember that the article was published in 1988 - editor's note), and this greatly increases the cost of using superconductors. However, recently, so-called superconducting oxides, or ceramics, have appeared, with a critical temperature in the region of 95 K. This is already noticeably higher than the boiling point of liquid nitrogen (77 K), the cost of which is 10 kopecks per liter. It is possible that higher temperature materials will be found. (At the beginning of 1988, it became known about the discovery of superconductors Bi–Sr–Ca–Cu–O and Tl–Ba–Ca–Cu–O with a critical temperature of 105–125 K.) And now I will dwell on the history of the discovery and the properties of such superconducting ceramics. From 1973 until mid-1986, the record for the maximum critical temperature belonged to films of $Nb_{3} Ge$, which retained superconductivity up to 23 K. However, in the fall of 1986, a message appeared from physicists G. Bednorz and A. Muller (Switzerland) about the discovery of superconductivity of the La – Ba – Cu – O compound with a critical temperature in the region of 30 K. These authors did not immediately approach their discovery. The fact is that even before them, the superconducting oxide Ba – Pb – Bi – O with a critical temperature of 14 K was known. The strange thing about this compound was that the density of free electrons carrying current was $10^{21} см^{3}$, which is an order of magnitude lower than that of ordinary metals. At the same time, according to the Bardeen–Cooper–Schrieffer (BCS) theory, the value of the critical temperature increases with the number of free electrons. It is clear that a decrease in the number of electrons should lead to a decrease in $T_{кр}$. And 14 K was a relatively high critical temperature. Next, the attention of Bednorz and Müller was attracted by oxides containing copper in a state with an intermediate valency: partly Cu, and partly Cu. Such oxides were studied by French physicists. They included the rare earth element lanthanum as a structural element. If we take compound $La_{2} CuO_{4}$, then the copper in it is only divalent, and this substance behaves as an insulator. Müller and Bednorz began to replace trivalent lanthanum with divalent elements in order to take away some of the electrons from copper and thereby partially convert copper into the trivalent state. As a result, the first high-temperature superconductor $La_{2} x Ba_{x} CuO_{4}$ was obtained, where x = 0.1–0.2. At first, no one believed Bednorz and Muller, and the leading American physics journal, Physical Review Letters, refused to publish their article. Then they sent it to the German journal Zeitschrift fur Physik, where it was published in the fall of 1986. Initially the article did not arouse interest. But then Japanese experts decided to check the message and were convinced that Muller and Bednorz were not mistaken. After this, these compounds were studied by American physicists, and from the beginning of 1987 a real “superconducting boom” broke out. There are now several thousand articles on this topic. I can't talk about all the details of this race, but I will note the main points. It was natural to try to increase the critical temperature by replacing the elements with their chemical analogues. Replacing Ba with Sr led to $T_{кр} = 45$ K. An American group from Houston, led by P. Chu, subjected these samples to compression and found that the critical temperature increases rapidly during compression, although in ordinary superconductors under similar conditions Tcr, as a rule, decreases slightly. Then they decided to try to create a “chemical” compression by replacing the lanthanum atoms with smaller yttrium atoms. The result was a connection with a fantastically high

Their characteristic feature is layering (periods in two directions are about 2.8 E, and in the third 12 E). In copper “layers,” each copper atom is surrounded by an octahedron of oxygen atoms. Calculations show that the main conduction occurs through the copper-oxygen layers as a result of the overlap of the d-shells of copper with the p-shells of oxygen. Rare earth atoms apparently do not play a role: “free” electrons simply do not enter there. Further, both substances have, in principle, two modifications: tetragonal and orthorhombic. In the first, the unit cell has the form of a regular tetrahedral prism, and in the second, a rectangular parallelepiped with arbitrary edge lengths. But the difference from tetragonality is small. It is interesting to note that pure $La_{2} CuO_{4}$ is orthorhombic at low temperatures, but the addition of Ba suppresses this transition and the substance remains tetragonal. On the contrary, the yttrium compound “1 – 2 – 3” is orthorhombic at low temperatures. In principle, it can also be obtained in a tetragonal modification by expelling part of the oxygen from it by heating, but this modification is not a superconductor. As for the behavior in a magnetic field, the new substances are extreme superconductors of the second type, because the lower field $B_{кр1}$ in them is of the order of $10^{2}$ T, and the upper field $B_{кр2}$ at low temperatures is estimated as $10^{2}$ T. It should be noted that these substances are fragile, and it is not so easy to make wire for a superconducting magnet from them. Another negative property is that the critical current density in them is of the order of $10^{2} 10^{3} A см^{2}$. Moreover, this value drops very quickly when substances are placed in an external magnetic field. Further, these substances have a number of specific features, but it is not very clear which of them are of fundamental importance. For example: what role does the layering of crystals play; Are lanthanum or yttrium atoms essential to the mechanism of superconductivity, or do they simply play the role of a mechanical truss that holds the crystal lattice together? What is the role of oxygen? It is known that in the yttrium compound there are layers consisting of chains Cu – O – Cu – O, and there are planes in which there are two oxygen atoms per copper atom. When oxygen is removed, it first leaves the chains, and the substance loses superconductivity. But in the lanthanum compound there are no such chains.

So, it’s not very clear what to cling to. It has been experimentally established that in new superconductors, electrons are combined into Cooper pairs. But what is the mechanism of attraction? The mechanism of transfer of phonons - quanta of lattice vibrations - entails an isotopic effect, i.e. change in Tcr with transition to another isotope. Measurements were made on samples with the isotope $O^{16}$ replaced by $O^{18}$. For the lanthanum compound, the effect was observed, although less than predicted by the BCS theory. But in a 90-degree yttrium superconductor, as well as in the same substance with europium instead of yttrium, this effect is practically absent. Hence the conclusion is drawn that in addition to the long-known phonon mechanism, there is another mechanism for transmitting interaction between electrons. In principle, quasiparticles of other types can exist in a substance – for example, those associated with the excitation of electrons remote from conducting layers. They are called plasmons. There is another idea: the substance may be close to the transition to a magnetically ordered state. This state has its own quasiparticles - magnons. But even if there is no real ordering, it can arise in the form of fluctuations and create interaction between electrons. Theories have emerged that take advantage of this circumstance. I cannot list all the theories - there are a lot of them. I will just note the very interesting concept of two-electron centers. It is known that oxygen leaves very easily from new superconductors, while in ordinary oxides it is bound very tightly. There is a concept that two electrons can immediately leave the oxygen atoms for the copper; this makes the oxygen neutral and thus makes it easier to escape from the lattice. And the fact that the electrons are sometimes in a collectivized metallic state, sometimes localized in pairs on oxygen, leads to their attraction.
