WHY SHOULD YOU STUDY MATHEMATICS? In 1267, the English philosopher Roger Bacon already answered this question: “He who does not know mathematics cannot learn any other science and cannot even reveal his ignorance.” Actually, this could be the end of the lecture, but people think that maybe something has changed over seven centuries... Let's listen to more modern evidence - one of the creators of quantum mechanics, Paul Dirac, claims that when constructing a physical theory, “one should not trust all physical concepts.” What to trust? “Trust a mathematical scheme, even if at first glance it is not related to physics.” Indeed, all purely physical concepts of the beginning of the century have been discarded by physics, and the mathematical models adopted by physicists are gradually acquiring physical content. And this showed the stability of mathematics. So, mathematical modeling is a productive method of knowledge in natural science. We will approach mathematical models from a different angle, looking at problems in mathematics education.

Academician Vladimir Igorevich Arnold gave a lecture on this topic on April 16, 1992 at the Republican Institute for Advanced Training of Education Workers (Moscow). The recording of the lecture was provided to us by Associate Professor Yu. Fominykh (Perm). This article was first published in «Kvant» No. 1/2, 1993.

In our mathematical education (both secondary and higher) we follow the footsteps of the European system, based on the “Bourbakization” of mathematics. A group of young French mathematicians, speaking under the pseudonym Nicolas Bourbaki, published several books beginning in 1939 that formally (i.e., using the axiomatic method) presented the main branches of modern mathematics based on set theory. The formalization of mathematics also leads to a certain formalization of its teaching. This is where the costs of the “burbakization” of mathematical education are manifested. A typical example. Second grade students in a French school are asked the question: “What is two plus three?” Answer: – Since addition is commutative, it will be three plus two. Wonderful answer! It is absolutely correct, but it doesn’t even occur to the student to add these two numbers, because when teaching, the emphasis is on the properties of operations. Europe has already realized the shortcomings of this approach to education, and a rollback from “bourbakization” has begun. In recent years, the Americanization of mathematics education has been taking place in our country. It is based on the principle: teach what is needed for practice. The test for 14-year-old American schoolchildren was asked to estimate (not calculate, but only estimate) what would happen to the number 120 if 80% of it was taken. MATHEMATICS?

One can only be surprised that there are so many wonderful mathematicians and physicists in the United States (though many of them are immigrants; the best students at American universities today are Chinese). Now our mathematical education is slowly turning from the European system to the American one. As always, we are late, we are 30 years behind Europe, and we must be prepared to save the situation in 30 years and get out of this dead end into which the Americanization of education with its pragmatism, optionality, and widespread computerization will lead us. Our traditional domestic teaching of mathematics was of a higher level and was based on the culture of arithmetic problems. Until two decades ago, families retained the old “merchant” tasks. Now it's lost. The algebraization of the latest mathematics teaching reform turns schoolchildren into automata. Namely, the arithmetic approach demonstrates the meaningfulness of the mathematics we teach. Consider, for example, the following problems: 1. There are 3 apples, 1 was taken. How many are left? 2. How many cuts do you need to make for the log to split into 3 parts? 3. I have 3 more sisters than brothers. How many more sisters than brothers are there in our family? From the point of view of arithmetic, these are all different problems - they have different content. The intellectual effort required to solve these problems is completely different, although the algebraic model is the same: 3 – 1 = 2. What is most striking about mathematics is the amazing universality of its models and their incomprehensible effectiveness in applications. Let us remember V.V. Mayakovsky: “The person who first formulated that “two and two are four” is a great mathematician, even if he received this truth from adding two cigarette butts with two cigarette butts. All subsequent people, even if they added up immeasurably larger things, for example, a steam locomotive with a steam locomotive, are not mathematicians.”

Counting locomotives is the American way of mathematics education. This is doom. The example with the development of physics shows that “locomotive” mathematics at the beginning of our century turned out to be worse than “cigarette-butt” mathematics: applied mathematics did not keep up with physics, but theoretical mathematics found everything that was necessary for the further development of physics. “Locomotive” mathematics cannot keep up with practice: while we are learning to count on an abacus, computers appear. We need to teach how to think, not how to press buttons. True, a mathematical model does not always provide immediate practical results. It happens that it will be useful only after two thousand years. An example of this is conic sections, they were discovered in Ancient Greece and described by Apollonius of Perga (c. 260 - c. 170 BC) in an 8-volume treatise. And Johannes Kepler needed this theory in the 16th century, when he deduced the laws of planetary motion. His teacher Tycho Brahe at the Uraniborg Observatory scrupulously measured the positions of the planets in the solar system for 20 years. After the death of his teacher, Kepler took up the mathematical processing of the results of these observations and discovered that, for example, the trajectory of Mars is an ellipse. An ellipse is a locus of points on a plane, the sum of the distances from which to two given points, called foci, is constant. The fact that the section of a cone by a plane sufficiently inclined to its axis is an ellipse is a remarkable geometric theorem, which, unfortunately, cannot be proven in school. Its proof is very simple (Fig. 1). Spheres inscribed in a cone and touching the plane (at the foci E and F of the ellipse), on which the proof is based, are called Dandelin spheres. To understand Kepler's reasoning, we need some simple facts about the geometry of the ellipse. The tail was saved (jet deflection is proportional to A), and the resulting thrust force remained virtually unchanged (loss of $z A^{2} 2$, where A is the angle in radians; for an angle of 3o, only about 1,800 power is lost). Let's return to Kepler. At first Kepler thought that the orbit of Mars was a circle. However, the Sun turned out to be not in the center, but shifted by about 1 10 parts of the radius. But Kepler did not stop at this (already remarkable) result - because he knew the theory of conic sections. Kepler knew that an ellipse with a small eccentricity is very similar to a circle, and he tested how the small deviation of the orbit from the circle that still remained behaved. Interestingly, this could only be done thanks to the exceptional accuracy of Tycho Brahe’s observations made with the naked eye. In those days, astronomers did not really trust telescopes, and even at the end of the 17th century it was necessary to prove that telescopic observations could achieve just as great accuracy as observations with the naked eye. New physics often begins with a refinement of the last significant digit of a previous theory - if Kepler had been content with his eccentric circular orbit, or if Tycho Brahe's observations had been less accurate, the development of celestial mechanics (and perhaps all theoretical physics) might have been delayed - perhaps even for centuries. The orbit of Mars turned out to be slightly flattened in the direction perpendicular to the diameter on which the Sun lies - by about half a percent, i.e. to $e^{2} 2$. This is how Kepler came to the idea of ​​elliptical orbits of planets. If the theory of conic sections had not been developed in advance by mathematicians, then the fundamental laws of nature would not have been discovered in a timely manner, modern science and technology would not have arisen, and our civilization would have remained at the medieval level - or, at least, the paths of history would have been completely different.

Kepler discovered the law of planetary motion, but the fact that they move in ellipses was proven by Isaac Newton in his book “Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy” (1687), which served as the basis for all modern theoretical physics. He obtained the ellipticity of planetary trajectories as a consequence of the law of universal gravitation. Note that before Newton, this problem was dealt with by his contemporary Robert Hooke. He studied the law of body motion in a gravitational field, believing that attraction is inversely proportional to the square of the distance. Having approximately integrated the equation of motion, Hooke drew the orbits and saw that they looked like ellipses. Scientific honesty did not allow him to call them ellipses, and he could not prove ellipticity. Therefore, Hooke called the trajectory an elliptoid and suggested that Newton prove that Kepler's first law (planets move in ellipses) follows from the inverse square law. Newton, who knew the ancient theory of conic sections well, coped with this task with the help of ingenious elementary geometric constructions. Subsequently, second-order curves began to appear more and more often in natural science research. Why has this model been so fruitful for applications? Why, in particular, does the cone section model describe the motions of the planets? Mysticism. Mystery. There is no answer to this question. We believe in the power of rational science. Newton saw in this proof of the existence of God: “Such a most graceful combination of the Sun, planets and comets could not have happened except by the intention and power of a powerful and wise being... This One rules everything not as the soul of the world, but as the ruler of the Universe, and by His dominion should be called the Lord God Almighty.” Modern space researchers, when designing the launch of artificial satellites, also use the properties of conic sections. Long before the appearance of the first computers in mathematics, two of their main mathematical components were prepared: mathematical logic (the algebra of logic was developed by George Boole, 1815–1864) and the circuit diagram of a computer. By the time Schrödinger turned to the oscillatory problem, the matrix version of quantum mechanics by W. Heisenberg was already known. The question arose: how to obtain from the theory of waves in all space a discrete rather than a continuous spectrum? Schrödinger was helped by the famous German mathematician Hermann Weil. Without his results on spectral theory on an unlimited interval, we would never have known about the famous Schrödinger equation. Again the same story: a mathematician was found who already had a theory ready - boundary conditions at infinity - all that was left was to use it.
