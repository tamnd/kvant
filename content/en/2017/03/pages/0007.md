MASS TRANSFER

The sputtering rate is small: the incident ion penetrates too deeply into the material and the atoms knocked out of their places do not reach the surface. It is clear why the sputtering coefficient is small at low energies. But a much larger number of facts are unclear or not entirely clear. For example, during ion bombardment, a relief may appear on the surface, and a rather complex one at that. And polishing and smoothing of the surface when bombarded with low-energy ions is also possible. This is apparently due to the fact that the atoms located on the protrusions have a lower binding energy (they need to be separated from a smaller number of atoms) and therefore it is possible to choose the energy of the ions in such a way that these atoms will be sputtered strongly, and the rest - weakly. At a high flux density of ions bombarding the electrode, the concentration of atoms embedded in it and lattice defects that arise in it reaches such values ​​that the crystal lattice is completely destroyed and the material passes into an amorphous state. At the same time, the clearly defined melting point disappears, and this incomprehensible mixture of metal and gas begins to creep and float (like a candle) under the influence of gravity. But what happens during cathode sputtering of multicomponent compounds is even more complicated. You can still guess why an admixture of one metal to another sometimes causes the formation of a relief during sputtering: a weakly sputtering impurity, if its atoms are concentrated in certain areas, protects the material underneath it from sputtering. But why adding a few percent bismuth to copper increases the sputtering efficiency by half or why adding a few percent aluminum to gold reduces sputtering by a factor of ten is unknown. There are several theories for sputtering alloys, sometimes one or the other is able to correctly predict the results of individual experiments.

Mass transfer during current flow

The most obvious is mass transfer during melt electrolysis. The greater the mass of the ions and the lesser their charge, the greater it is, and the lesser it is, the lighter the ions and the greater the charge. In the first case, our attention is drawn to CsI, it melts at approximately $630 \infty C$, in the second - BN, it melts at $3000 \infty C$. At these temperatures, they already conduct current fairly well, but in general, a melt is not necessary for mass transfer during ionic conductivity. In the solid phase, mass transfer will also occur - the Faraday number does not care what state we are talking about. And if we talk not about melts, but about solutions, then mass transfer will only increase due to solvation (electrostatic interaction between particles of a solute and a solvent) - each ion will drag a shell of solvent molecules behind it. True, BN has poor solubility, so you will have to limit yourself to LiF. Now let's look at electronic conductors. With the most ordinary current, not only charge, but also mass is transferred in a wire, although the ratio of mass to charge is the ratio of the mass of the electron to its charge, i.e. approximately 5.7 ◊ $10^{-} 12$ kg Cl. Exactly the same thing happens when an electron current flows in a vacuum, and when an ionic current flows in a vacuum, the mass transfer will be of the same order as in the electrolyte. The situation is more complicated in plasma, where both electron and ion currents flow simultaneously. If the mean free paths and energies of ions and electrons are approximately equal, then (try to show this by calculation) the addition to the thermal speed that particles acquire under the influence of an electric field is inversely proportional to the root of the mass, and the “mass flow,” accordingly, is simply proportional to the root of the mass. The current in such a plasma is predominantly electronic, but the mass is carried mainly by ions.
