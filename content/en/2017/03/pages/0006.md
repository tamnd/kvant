Tentatively, we can assume that the following elements are more resistant to destruction by ion flows: C, B, Be, Al, Ti, V, Zr, Nb, Ta, Mo, W, Fe, as well as their alloys and compounds. The third situation is when we can choose the material and structure of the part of the device that is destroyed by ions or other particles, and it is important for us both that the material is not destroyed and what products of its destruction end up in the device. This is the situation of the Tokamak - a reactor for producing controlled thermonuclear fusion, and there this problem is so important that it received its own name: “The problem of the first wall.” The fact is that impurities of heavy ions in the plasma are very harmful to its operation. This immediately reduces the list of suitable elements to C, B, Be, Al, Ti, V, their alloys and compounds. After a lot of research, beryllium was chosen for the Tokamak international project being built in France. This story is so important and interesting that it is worth telling about it in a little more detail. The requirements placed on the material of the first wall of the Tokamak opened a new page in materials science. The entries on this page have just begun. Therefore, for now we will limit ourselves to a list of the main processes occurring under such influences. Firstly, sputtering with ion streams. Secondly, sputtering under the influence of neutron fluxes. Thirdly, during neutron bombardment, “pieces” of approximately $10 мкм^{3}$ each fly out from the wall surface - the so-called “changs”. Fourth, helium and hydrogen atoms embedded in the material gather into bubbles, and as a result, the material “swells” - this process is called “swelling”. None of these processes can be said to be sufficiently studied or understood. Let's consider the most studied of them - ion sputtering. Spray coefficient, i.e. the ratio of the number of sputtered atoms to the number of ions hitting the surface increases with increasing ion mass and with decreasing evaporation energy (the energy required to evaporate an atom) of the target material. As the energy of the incident ions increases to values ​​of the order of 10 keV, the sputtering coefficient first increases and then begins to decrease. However, there is no strict theory of cathode sputtering that could reliably predict experimental data before they are obtained. However, this situation is not new for surface physics. Numerous existing theories of cathode sputtering can be divided into two groups: thermal and mechanical. Thermal ones explain sputtering by heating the surface at the site of ion impact and evaporation; mechanical - by the collision of an ion with target atoms, as a result of which the target atoms (colliding with each other) receive an impulse directed towards the target surface and fly into the vacuum. Mechanical theories differ from one another in their hypotheses about the specific mechanism of interaction of an ion with atoms and give different results that are consistent with certain experimental data.
