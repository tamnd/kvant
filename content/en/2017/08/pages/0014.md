The question of the strength of the applied or modified layer also arises in this case - it can peel off due to thermal stresses arising from temperature changes, due to vibrations and shocks. The combination of two materials is, let’s say, somewhat exotic, but it is also possible: imagine that you spray two different substances at the same time, and the resulting particles immediately stick together and form a single composite material. If we talk about connecting two parts, then we will consider only non-separable, or more precisely, connections not intended for disassembly. Bolts, screws, nuts, etc. - on the side. What about the rivets? They are also “inseparable”, although they are often disassembled by inquisitive young “unnaturalists”. It is probably necessary to highlight situations when significant chemical interaction (intermolecular bonds) or only physical interaction, such as friction, occurs between materials. But, according to modern theories, friction is also due to chemical interaction (the “welding-cut” mechanism). In general, an attempt to give an exact definition leads into such a forest, into such a thicket... So let's pretend that we forgot about the rivets and move on. Situations of permanent connection of two parts are usually divided into cases when no third substances are introduced into the connection zone, except for the two being connected, and when they are introduced. The first case is welding, the second is soldering, if there is heating until this third substance melts, and gluing, when there is no heating. This classification, like probably all engineering classifications, is not rigid; The role of the discoverer (who first invented, named and introduced) and traditions are also important. For example, there is “thermocompression welding” - parts are heated and strongly compressed, but do not melt. And there is “submerged arc” welding, when a substance is introduced, but then does not become part of the part, as with soldering, but only

In general, welding and soldering technology has been improved enough that the problems of strength itself have been solved. In other words, in many cases the seam material is no less strong than the weaker of the materials being joined, and in any case meets the strength requirements. The situation is more complicated with vibration resistance and the possibility of further processing. The transition layers that form during welding sometimes consist of “intermetallic compounds” - metal compounds that are brittle. Well, in this case, soldering is used. Today, welding and soldering technology has reached a state where most materials can be joined one way or another and the strength of the connection is not inferior to the materials being joined themselves. But there are a few particularly difficult cases. One of them is a combination of polyethylene and fluoroplastic. And this is no coincidence: the materials with the lowest friction are the materials with the lowest adhesion. Only after complex surface treatment (“activation”) is it possible to obtain a bonding strength of 14 MPa - this is only half that of the fluoroplastic itself. Another difficult case is adhesives for underwater bonding. Actually, they exist, but they provide a rather modest bonding strength of 10 MPa. Cuttlefish and mussels created such glue a long time ago - they have special cells for this, and not only them. And biologists are trying to create underwater glue based on mussel glue. Most likely, they will succeed. It would be interesting to try it on land and on polyethylene with fluoroplastic. What if the mussel can cope with this?
