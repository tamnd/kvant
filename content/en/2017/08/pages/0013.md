resisting attempts to separate one material from another is called adhesion. It determines capillarity, wettability, surface tension, and is essential for friction, gluing, soldering, welding, and coating. The adhesion of the matrix and filler of composites affects their strength. After all that has been said, it should not surprise you that the adhesion theory is not doing very well. The difficulties stem from the fact that this is a surface effect, and from the fact that if we combine two different materials, we have a “bonus track” - a doubled number of parameters and a multiplied number of combinations of their values. There are at least four adhesion mechanisms. First of all, a molecular interaction occurs between the material and the adhesive, which in some cases turns into a chemical reaction. The electrical theory of adhesion is based on the formation of an electrical double layer when two bodies come into contact. In this case, the adhesion strength is influenced by the presence of polar groups in the molecules of the adhesive and the object being bonded, and adhesion is determined by the attraction of charges. The diffusion mechanism is the process of diffusion of the adhesive into the material, as a result of which the sharp boundary between the bonded surface and the adhesive disappears, and the transition layer is quite strong. And finally, the mechanical adhesion of the glue and the material - the penetration of liquid glue into the pores of the materials being glued and the formation (after the glue hardens) of bridges, “rivets”. This process occurs only if at least one of the materials being glued is porous. In many cases, what is needed from a connection is not just strength, but strength under specific operating conditions - for example, vibration or impact resistance. They say “vacuum density”, and there the requirements are usually stricter - unless you are developing bacteriological or toxin weapons. In vacuum technology, the vapor pressure of the material of the formed compound is important - it should not be too high. This requirement limits, for example, the choice of solders - low-melting solders often produce a seam whose material has a high vapor pressure. Sometimes the electrical properties of the connection are important - it must have low resistance, like the metals being connected. A special problem is if we want to connect two superconductors by soldering. Or vice versa, high resistance if we connect dielectrics and want to maintain dielectric properties. In some cases, the ability to survive further technological processes is important - for example, if materials are combined and then processed in some way. There are often some significant restrictions on the technological process itself - when the parts being joined, say, cannot be heated too much or when the joining process itself must take place under water (and there is nothing to be surprised about, there is both underwater welding and underwater gluing). When joining fragile materials, e.g. glass and ceramics, the coefficients of thermal expansion must be matched with metals, otherwise upon cooling (from the temperature at which the connection occurs), the junction will collapse on its own - there is no need to apply external forces. It is not easy to classify situations of joining two materials or parts - many methods smoothly transition into one another, and some overlap, giving rise to hybrids. Probably, we should be the first to recognize the division of situations into such connections: a part and a material, two materials and, finally, two parts.
