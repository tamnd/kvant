This is essential both for the operation of the computer and for the operation of your dear body (and by the way, where exactly?)... A very simplified picture of friction looks like this - materials touch, contact along small areas of “true contact”, manage to form bridges of a single material, which are immediately cut off during movement. The second mechanism of friction is that the harder material cuts into the less hard material with its microprotrusions and begins to plow furrows in it as it moves. If we want to reduce friction, we must first apply a liquid lubricant - it will partially or completely separate the surfaces. And if there is no such lubrication, then to reduce friction it is necessary to use hard materials (so that they “crash” less and have a smaller area of ​​true contact) and dissimilar ones (so that the “single material” is less formed or less durable). This is partly confirmed. Thus, the coefficient of friction for hard substances in the diamond/diamond pair is 0.1, in the sapphire/sapphire $( Al_{2} O_{3} )$ and tungsten carbide/tungsten carbide (WC) pairs it is 0.2, and for soft substances in the aluminum/aluminum (Al) or silver/silver (Ag) pair it is 1.4. But this is in air, when there is an oxidized film on the surface that has absorbed moisture and everything else in the world. If the surfaces are cleaned and placed in a vacuum, without allowing them to become dirty, then for diamond we will get a coefficient of not 0.1, but 0.4, for sapphire – 0.6. The nickel (Ni)/tungsten (W) pair has a friction coefficient of 0.3 in air and 0.6 in a vacuum. Nickel/nickel (Ni) and copper/copper (Cu) pairs have a coefficient of 0.5 in air, and 4.8 in a vacuum! I have not seen higher numbers. However, humanity is mainly interested in reducing friction. The record holders are usually called fluoroplastic-4, also known as Teflon; paired with Teflon or metals, their coefficient of friction is 0.03–0.04. Graphite and molybdenum disulfide provide low friction because they disintegrate into microflakes, i.e. on a plane composed of hexagons of carbon atoms. These planes are weakly connected to each other and slide easily over each other. True, graphite ceases to be a good lubricant in a vacuum; its coefficient of friction increases catastrophically - up to 0.8. The reason for this is this: to be a good lubricant, graphite flakes must adhere well to both rubbing surfaces, so that it is actually flakes rubbing against flakes, and not flakes against metal. Graphite adheres well to metal due to the dipole moment that occurs if it is oxidized at the edges, i.e. if the planes are framed by oxygen. In air this is true, but in a vacuum and after heating, oxygen flies away. Disulfide flakes have a dipole moment (Mo–S bond) anyway; they do not need oxygen for this. However, fluorinated graphite, i.e. with a “frame” of fluorine (F), also operational in a vacuum. Sometimes ice is cited as an example of low friction - when a skate slides, the friction coefficient is 0.015. The explanation usually cited is the decrease in the melting temperature of ice with increasing pressure. The melting point of ice (as well as several other substances) does decrease with increasing pressure, but according to some estimates, this decrease is not enough to cause melting. The surface layer of ice itself has properties that differ from the properties of the volume, and these differences depend on temperature. In an ice/ice pair, the friction coefficient is about 0.5 at low temperatures (about $- 50 \infty C )$ and 0.05–0.1 at high temperatures (above $- 20 \infty C ).$).
