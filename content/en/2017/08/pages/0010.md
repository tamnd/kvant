Because, seriously speaking, the surface of any material has significantly more parameters than the volume. Although the reason for this is not entirely honest... It would seem that the surface may have fewer parameters than the volume. For example, let's take density or specific gravity - volume has this parameter, but the surface does not. The distance between atoms is something both he and she have. Dislocations - he has them, and in a certain sense (deviations of the structure from the ideal) she has, but it is not a fact that she has more of them, rather the opposite. So it seems that the winner is not obvious. But the trouble is that the surface is a kind of ideal, geometric concept, and from the point of view of physics, the surface is also a volume! Because near-surface layers take part in all surface processes. For example, a certain reaction occurs on the surface, but the material for it is supplied by diffusion from the volume. And moreover, as a result of these very superficial reactions and processes (for example, evaporation), the composition of the material near the surface changes, and a near-surface layer is formed, which is always tacitly classified as the “surface”. Therefore, do not be surprised by the words of Wolfgang Pauli, who said: “The surface was invented by the devil.” But of course, it is necessary to study and investigate the surface and the processes on it in general, and friction, wear and adhesion in particular. Because in the world, more than a third of the energy is spent on overcoming friction forces, and a quarter of the metal is spent on restoring losses associated with the wear of parts and assemblies in machines. The most advanced internal combustion engines spend from 12 to 20% of hydrocarbon fuel to overcome their own internal mechanical losses, plus at least 10% is lost in the transmission, totaling up to 30% of the fuel is spent to overcome friction.
