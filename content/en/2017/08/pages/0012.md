If there's anything murkier and more confusing than friction, it's its brother, wear. More than ten wear mechanisms are considered in the literature, and all of them depend on materials and friction conditions (speed, pressure, temperature, lubrication). In general and in general, we can only say that wear is usually less if there is less friction, because the less force, the less destruction. In addition, wear decreases with increasing strength - all other things being equal, a strong material breaks down more slowly. For example, there are successful attempts to create wear-resistant coatings based on TiC, WC, and SiC carbides. The expression “wearless friction” is found in the literature. In advertising publications, it means the desire to attract a buyer. In those cases when there is something behind it, it can mean the following. The first option is to create structures on the surface (such as porosity or dimples) that hold the lubricant, truly reducing friction and, to some extent, wear. The second option, more interesting, is when the components of the material (for example, an alloy) separate and one, more durable, component forms a porous structure, and the second, softer one, fills the pores or holes, beginning to play the role of a lubricant. Finally, the third option is the formation of a soft material on the surface (from the source material and lubricating layer components), which itself plays the role of a lubricant, reduces friction and, to some extent, wear. Adhesion and Adhesives

If we look around us, we will see that almost all things are made of several contact materials. The only obvious exception is a piece of paper, and then only before something is written on it. Moreover, the number of different materials in one thing can be measured in tens or hundreds. Technology has constantly developed along the path of creating new materials and combining them in products, and it is unlikely that it will refuse this opportunity. Therefore, enthusiastic words about the new era of 3D printers, that tomorrow we will make a computer, tomorrow we will make an engine, tomorrow this, tomorrow that ... - all this can only be written by journalists who do not understand what is made of what they use and on what they write. Those who believe that the world is structured like sand, a sandbox, molds and Easter cakes. Technology consists of a great variety of materials. Many of them are “just in contact”, i.e. they should neither interact nor destroy each other. But many must hold tightly to each other: paints on paper, ski bindings on skis, insulation on wires, seals on the refrigerator door... with food.
