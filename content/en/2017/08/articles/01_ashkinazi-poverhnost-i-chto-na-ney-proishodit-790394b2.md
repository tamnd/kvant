Show firmness

For many practical applications, what we call hardness matters. It seems that everyone understands what it is. For example, strength of character: “I don’t study and won’t study!” There are several methods for measuring hardness in technology, and all of them in one way or another imitate operating conditions - the introduction into a material of something made from something else, capable of being introduced into it, i.e. harder material. As an object to be embedded, it is called an indenter; either a steel ball (which, as you understand, cannot be embedded in everything) or a diamond pyramid is chosen. In different cases, the balls, pyramids and applied forces are different, but the criterion of hardness is always the size of the remaining imprint. In mineralogy, they use another method for measuring hardness - they scratch one material with another. Please note that if you try to connect hardness with the basic parameters characterizing a solid body, it turns out that it is ambiguously related to them. For ductile materials, hardness is based on the yield strength, and for brittle materials, it is based on the tensile strength. It is obviously impossible to say which material is the softest - all these balls and pyramids will simply fall into the soft. Of the metals, the softest is probably cesium Cs, especially in hot weather (it melts at $28,4 \infty C ).$ The hardest today is diamond, followed by boron nitride BN (one of the modifications). Different faces of a diamond single crystal have different hardness, but BN is inferior, although slightly, to even the less hard one. According to the publications of one group of authors, a material harder than diamond was obtained - fullerite, a material based on C60 fullerene molecules. However, this is still an obscure question... As for practical applications, for example, as an abrasive, other properties are also important: thermal conductivity (the processing zone heats up); chemical resistance, especially in the conditions that are created in the cutting zone; so, not only the indicated champions are used.

A very simplified picture of friction looks like this - materials touch, contact along small areas of “true contact”, manage to form bridges of a single material, which are immediately cut off during movement. The second mechanism of friction is that the harder material cuts into the less hard material with its microprotrusions and begins to plow furrows in it as it moves. If we want to reduce friction, we must first apply a liquid lubricant - it will partially or completely separate the surfaces. And if there is no such lubrication, then to reduce friction it is necessary to use hard materials (so that they “crash” less and have a smaller area of ​​true contact) and dissimilar ones (so that the “single material” is less formed or less durable). This is partly confirmed. Thus, the coefficient of friction for hard substances in the diamond/diamond pair is 0.1, in the sapphire/sapphire $( Al_{2} O_{3} )$ and tungsten carbide/tungsten carbide (WC) pairs it is 0.2, and for soft substances in the aluminum/aluminum (Al) or silver/silver (Ag) pair it is 1.4. But this is in air, when there is an oxidized film on the surface that has absorbed moisture and everything else in the world. If the surfaces are cleaned and placed in a vacuum, without allowing them to become dirty, then for diamond we will get a coefficient of not 0.1, but 0.4, for sapphire – 0.6. The nickel (Ni)/tungsten (W) pair has a friction coefficient of 0.3 in air and 0.6 in a vacuum. Nickel/nickel (Ni) and copper/copper (Cu) pairs have a coefficient of 0.5 in air, and 4.8 in a vacuum! I have not seen higher numbers. However, humanity is mainly interested in reducing friction. Graphite and molybdenum disulfide provide low friction because they disintegrate into microflakes, i.e. on a plane composed of hexagons of carbon atoms. True, graphite ceases to be a good lubricant in a vacuum; its coefficient of friction increases catastrophically - up to 0.8. The reason for this is this: to be a good lubricant, graphite flakes must adhere well to both rubbing surfaces, so that it is actually flakes rubbing against flakes, and not flakes against metal. Graphite adheres well to metal due to the dipole moment that occurs if it is oxidized at the edges, i.e. if the planes are framed by oxygen. In air this is true, but in a vacuum and after heating, oxygen flies away. Disulfide flakes have a dipole moment (Mo–S bond) anyway; they do not need oxygen for this. On the other hand, wear and tear turns out to be fundamentally important - because wear products enter the body, which does not know what to do with them. If there's anything murkier and more confusing than friction, it's its brother, wear. More than ten wear mechanisms are considered in the literature, and all of them depend on materials and friction conditions (speed, pressure, temperature, lubrication). In general and in general, we can only say that wear is usually less if there is less friction, because the less force, the less destruction. In addition, wear decreases with increasing strength - all other things being equal, a strong material breaks down more slowly. For example, there are successful attempts to create wear-resistant coatings based on TiC, WC, and SiC carbides. The expression “wearless friction” is found in the literature. In advertising publications, it means the desire to attract a buyer. In those cases when there is something behind it, it can mean the following. The first option is to create structures on the surface (such as porosity or dimples) that hold the lubricant, truly reducing friction and, to some extent, wear. The second option, more interesting, is when the components of the material (for example, an alloy) separate and one, more durable, component forms a porous structure, and the second, softer one, fills the pores or holes, beginning to play the role of a lubricant. Finally, the third option is the formation of a soft material on the surface (from the source material and lubricating layer components), which itself plays the role of a lubricant, reduces friction and, to some extent, wear. Adhesion and Adhesives

If we look around us, we will see that almost all things are made of several contact materials. The only obvious exception is a piece of paper, and then only before something is written on it. Moreover, the number of different materials in one thing can be measured in tens or hundreds. Technology has constantly developed along the path of creating new materials and combining them in products, and it is unlikely that it will refuse this opportunity. Therefore, enthusiastic words about the new era of 3D printers, that tomorrow we will make a computer, tomorrow we will make an engine, tomorrow this, tomorrow that ... - all this can only be written by journalists who do not understand what is made of what they use and on what they write. Those who believe that the world is structured like sand, a sandbox, molds and Easter cakes. Technology consists of a great variety of materials. Many of them are “just in contact”, i.e. they should neither interact nor destroy each other. But many must hold tightly to each other: paints on paper, ski bindings on skis, insulation on wires, seals on the refrigerator door... with food. resisting attempts to separate one material from another is called adhesion. It determines capillarity, wettability, surface tension, and is essential for friction, gluing, soldering, welding, and coating. The adhesion of the matrix and filler of composites affects their strength. After all that has been said, it should not surprise you that the adhesion theory is not doing very well. The difficulties stem from the fact that this is a surface effect, and from the fact that if we combine two different materials, we have a “bonus track” - a doubled number of parameters and a multiplied number of combinations of their values. There are at least four adhesion mechanisms. First of all, a molecular interaction occurs between the material and the adhesive, which in some cases turns into a chemical reaction. The electrical theory of adhesion is based on the formation of an electrical double layer when two bodies come into contact. In this case, the adhesion strength is influenced by the presence of polar groups in the molecules of the adhesive and the object being bonded, and adhesion is determined by the attraction of charges. The diffusion mechanism is the process of diffusion of the adhesive into the material, as a result of which the sharp boundary between the bonded surface and the adhesive disappears, and the transition layer is quite strong. And finally, the mechanical adhesion of the glue and the material - the penetration of liquid glue into the pores of the materials being glued and the formation (after the glue hardens) of bridges, “rivets”. This process occurs only if at least one of the materials being glued is porous.
