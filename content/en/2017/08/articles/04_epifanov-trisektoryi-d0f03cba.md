Trisectors

In the problem of trisection of an angle, you need to use a compass and a ruler to divide a given angle into three equal parts (or, which is equivalent, to construct an angle equal to a third of a given angle). As in all classical construction problems, the compass and ruler are assumed to be ideal: with the help of a compass you can construct circles of any radius (and lay equal segments on a straight line), and with the help of a ruler (one-sided and without divisions) you can draw straight lines. This problem, along with two other well-known construction problems—doubling the cube and squaring the circle—has defied mathematicians since ancient times. Only in the 19th century was it proven that none of these three problems is solvable in the general case. However, some specific angles can be divided into three parts. For example, it is not difficult to do this with a right angle.

Exercise 1. Give an example of an angle whose degree measure is not a multiple of $15\infty$ and which can be divided into three equal parts using a compass and ruler.

The proof of the impossibility of trisection of an arbitrary angle was given in 1837 by the French mathematician Pierre Laurent Wantzel. Oddly enough, the proof is not geometric at all, but algebraic.

To greatly simplify it, the idea is as follows. First, let's translate everything from geometric language into algebraic: any construction with a compass and straightedge reduces to a sequential solution of systems consisting of linear and quadratic equations (since a straight line is given by an equation of the first degree, and a circle by an equation of the second degree). Secondly, note that constructing an angle is equivalent to constructing two segments, the ratio of the lengths of which is equal to the cosine of this angle. From this, using the cosine formula for a triple angle, we can obtain that angle A can be trisectioned if the equation $x^{3} - 3 x - - 2$ cos A = 0 is solvable in square radicals, and this is not always the case.

Nevsis

Nevsis is a method of geometric construction, the purpose of which is to inscribe a given segment between two given curves so that the segment or its continuation passes through a given point. To do this, use a ruler on which you can make notes. This ruler with a segment marked on its edge is positioned so that one of its ends falls on one of the given curves, and the edge of the ruler passes through this point.
