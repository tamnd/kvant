As for the directionality, it will not be enough not only to create a narrow shadow with clear ends, but generally for any structured image. In conclusion, I will express one “positive” idea - how, in my opinion, could still explain the observed phenomenon. I myself think the most likely is this simple hypothesis: the observed “shadows” are precisely shadows from snowfall. The phrase “slanting rain” has become established in the Russian language (for example, in Mayakovsky: “I’ll walk through my native country like slanting rain”), but the concepts of “slanting snow” or “slanting snowfall” are probably not found. However, it seems to me that this could be a trace from just such a snowfall. How could this hypothesis be confirmed or refuted? Here you can move from two directions, “on opposite paths.” You can try to prove that such a movement of small particles - snowflakes - in the air (i.e., rectilinear, or at least lying in the same vertical plane) is impossible under any conditions. Or, on the contrary, you can try to demonstrate such a movement with at least one example. In this case, the task will split into two parts. The first part, let’s call it “physical”, is to show that under a certain set of physical conditions (wind speed, particle size, temperature, etc.) such movement is possible. And an equally important second part, the “meteorological” one, is to show that such physical parameters can, in principle, be realized in nature during a snowfall. Of course, these paths are not replacing, but complementing each other, and which of them will be successful depends not on us, but on the actual nature of the phenomenon. However, the first of these two paths requires going far beyond the boundaries of “school” physics, while the second path, it seems to me,

Therefore, for those who suddenly find this interesting, I will give some advice on how such work could be carried out and what problems will have to be solved. First of all, we must understand that in real conditions in a home laboratory we will most likely have to work not exactly with the physical system that interests us, but with some model of it, i.e. with something that would be most similar to it in its main parameters. So, you are unlikely to be able to get a snow cannon. But even if you become its happy owner, you are unlikely to be able to vary the wind speed and particle size it creates within wide enough limits. This means you will have to work with some other particles suspended in the air. At home, the easiest way to experiment is with water droplets - their streams can be obtained using a humidifier, a spray bottle, and, in extreme cases, a boiling kettle. An atomizer will produce larger droplets than a humidifier, and differently configured atomizers will produce different droplet sizes, i.e. within certain limits it will be possible to select the necessary one. The “modeling” of the experiment will consist not only in a different particle material, but also in the geometric dimensions of the system: the size of the bushes, trees and their shadows shown in the photographs clearly exceeds the size, if not of an apartment, then of a workplace acceptable for the experiment. You will have to work with smaller models and you will need to choose the right size. It will not be possible to get clear shadows from too small figures using relatively large drops. And large figures will require the uniformity of the flow you create over a larger area and greater distance. So you will need to find a compromise size.
