The first three hypotheses suggest mechanisms that involve one common element: uneven heating of the snow surface. It is this heating that we will try to evaluate. To do this, we will need the following data, which are easy to find in physical reference books: the intensity of solar radiation near the Earth's surface without taking into account attenuation by the atmosphere (the so-called solar constant), the degree of attenuation of radiation in the atmosphere, the declination of the sun, the albedo of snow (the proportion of light reflected by it), as well as some heat transfer characteristics for snow, which we will discuss below. The highest altitude, and therefore the maximum value of E, is observed at noon, when $h = 90 ^\circ - \phi + \delta$, where $\phi$ is the latitude of the place, $\delta$ is the declination of the sun. The latitude of Moscow is $\phi = 56 ^\circ$, the declination of the sun at the moment of observation is $\delta \approx - 8 ^\circ$ [1], i.e. $h \approx 26 ^\circ$, and at this height $\alpha_{атм} \approx 0,5$ [2]. Thus, $E \approx \approx 300$ W $м^{2}$. Taking the albedo of snow equal to 0.9 [3], we find that $1 м^{2}$ absorbs about 30 W of solar energy in 1 s. This energy can lead to heating of the snow cover, which is partly compensated by its outflow from the surface layer of snow due to heat exchange with the lower layers and with the air.
