THIS MATERIAL IS A SMALL ADDITION to the recently published article “Let's save the diver” (see «Kvant» No. 2 for 2016). Recall that the Cartesian diver was first described by Raffaello Maggiotti in 1648 in his treatise on the incompressibility of water. In the modern version, the Cartesian diver is a glass vial, partly filled with water, partly with air, and floating upside down in a hermetically sealed plastic bottle. If you squeeze the bottle, the diver sinks to the bottom, and if you release the bottle, he rises. Maggiotti used a diver to use

However, glassblowers have long blown it in the form of a funny little devil (Fig.2). Look - the devil has a curled tail with a hole at the end. Due to this, the devil not only jumps up and down, but also spins around its axis, like a real dancer. That's what the French call it - ludion. When the bottle is compressed, a portion of water flows inside the devil due to excess pressure, and when the bottle is released, water flows out of it through the hole in the tail and twists the devil. This is exactly how the Segner wheel works - a small water turbine often used for watering lawns (Fig.3). Water under pressure is pumped into the S-shaped tube, flows out of it and causes the tube to rotate at high speed.
