Is it so familiar to you?

?

bionics

This term is absent from the statements given in the epigraph; it was born only a little more than half a century ago, when a new scientific direction was identified. Receiving the name “bionics”, it combined the approaches of many disciplines, not least physics. The goal was to understand the structure and functioning of living organisms in order to identify the best opportunities for creating man-made systems, mechanisms and structures. After all, nature, in the course of billions of years of evolution, has “learned” to solve many problems more reliably and economically than scientists and engineers can. This includes the accumulation and processing of information; and communication and navigation tasks; and the transformation of some types of energy into others... However, the thoughts of professional researchers were preceded by countless attempts by our distant ancestors to spy on and borrow from nature something convenient, practical and useful. Let's add to their inquisitiveness and perseverance our knowledge, primarily in physics, in order to cope with today's unusual tasks. After all, they will require you to be observant, to draw analogies, to look for similarities between the natural and the artificial.

Questions and tasks

1. The egg can withstand the weight of the brood hen, but is easily destroyed under the blows of the weak chick’s beak from the inside. Why? 2. How can the appearance of the inhabitants of the animal world help the designers of ships, aircraft, and cars? 3. Cyclists in team racing stretch out one after another in a chain, and fighters in a group flight line up in a wedge. For what? 4. How do the feet of waterfowl move? Who borrows this method of transportation? 5. Kangaroos start moving from a place, bending low to the ground, which helps them reach high speed faster. Who could benefit from such an observation? 6. For a long time, aircraft manufacturers could not find a way to combat flutter—harmful vibrations of an airplane wing in flight—until they figured out how to add weights to the end of the wing. How does nature solve the problem of damping such vibrations? 7. The squid makes throws, sucking water inside the cavity in its body and “shooting” it through a narrow funnel, and it can turn, changing the direction of its movement. This method is very economical and allows the animal to achieve

Microexperience

Surely, while walking in nature, burdock thorns stuck to your clothes or shoes. Try to find examples in your wardrobe of using these plant features.

It's interesting that...

... eggs of birds and amphibians, nut shells, shells of turtles and crabs, shells of mollusks are examples of strong and light natural shells, studied since ancient times by builders and architects and used by them, for example, in the construction of arched vaults. ... Leonardo da Vinci devoted almost a quarter of a century to researching the flight of birds in the hope of creating a flying machine for humans, for which many contemporaries considered him a magician and even crazy. ...car designers initially sought a purely external resemblance of their cars to fast-moving inhabitants of the deep sea - stingrays and sharks. But later, the engineers of the Mercedes concern, together with biologists, managed to create a truly bionic car (Bionic Car), based on the structural features of the box fish, namely, to make it more streamlined, 40% stiffer and a third lighter than previous models of similar sizes. ... the ability of gecko lizards to climb smooth walls and even run on ceilings haunts robot designers. By studying the structure of gecko feet, we have already been able to understand the interaction of the microhairs covering them with surfaces - this is the electrostatic attraction of molecules. Using artificial nanofibers, Canadian engineers have created a robotic wedge that crawls up a wall, albeit at a low speed. ...insects, attracted by the brightly colored pitcher leaves of the predator plant Nepenthes, despite any tricks, escape from them. This natural structure prompted materials scientists to create surfaces that repel a wide variety of substances. ... as Japanese scientists found out, representatives of one of the types of leeches can withstand temperatures of minus 196 degrees Celsius for 24 hours, and endured minus 90 for as long as 32 months. Solving this phenomenon will help preserve human cells or organs for transplantation. ...nature provides examples of efficiency so far unattainable by many of our devices and machines. For example, during photosynthesis the efficiency approaches 75%, bacteria convert up to 90% of chemical reactions into light, and in fireflies it reaches 100%!
