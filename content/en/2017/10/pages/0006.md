For brevity, we call movement along the equidistant family sliding. As we know, sliding along a line h is a composition of symmetries about two lines (a and b) perpendicular to h. We will use the concept of sliding in two other geometries. In the Euclidean case, sliding is a parallel translation along the straight line h (Fig.25);

So, on the Lobachevsky plane, the composition of two symmetries is either a rotation, a shift along a family of horocycles, or a sliding. In the Euclidean case, only rotation and sliding remain, and in the spherical case, these two cases coincide and only rotation remains. Note that in each of the three cases, motion can be represented as a composition of two symmetries in more than one way. Indeed, the rotation $R_{A}^{J}$ is equal to $S_{a} o S_{b}$ for any two straight lines a and b intersecting at point A at an angle J 2 (recall that the angle is considered with a sign: it is important in what order its sides are taken). Consequently, as a straight line a we can take an arbitrary straight line passing through the point A. The second straight line is determined uniquely. The same thing happens in cases (2) and (3). For (2), passing to a special case using conjugation, we can take any special line as a, then b will be a special line at a given distance from a. For (3), as a we can take any line perpendicular to h. For uniformity, we call the set from which we can choose a line a a pencil of lines. In case (1) the pencil consists of lines passing through a given point; in case (2) – from parallel lines; finally, in case (3) – from lines perpendicular to a given line h. For the Euclidean plane, cases (2) and (3) coincide: a bundle of parallel lines is also a bundle of lines perpendicular to the given one. On a sphere, only case (1) is possible. Thus, the following statement is true in all three geometries. Lemma on replacement of symmetries: The composition $S_{a} o S_{b}$ can also be written in the form $S_{a} 1 o S_{b} 1$, where one of the lines $a_{1}$ and $b_{1}$ is chosen arbitrarily from the pencil containing the lines a and b (the other line is uniquely determined). This lemma will be useful to us for calculating the composition of three symmetries, $S_{a} o S_{b} o S_{c}$.
