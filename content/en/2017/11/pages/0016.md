Stayers and marathon runners. The role of fat oxidation

The total energy reserve in the adipose tissue of a 75 kg athlete is more than 300 MJ, and it is very far from being depleted. But it takes a lot of time to significantly deplete your fat reserves. Anyone who is trying to lose weight can confirm this. During the stayer's distance, the power consumed is 1–1.5 kW, and the energy consumption is 4–5 MW for each hour of running. Record holders consume about 5000 kcal of energy (ª 20 MJ) per day. If we assume that half of this energy can be expended during running, then only 10 MJ of energy is available. As can be seen from our estimates, such energy is spent in about two hours of fast running. At figure 1 this corresponds to the duration of a marathon race. Indeed, marathon runners, not to mention longer distance runners, are forced to replenish their energy reserves during the run with mixtures rich in carbohydrates. Do marathon runners have to drink water? Certainly. Since it takes 2.3 MJ to evaporate a liter of water, an hour's run corresponds to the evaporation of about 2 liters of water. But is it necessary to completely replace water losses? No, it's dangerous. Water may not have time to be absorbed by the stayer’s body during the run, and the task of replenishing losses is solved after the finish. How do human running champions compare to the speed of movement of other mammals? Let's face it - very mediocre. In many ways, this is the price for upright walking and the associated running on two legs. Quadrupeds run faster. The champion among them is the cheetah, which has a recorded maximum running speed of 35 m/s for 10 seconds. Most likely, he doesn’t need more for a successful hunt. The rest of the time he rests or moves at a significantly lower and more economical “cruising” speed.
