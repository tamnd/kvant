The movement of cars and living creatures - and the theorem about kinetic energy

A. RYBAKOV

LET'S START WITH A QUOTE FROM ONE REMARKABLE book written more than a century ago: “When a system consists of a large number of parts, a complete study of its motion can turn out to be very difficult and even impossible. In such cases, it is useful, before a detailed study of the movement of parts of the system, to obtain some understanding of the general movement of the entire system as a whole.” This is exactly what the conversation will be about - about the fundamental aspects of the dynamics of some systems that we see around us. In many textbooks - from a basic physics textbook for grade 9 to the most serious courses in theoretical mechanics - there is a theorem about kinetic energy. And the formulations of this theorem in different textbooks are almost the same. Here's how it is formulated: the change in the kinetic energy of a body is equal to the work done by all the forces acting on it, or

Some authors, instead of a body in the condition of the theorem, talk about a material point. But the usual mode of car traffic is not like that...

What force accelerates the car?

In some old mechanics textbook, I saw the following title for one of the sections: “Self-propelled vehicles.” Wonderful title! And we will primarily talk about self-propelled carriages on wheels (or on tracks) with the principle of movement traditional for such devices. The last remark excludes from our consideration such specific devices as snowmobiles, sailboats or rocket-powered cars (specially designed for setting speed records). Let's discuss some fundamental points of the mechanics of self-propelled vehicles, i.e. those moments that are not related to the specific design of the engine, transmission and other things dear to the heart of every motorist. For convenience, we will talk about a car, but everything said will be equally applicable to a diesel locomotive, a tank, a bicycle, and even a children’s wind-up little car. The answer to this question of some kind of “traction force”, we note, does not explain anything. It is necessary to specifically indicate the interacting bodies and describe the properties of the force. For our purposes, it is better to pose two different questions. One may ask: what force causes a change in the momentum of the car? And you can ask: where does the kinetic energy of a car come from? Oddly enough, below we will see that these are indeed different questions. We will talk about normal driving mode, when the wheels do not slip relative to the road. To answer the first question, it is necessary to indicate a specific external force (internal forces cannot change the movements of the center of mass of the system). And such power is easy to find. Indeed, a torque is transmitted to the drive wheel from the engine, under the influence of which the wheel tends to turn relative to the road, but the friction force arising between the wheel and the road prevents it from doing so. It is clear that the friction force applied to the wheel is directed opposite to possible slippage, i.e. forward, in the direction the car is moving. It is the action of this force that leads to an increase in the momentum of the car, one might say, it makes the car move.

Properties of this force

So, we are talking about the friction force between the drive wheels and the road. Motorists call it traction force. And it has amazing properties. However, we can encounter the properties of this force while solving ordinary educational problems and without thinking about the car. For example, here is a well-known problem that cannot be avoided when studying the dynamics of rotational motion - the problem of rolling a cylinder off an inclined plane without slipping. It is very useful to solve it in several different ways. Note that it is the friction force on the plane that spins the cylinder (see figure).
