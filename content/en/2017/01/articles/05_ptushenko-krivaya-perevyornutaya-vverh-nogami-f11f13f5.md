A curve turned upside down in the humor “Physicists continue to joke” contains one anecdote about the outstanding theoretical physicist Yakov Ilyich Frenkel: “They say about Ya.I. Frenkel that supposedly at the Physics and Technology Institute in the 30s, a certain experimenter caught him in the corridor and showed him the curve obtained experimentally. After thinking for a minute, Ya.I. gave an explanation of the course of this curve. However, it turned out that the curve was accidentally turned upside down. The curve was put in place, and, after thinking a little, Ya.I. explained this behavior of the curve as well.” No matter how funny it may seem, we actually come across such “anecdotes” at every step. No, not that “theorists can explain anything.” And phenomena that seem to contradict each other, which, however, require their own explanation. You don't have to go far. Imagine that you are skiing and come across a picture captured on figure 1. It seems that there is nothing surprising:

But then you walk a few more kilometers and suddenly you see something opposite. On figure 2 car tracks are clearly visible, but they are not melting - everything around is melting, but they are not! Let us immediately warn you that there is no prank here: these photos were actually taken on the same day, just a few kilometers from each other. Time of year, climate, weather conditions - everything is the same here. And the event also seems to be the same: a car drove through the snow. But the results are just the opposite. Well, let's remember Frenkel with respect and try to explain these phenomena? First of all, we admit that the phenomenon depicted in figure 1 is much more rare (in any case, if it happens in a forest or field, and not near some quarry), but it is easier to explain. Let us clarify that “to explain” means to name the physical phenomenon that underlies the picture we observe, to describe the conditions under which this phenomenon manifests itself, and to understand why exactly such conditions arose here. The third stage, however, often goes beyond the purely physical task. But – first things first. Let's try to go through all the stages. Let's look at the options on which, in principle, the rate of melting (melting) of a substance could depend: on its heat capacity, thermal conductivity, intensity of heat removal and its absorption from the outside... Stop, it seems that something plausible sounded in this list, namely, heat absorption. Where does snow get its heat from? From those objects or environments with which it interacts through direct (one might say “contact”) heat exchange or radiation: this is earth, air, water (in the form of rain or hollow water), the sun. The ground can actually play a significant role in melting snow in the fall. Many may have noticed how poorly early snow falls on the ground that has not yet cooled - even if there is a lot of it and if it is cold, it still melts below and clings to your skis if you decide to stand on them without waiting for the real, deep winter. But by the end of winter (and in our photographs it is the end of winter), the earth still has time to cool down - unless the sun warms it up where it is free of snow. Warm air can also play a role in melting snow - but its temperature does not change so much over several meters along the surface of the earth and, most importantly, is not able to maintain these differences for a long time (the air mixes quickly) to cause such uneven melting. Water - well, it could very well help. If the wheels cut a channel for water in the snow and the first streams of meltwater rushed through this snow, then they could accelerate the melting of the snow closest to the channel. Just in case, let's remember this version, although some of its shortcomings can already be noticed: no traces of water, much less powerful flows of water, are noticeable in the thawed ruts. A powerful stream would wash out the bottom, and it would look a little different. We forgot about the most important, most “physical” part: what kind of physical phenomenon is here? Why does compacted snow or ice melt more slowly? It would seem that everything is simple here: both in compacted snow and in ice there is less air, fewer voids, which makes it more difficult to melt it. Wait, but “emptiness”, air, is excellent thermal insulation! “A blanket of snow”, “snow protects the ground from freezing” - we have heard such phrases since childhood. Because of its porous structure, it is easier for loose snow than ice to “not let” the sun’s heat inside – its thermal conductivity is lower. Okay, but because of this porous structure, there is simply less frozen water in the snow that needs to be melted. Therefore, it requires less heat to melt. However... Try an experiment - melt blocks of loose and compressed snow in the sun, and you will see that even with the same masses, loose snow still melts faster. No, the effect is something else.
