Let's look at the options on which, in principle, the rate of melting (melting) of a substance could depend: on its heat capacity, thermal conductivity, intensity of heat removal and its absorption from the outside... Stop, it seems that something plausible sounded in this list, namely, heat absorption. Where does snow get its heat from? From those objects or environments with which it interacts through direct (one might say “contact”) heat exchange or radiation: this is earth, air, water (in the form of rain or hollow water), the sun. The ground can actually play a significant role in melting snow in the fall. Many may have noticed how poorly early snow falls on the ground that has not yet cooled - even if there is a lot of it and if it is cold, it still melts below and clings to your skis if you decide to stand on them without waiting for the real, deep winter. But by the end of winter (and in our photographs it is the end of winter), the earth still has time to cool down - unless the sun warms it up where it is free of snow. Warm air can also play a role in melting snow - but its temperature does not change so much over several meters along the surface of the earth and, most importantly, is not able to maintain these differences for a long time (the air mixes quickly) to cause such uneven melting. Water - well, it could very well help. If the wheels cut a channel for water in the snow and the first streams of meltwater rushed through this snow, then they could accelerate the melting of the snow closest to the channel.
