PHYSICS ELECTIVE

The movement of cars and living creatures - and the theorem about kinetic energy

A. RYBAKOV

LET'S START WITH A QUOTE FROM ONE REMARKABLE book written more than a century ago: “When a system consists of a large number of parts, a complete study of its motion can turn out to be very difficult and even impossible. In such cases, it is useful, before a detailed study of the movement of parts of the system, to obtain some understanding of the general movement of the entire system as a whole.” This is exactly what the conversation will be about - about the fundamental aspects of the dynamics of some systems that we see around us. In many textbooks - from a basic physics textbook for grade 9 to the most serious courses in theoretical mechanics - there is a theorem about kinetic energy. And the formulations of this theorem in different textbooks are almost the same. Here's how it is formulated: the change in the kinetic energy of a body is equal to the work done by all the forces acting on it, or

$E_{кин2} - E_{кин1} = A_{12}$.

Some authors, instead of a body in the condition of the theorem, talk about a material point. In the 9th grade, the proof of this theorem is carried out under the assumption that the force acting on the body is constant; in a university textbook, the corresponding integral will be written out for the work of force, but this is not the essence of the matter

Meanwhile, for the majority of bodies whose movement we observe, the theorem is obviously unfair! “The author subverts the laws of mechanics?!” - the reader will be horrified. No, of course not. The author only reminds that in life and, especially, in the exact sciences, usually “the devil hides in the details.” It is these details that are proposed to be discussed. What bodies should we choose to apply the theorem formulated above to them? Let's look out the window: there's a car driving, there's a man walking, there's a cat running, and here's another beetle crawling along the cornice. We will choose them. Let's start with the car. Only in one case - in the case of towing on a cable - does the application of this theorem to the movement of a car do not cause any difficulties. But the usual mode of car traffic is not like that...

What force accelerates the car?

In some old mechanics textbook, I saw the following title for one of the sections: “Self-propelled vehicles.” Wonderful title! And we will primarily talk about self-propelled carriages on wheels (or on tracks) with the principle of movement traditional for such devices. The last remark excludes from our consideration such specific devices as snowmobiles, sailboats or rocket-powered cars (specially designed for setting speed records). Let's discuss some fundamental points of the mechanics of self-propelled vehicles, i.e. those moments that are not related to the specific design of the engine, transmission and other things dear to the heart of every motorist. For convenience, we will talk about a car, but everything said will be equally applicable to a diesel locomotive, a tank, a bicycle, and even a children’s wind-up little car.
