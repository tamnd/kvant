Chall's theorem. Any movement of the Euclidean plane is either a rotation, or a parallel translation, or a sliding symmetry. This theorem, proven by the French geometer Michel Chales in the mid-19th century, is quite widely known and appears in many books on elementary geometry; there is an article about her in $«Кванте»^{4}$. In this article we will prove Chall's theorem and its analogues in three geometries: flat Euclidean, spherical and Lobachevsky geometry. The axiomatics of Lobachevsky geometry differs from Euclidean only in one axiom - the axiom of parallels. Euclid's axiom looks like this: Euclid's axiom. Through a given point A that does not lie on a given line a, it is possible to draw at most one straight line parallel to line a (i.e., not having common points with it). This axiom, in comparison with others, does not seem clearly obvious and rather looks like a theorem that should be proven. However, attempts to do this, which were made by many geometers from the time of Euclid until the 19th century, were unsuccessful. After all, Nikolai Ivanovich Lobachevsky in his work “On the Principles of Geometry” (1828) suggested that if you replace the Euclidean axiom about parallels with its negation, you will get a consistent theory, and thus it is impossible to prove this axiom as a theorem from the other axioms (the simultaneous presence of a statement and its negation makes the theory contradictory). Around the same time, Janos Bolyai and Carl Friedrich Gauss also came up with this idea. Based on the assumption that replacing the axiom of parallels with its negation does not lead to contradictions, Lobachevsky made significant progress in the development of a new geometric theory, which we now call Lobachevsky’s geometry - however, it was possible to strictly prove that contradictions in it are in principle impossible to obtain only half a century after Lobachevsky’s discovery. For this purpose, models of Lobachevsky geometry were built, one of which we present below. So, in Lobachevsky’s geometry, instead of the axiom of parallels, its negation is accepted. Lobachevsky's axiom. There exist a line a and a point A not lying on it such that through the point A it is possible to draw at least two lines that do not have common points with A. In the presence of other Euclidean axioms, Lobachevsky's axiom implies its stronger version: for any line a and a point A not lying on it, more than one line parallel to a can be drawn through A. All other axioms in Euclid and Lobachevsky coincide. To these two geometries - Euclid and Lobachevsky - we will also add spherical geometry. The points of this geometry are located on a sphere (in ordinary three-dimensional Euclidean space). The role of straight lines is played by great circles formed when a sphere is cut by planes passing through its center: the arc of a great circle is the shortest path between two points if movement is allowed only along the sphere. It may seem that one can reason about figures on a sphere using the methods of ordinary Euclidean stereometry, and it is unnecessary to single out spherical geometry separately. However, looking at the sphere as a kind of analogue of a plane is sometimes very useful, both for theoretical and practical $задач.^{5}$ Spherical geometry is indeed similar in many ways to the planimetry of Euclid and Lobachevsky (in particular, many of the theorems used in the proof of Chall’s theorem are true - see below), but it differs in many ways. For example, a triangle is possible on a sphere, all of which have right angles (Fig.5).
