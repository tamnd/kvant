If direct rays of the Sun fall on obstacles in the air, then in the shadow region the air scatters radiation previously scattered by other parts of the air (i.e., secondary scattering of rays occurs). The glow intensity of this part of the air decreases significantly. For example, if you look along the shadow area created by the contrail of an airplane flying in a straight line, then a dark beam (Fig.3) is visible in the air.

Parallel beams of light piercing the air where there are no obstacles cause the air to glow, and these luminous areas are clearly visible against the background of faintly glowing air in the shadow of obstacles (Fig.4). The intensity of light scattered by a certain amount of air (for example, one mole of particles) depends both on the wavelength of the light rays and on the angle between the direction of propagation of the direct rays of the Sun and the direction of propagation of the scattered light. The absorption regions of the molecules of which air is mainly composed are in the range of ultraviolet rays, so in the visible range the air is transparent. In air molecules located in the electromagnetic field of light rays, polarization of electric charges occurs, and in this case the electric dipole moment of the molecules is proportional to the electric field strength $E_{0}$ cos $\omega t$ of the light wave. Atoms and molecules are massive, and the electrons in the outer shells are light, so we can assume that the dipole moment is associated with the displacement of light electrons relative to the heavy remainder of the molecule.
