However, the general theory of relativity (GR) also predicts gravitational waves, which should arise when the shapes of massive stars oscillate. It is fundamentally important and practically tempting to learn how to register these waves. Indeed, in principle, for example, any student, moving quickly from the lecture hall to the cafeteria, changes the distribution of masses in the Universe. For almost a hundred years, humanity has been studying the possibility of registering on Earth gravitational waves coming to us from outer space. The detectors used by J. Weber (sixties of the last century) could “feel” the deformation of the receiving device with an accuracy of $10^{16}$ m - ten times less than the radius of the proton! In the experiments of V.B. And relatively recently, the tracking system recorded gravitational waves from a double black hole with a total mass of almost a hundred times the mass of our Sun, located 400 megaparsecs away from us (1 parsec = 3 $10^{16}$ m). Of course, both parts of it rotated around a common center of mass (for example, like the Earth and the Moon) with an enormous and ever-increasing speed on the order of the speed of light (“order” in no way means “equal”). And as the distance between them changed, a strong tidal deformation of these stars and the surrounding space arose, which gave rise to gravitational waves (Fig.2). And if the vibrations of a drop of water are characterized not only by a set of frequencies, but also by shapes - what can we expect from the stars! Among the spectrum of these vibrations, quadrupole modes play an important role. One of the researchers compared the difficulty of isolating the right signals to trying to hear a friend whispering at the other end of a noisy cafe. Perhaps this comparison is not clear enough - it is even more difficult to “hear” a gravitational signal using one detector. So, two detectors were installed at two points in the United States at a distance of approximately 3000 km from each other. The gravitational wave should have traveled this path in approximately 10 microseconds, assuming its speed was equal to the speed of light. Based on this, from a huge variety of noise it was possible to isolate two similar signals, shifted in time relative to each other by a known delay value (Fig.3). The frequency of recorded oscillations was in the range of 35–250 Hz. At the same time, the reliability of the experiment is considered very high: the probability of a false alarm is estimated at one event every 203 thousand years.
