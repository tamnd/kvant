Frequency is important in real life, for example in applied linguistics and cryptology (it has two branches: cryptography and cryptanalysis), and is also found in fiction. Edgar Poe's story “The Gold Bug” (1843) is one of the first popular (and artistic!) presentations of both the real method of encryption using the substitution method, replacing letters with some signs, and the method of decrypting it - frequency analysis. And in 1903, Arthur Conan Doyle, in a series of stories about Sherlock Holmes, published the story “The Dancing Men,” which is mathematically very similar to “The Gold Bug.”

There is no universal, correct corpus for a language, but we must learn to extract general properties, features, and peculiarities of a given language even from individual, one way or another “colored” corpora. For example, the frequency of the word anchor in NKR texts distributed by decade, from 1970 to the present day, looks strange: 1970s - occurs 160 times per million; 1980s – 6.8; 1990s – 8.4; 2000s – 6.6; 2010s – 6.7. The reason for the anomaly is the “Book of Anchors” included in the NKR, published in 1973. In it alone, the word anchor occurs 1769 times, and in the rest of the corpus - only 2896. The frequency of the word anchor throughout the entire array obtained by simple calculation - 21.9 per million - is clearly overestimated. But if you sort the frequency values ​​by decade and take the number from the middle of the list (the median), you get a more realistic result: 6.8 per million. In conclusion, we note that automatic language processing began to actively develop in the 1950s. In particular, at first machine translation was based on manually created rules that prescribed exactly how to translate a particular phrase under certain conditions. Gradually it became clear that creating rules requires a huge amount of human labor, but they still work poorly.
