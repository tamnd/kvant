Language statistics

A.PIPERSKI

THE DEVELOPMENT OF COMPUTERS has led to the creation of large collections of digitized texts in different languages ​​- the so-called linguistic corpora. These corpora can be processed using mathematical statistics methods. Mathematical models, sometimes unexpectedly simple but effective, allow computational linguists to offer humanity and the individual user a solution to problems associated with automatic natural language processing: speech recognition, text language detection and machine translation, text classification by topic, knowledge extraction from text, keyword extraction, text sentiment analysis (i.e., finding out whether it contains a positive or negative assessment), spam detection, creating chat bots, etc. Let's consider two tasks - automatic detection of the language of the text and correction of typos, good solutions for which are based on an analysis of the frequency of individual letters and words, as well as their combinations in real texts. Surprisingly, this approach allows one to solve these problems without having knowledge of either the grammatical rules of languages ​​or the meanings of the texts being analyzed. We will assume that the range of candidates is not too wide: English, Belarusian, Bulgarian, German, Russian, Ukrainian, French. The simplest idea that comes to mind is to determine the language alphabetically. In our case, this is Cyrillic, so we can immediately discard English, German and French. But this method will not solve the problem completely, for example, it does not cope well with the Russian and Bulgarian languages: the Bulgarian alphabet is part of the Russian one (there are no letters E, Y, E in Bulgarian), so any Bulgarian text can be mistaken for Russian. The relationship between the Russian and Ukrainian alphabets is more complex; neither is part of the other: Ukrainian does not have the letter Ъ, but there are letters to denote the vowel sounds ª, ², ≢ and the consonant ¥. But all the letters of this phrase are present in it. Belarusian does not have I (the letter ² is used instead), so it is not suitable. So, the alphabetic approach is not up to the task: there are three candidate languages ​​left. The presence of linguistic corpora allows one to analyze languages ​​and find the characteristics that distinguish them. In particular, the “passport” of a language can be the set of frequencies with which letters occur on average in this language. The frequency of letters was noticed back in the pre-computer era. For example, in the telegraph Morse code, which arose in the first half of the 19th century, the most frequently used letters were assigned shorter combinations of dots and dashes.
