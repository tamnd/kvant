The top row of the standard English keyboard layout, inherited from typewriters, is QWERTY. And in the German layout, the row familiar to the eye is replaced by QWERTZ - the letter Y in the German language is found much less frequently than Z, and is relegated to the periphery. Another example: in cryptography, simple ciphers based on letter substitutions lost their meaning once the frequency characteristics of languages ​​were studied. Naturally, in the 19th century frequency calculations were performed manually. Now, with the advent of linguistic corpora, the frequency of letters or words can be calculated on a computer, and this data will be more accurate and objective. If we agree that the Russian alphabet consists of 33 letters and a space, then it turns out that the most common character is a space (14.46%), followed by the vowels O (9.42%), E (7.33%), I (6.72%), A (6.52%) and the consonants N (5.83%), T (5.56%) (see figure). And the least common letters are F (0.27%), Ъ (0.03%) and Ё (0.01%). Of course, in each specific text the frequencies may differ from those given, but these deviations will be insignificant. The letter Ъ is rare in Russian, but in Bulgarian it is used many times more often: it denotes a special vowel sound such as the short “a” and is found even in the word bulgarski itself. The last letter in terms of frequency is b. All this shows that the frequency of letters is indeed an individual characteristic of the language. In computer analysis (for example, when identifying a language), text is a sequence of letters. In the simplest model, it is assumed that each letter in this sequence appears independently of the previous ones, i.e. the text is viewed as a chain of independent random events: having “read” a few letters, the reader does not know what awaits him next. Due to independence, the probability of encountering a given sequence of letters in the chosen language is equal to the product of the probabilities (frequencies) of occurrence of letters in this language.
