LANGUAGE STATISTICS

The frequency of sequences of two (or better yet three) letters is a very accurate characteristic of a language. The above method is the basis of all used language detectors, the most famous being the module in Google Translate. It turns out that solving this linguistic problem does not require knowledge of languages, pure statistics works. Correcting typos. Text editors and smartphones solve this problem using methods similar to those used in the language detection task. Only now the frequencies of not letters, but words and their sequences in the selected language are compared. Let's assume that the user entered the phrase:

His sleeve hurts a little, and the computer’s job is to find and correct typos in it. A person immediately understands that a typo was made in the word rukva, but the word rukva should be written. Let's try to teach this to a computer, using a gigantic linguistic corpus of Russian-language texts with a total length of 16 billion words. At the first stage, we will find suspicious words: words that are either absent from the corpus or appear there very rarely, say, for definiteness, no more than 100 times (the reason for the appearance of such words in the corpus may be typos). And words that appear more than 100 times make up a dictionary. Here is information about the frequencies of our four words in the corpus: his - 46643493, rukva - 50, a little - 3475296, pain - 203993. By convention, the algorithm decides that there is a typo in the word rukva. At the second stage, we will determine a set of words, one of which the user may have wanted to enter. It is obvious that these words must be similar, close in some sense to the word rukva: it is unlikely that the person wanted to print kolotka, but what they got was rukva. To measure the proximity of words in linguistics, the Damerau–Levenshtein distance (named after

American linguist and Russian mathematician). This distance is equal to the minimum number of “steps” required to transform one word into another. These steps are typical, standard errors when typing: replacing one letter with another, adding or deleting a letter, rearranging adjacent letters. For example, the distance between the words dog and cat is 3: replacing from to k (you get kobaka); replacing b with sh (cat); removal of the first a (cat). There is another path of length 3: dog o dog- o bipod o cat. But it will not be possible to carry out the transformation in less than 3 steps. This distance between words has all the usual properties of the distance between points on a plane: non-negativity, symmetry (the distance from dog to cat is equal to the distance from cat to dog), the triangle inequality is true. Now we can formalize the feeling that the word rukva can easily be obtained from the word rukva, but not from the word elbow: the Damerau-Levenshtein distance from rukva to rukva is 1, and from elbow to rukva - 5. There are usually few typos in one word, most often one. Let's find in the dictionary all the words that are at a distance of 1 from the suspicious word sleeve. There are not many candidate words: hand (deleting c), sleeve (rearranging a and c), letter (replacing p with b) and sleeves (adding a). You can stop here and offer the user a list of candidates - let him choose himself. This is exactly how spell checking works in Microsoft Word, for example. But the computer can go further and try to correct the typo, i.e. choose the most likely candidate and offer it to the user (this is what Google Docs does), or maybe substitute it into the proposal itself (this is how modules in smartphones usually work, “helping” type text). This selection of a single candidate is the next stage of the algorithm, which can be implemented in different ways.
