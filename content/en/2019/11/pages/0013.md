Therefore, by choosing the option with the maximum probability, in this example we get the correct answer: the phrase is written in Bulgarian. It is curious that such a simple algorithm worked well even on short text. But this doesn't always happen. For example, for the title of the book Mathematical Component an unexpected result is obtained: Bulgarian - 51.55%, Russian - 40.75%, Ukrainian - 7.7%. The affinity of this algorithm for the Bulgarian language is understandable and is of a general nature: it has fewer letters than the Russian or Ukrainian languages, which means that the frequency of an individual letter will be, on average, slightly higher. Therefore, the algorithm will consider most texts to be Bulgarian. The accuracy of determining the language of a text can be increased if we consider not the frequencies of letters individually, but the frequencies of combinations of characters of a certain length. The fact is that, in contrast to the simplest model used above, letters in real text are not independent: in fact, each letter depends on the previous ones, or at least on the previous one. So, according to the rules of the Russian language, after Ъ only the letters E, Ё, Yu or Ya can come. In Bulgarian after Ъ you can also find the letter L, and this is 10 times more likely than meeting with E, Yu and Ya combined. And in Ukrainian I is almost never used after a space - which means that our first phrase with the words ima and can hardly be Ukrainian. Academician Andrei Andreevich Markov (1856–1922) embodied this idea in a mathematical model, which was called the “Markov chain” in his honor. He studied the distribution of vowels and consonants in

sequences of 20,000 letters in the novel “Eugene Onegin” (the first chapter and the beginning of the second). The main conclusion was: “We see that the probability of a letter being a vowel changes significantly depending on whether it is preceded by a vowel or a consonant.” Calculations by A.A. Markov showed that the total share of vowels is 43.2%, but the probability of encountering a vowel after a vowel decreases to 12.8%, and after a consonant increases to 66.3%. It turns out that in a real text we are dealing not with the probabilities of independent random events, but with the conditional probabilities of sequentially occurring events. In the Markov model, the future depends on the present, but the past does not need to be analyzed: its influence lies in the present. An everyday example: when predicting the weather for tomorrow, you can focus on today’s. Winter thunderstorms are a rare occurrence, so if there is a thunderstorm today, tomorrow may be sunny or rainy, but it is unlikely to snow. On the other hand, if it snows today, it is unlikely to see a thunderstorm tomorrow. Markov chains as a mathematical tool can be used to analyze the distribution of not only vowels and consonants in a given language, but also for all pairs of letters of the alphabet. The dependence of the letter on the previous one is easy to notice. For example, in the Russian language, among the pairs starting with the letter Z, the most probable combinations are ZA (29.67%), ZN (10.18%), $З^{|}$ (space after Z; 8.36%), and after the letter A the same symbols A, $Н,^{|}$ give completely different results: AA (0.03%), AN (9.56%), $А^{|}$ (20.36%). To solve the problem of determining the language of a text, you can compare the frequency characteristics of pairs of identical characters in different languages. For example, the top three among pairs starting with the letter Z: in Russian texts – ZA, ZN, $З^{|}$; in Ukrainian – ZA, $З^{|}$, ZN; in Bulgarian - ZA, ZI, ZV. Knowing the frequencies of all possible pairs, it is possible to find the probability in the Markov model of words in each of the candidate languages.
