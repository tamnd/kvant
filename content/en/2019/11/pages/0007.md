SOUND IN SPACE. IS THIS POSSIBLE?

Sound on the Sun

The sun is a fairly dense ionized gas structure. We cannot doubt the existence of sound on the Sun. Of course, intense vortices of hot gas in its upper layers and turbulence create a terrible roar over a wide frequency band. However, on Earth we do not hear this roar, since between the Earth and the Sun there are 150 million kilometers of vacuum, which does not allow sound to pass through. In the upper layers of the solar atmosphere, the gas temperature is lower than in deeper layers. Based on the experience of studying the propagation of sound in a stratified medium, one can expect that sound will be concentrated in the area with the minimum speed of its propagation, i.e. in the layer with the minimum temperature. Sound, generated as a result of turbulent movements in convective zones, propagates in such a way as to remain in the upper layers of the solar atmosphere (Fig.4). At depth, the speed of sound and temperature increase sharply, and this causes sound to return back to the convective zone. The Sun has a spherical shape, so sound, propagating along cyclic trajectories along the surface of the Sun (p-modes at figure 4, which corresponds to pressure waves),

will increase if the number of such cycles is integer. In this case, we can consider the Sun to be a resonator in which certain vibrations – spherical modes – are amplified. Note that there cannot be shear waves on the Sun that would be effective in seismic problems on Earth or on other planets. The sun is a dense gas (or liquid), in such a medium there is no shear modulus and therefore the solar medium cannot ensure the propagation of a shear wave. At great depths, in the radiation zone and core, gravitational forces are significant, not elastic forces. Therefore, not elastic pressure waves will propagate there, but internal waves moving under the influence of buoyancy forces (g-modes at figure 4). So this is a hydrodynamic rather than an acoustic problem. These waves (g-modes) attenuate in the region of convective flows and do not reach the surface, and the surface of the Sun does not reflect information about the core. However, recently data has appeared on the possibility of observing these weak waves on Earth. The Sun's core rotates with a period of one revolution every seven days, which is much faster than the rotation of the radiation and convective zones. G-mode waves modulate the magnetic field created by the rotating core of the Sun, and these modulation components with a period of 7 days have been recorded by Earth magnetometers. Thus, it turns out that we can hear the sound of the Sun on Earth.
