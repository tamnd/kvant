KALEIDOSCOPE “QUANTA”

It is easy to show that copies of any triangle and any quadrilateral (even non-convex) can be used to tile a plane (see top row figure). It is more difficult to show that no convex polygon with seven or more sides can tile a plane and that only three families of convex hexagons allow such tessellation. The most difficult case is pentagons. By the end of the 20th century, 14 families of convex pentagons tiling a plane were found, another, 15th family was discovered in 2015 (see “Kaleidoscope «Kvant» No. 5 for 2017); but there is still no evidence that these are all families.

There are only two families of two-dimensional parallelohedra - parallelograms and centrally symmetric hexagons. In three-dimensional space, parallelohedra were classified by E. Fedorov in 1885. There are five families of three-dimensional parallelohedra with different combinatorial types. The most “regular” representatives of the families are the cube, regular hexagonal prism with equal edges, rhombic dodecahedron, elongated rhombic dodecahedron, truncated octahedron (see figure on the right). All the rest are obtained from them by affine transformations, as well as by “lengthening” operations of parallel edges.

Many convex polygons that can be used to tile a plane require that some of their copies be rotated or reflected about some line. Exceptions are parallelograms and centrally symmetric hexagons; they can be used to tile a plane using only parallel translations (see bottom row figure). As the dimension increases, the number of families grows very quickly. If in four-dimensional space there are exactly 52 families of parallelohedra, then in higher dimensions even their exact number is unknown. It is only known that in five-dimensional space there are at least 110,244 different families of parallelohedrons, and in six-dimensional space there are at least half a billion.
