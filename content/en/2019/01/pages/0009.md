The seminar was always conducted by Kapitsa himself and conducted it in an unusually organized manner - the main report and speeches were strictly limited in time and were interrupted “mid-sentence” in case of violation of the regulations. The presiding officer, with his questions, sharpened the very essence of the problem under discussion. And at the same time, the “climate” of these seminars was invariably favorable and respectful. And never at the seminars were there any pseudo-philosophical discussions of physics problems or ambitious speeches attacking colleagues. Only scientific truth confirmed by experiment was revealed. In the 30s, Kapitsa first of all completed his work on a turboexpander, which predetermined the development throughout the world of modern installations for separating air into nitrogen, oxygen, argon and helium. Liquid oxygen became one of the most important strategic materials during the war years. After all, steel for tank armor with additions of refractory metals (tungsten, etc.) could only be obtained using oxygen blast in open-hearth furnaces, which made it possible to achieve a higher melting point of such steel. And he makes great efforts to introduce his brainchild into industry - there were many opponents from among scientific officials who wanted to introduce their own inventions and dishonestly distorted the efficiency of the turboexpander. The widespread use of turboexpanders around the world influenced the country's leadership, and in 1941 Kapitsa, among the first scientists, became a laureate of the Stalin Prize of the first degree for his turboexpander, and in 1943 he was appointed head of Glavkislorod - the Main Directorate of the Oxygen Industry under the Council of People's Commissars of the USSR, to a position higher than the People's Commissar. In 1945 he became a Hero of Socialist Labor. But this engineering and organizational activity was largely forced for Kapitsa in connection with the events of those years. Kapitsa understood that tank battles would play a decisive role in the coming war. Kapitsa's main scientific interests were concentrated in another area. Having the required amount of liquid helium with the help of his turboexpander, he began work to study its properties. In 1937, a fundamental discovery was made. In extremely elegant experiments, studying the flow of liquid helium through narrow slits, Kapitsa found that at temperatures below 2.19 K its viscosity disappears. A phase transition (second order) occurs from helium-I to helium-II, which has a number of unusual properties and, above all, the absence of viscosity, i.e. superfluidity. The heat capacity and a number of other thermal characteristics of helium change abruptly. These experiments were not only elegant, they were very difficult. It is enough to note that at first only two people could pour superfluid liquid helium from a Dewar flask into a test tube - Kapitsa himself and his chief experimenter Shalnikov, and Shalnikov did it even better. It was clear to Kapitsa that the explanation for such strange properties of helium lies beyond the classical concepts of matter. Indeed, the first theory of superfluidity was created by L.D. Landau in 1941 as a quantum mechanical theory. Based on Kapitsa's experiments and Landau's theory, the physics of quantum liquids was born as a macroscopic manifestation of the quantum behavior of particles.
