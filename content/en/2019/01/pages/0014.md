G. F. Voronoi and the geometry of numbers

N. DOLBILIN

IN THIS ARTICLE WE WILL TRY to describe the general contours of the geometry of numbers - the science to which Georgiy Feodosievich devoted his most profound works. One of them – about the densest packings of spheres in multidimensional spaces – appeared in the last year of life. The other, on the theory of parallelohedrons, was published posthumously. Fast forward to 1904, when the third International Congress of Mathematicians took place in the picturesque German city of Heidelberg. Georgy Feodosievich Voronoi and Heinrich Minkowski (1864–1909), one of the largest geometers of the 19th century, were invited to make a presentation at this congress. An “invited talk” at an international congress is a very prestigious offer awarded to mathematicians who have achieved outstanding results. Minkowski 8 years earlier, in 1896, published the famous work “Geometry of Numbers” (in German “Geometrie der Zahlen”), in which he laid the foundations for a new field of mathematics - the geometry of numbers. The main idea of ​​this science, located at the intersection of geometry and number theory, is that in number theory there are many problems that can be reformulated in geometric language, after which the problem becomes more transparent and it becomes possible to apply geometric methods to solve it. Perhaps the most famous geometric theorem that helps solve problems in number theory is Minkowski’s theorem on a centrally symmetric convex body (figure).

Let us consider on the plane a rectangular coordinate system x, y and the set of all integer points, i.e. points for which both coordinates are integers. This set is called a square lattice (Fig.1).

Further, a figure F is called convex if for any two points A and B from F the segment connecting them > AB @ entirely belongs to the figure. Figure F has a center of symmetry, i.e. such a point $O ^{F}$ if for any point X F the point X c symmetric to it with respect to O also belongs to the figure F. A figure with a center of symmetry is called centrally symmetric. For example, a circle is a convex, centrally symmetrical figure, while a triangle is convex, but not centrally symmetrical. The famous Minkowski theorem states: Let the center of symmetry O be located at a lattice point. Then if the area of ​​the figure is $s^{F} t 4 2^{2}$, then inside or on the boundary of the figure there is at least one pair of lattice points symmetric with respect to O, different from point O.
