KVANT 2019/№1

In 1945, the “atomic age” broke out. In August of this year, the United States took revenge on the Japanese people for the Japanese air attack (without a declaration of war) on the American naval base in Hawaii in December 1941. But revenge was too inadequate. Then 2,403 people died and 1,178 were injured. And in Hiroshima and Nagasaki, more than 100,000 people died almost instantly, and another 200,000 died over the course of several years as a result of radiation damage. In addition to the idea of ​​\u200b\u200bvengeance, this action had the obvious nature of intimidating the USSR. It became clear to Soviet physicists that they were far behind foreign scientists in the use of nuclear energy. In 1946, a special State Committee was created - without a name, but simply No. 1 - under the command of L.P. Beria. Initially, it included only two physicists - Kapitsa and Kurchatov. But, unlike Kurchatov, Kapitsa was not entrusted with the highest state secret - the receipt by our intelligence of detailed (and, most importantly, reliable) information not only about the schematic diagrams of nuclear bombs, but also drawings of its main components. Knowing nothing about this, Kapitsa, driven by patriotic feelings, is developing a two-year project for the deployment of all the work necessary, in his opinion, to create nuclear weapons. Kapitsa is surprised why his project is not even being discussed. He does not suspect that Stalin and Beria were determined to trust intelligence data and copy the American bomb. But Kurchatov is clear that this can significantly speed up the creation of the Soviet bomb, and, most importantly, it is clear to him that after the atomic bombings there is no longer any secret. The designs of individual bomb components are no longer of fundamental importance. The main difficulty is the production of the required amount of nuclear “fuel” - the isotope of uranium-235 and plutonium. In the intelligence data, Kurchatov saw information about giant factories built for these purposes in the USA, and realized that this was the main field of activity

State Committee. But Kapitsa has no idea about the scale of this work; he is thinking about creating bomb physics. Stung by the fact that the head of the committee, Beria, does not understand anything in physics, he writes a letter to Stalin about his illiteracy, comparing Beria to a conductor who is trying to lead an orchestra without even knowing how to read music. Stalin shows the letter to Beria, and he, enraged, promises to erase the great physicist “into camp dust.” Stalin tells Beria that he will remove Kapitsa from all posts as punishment, but there is no need to touch him. And the next day Kapitsa is no longer a member of the State Committee, not the head of Glavkislorod, not the director of the Institute of Physical Problems. He is just an academician and professor at Moscow State University. A cottage equipped to his liking in the courtyard of the institute is taken away from him, since to enter the courtyard he needs a special pass, which is simply taken away from him. But Stalin leaves him an academic dacha in the village of Nikolina Gora near Zvenigorod. The President of the USSR Academy of Sciences S.I. Vavilov is doing everything he can. He supplies the country shed, which was soon nicknamed "the hut of physical problems", with several machines, materials, workers and an engineer. In the “hut”, working with liquid helium was, of course, impossible. And Kapitsa switches to completely different things - the creation of powerful ultra-high-frequency pulsed and continuous generators and the study of the properties of the plasma resulting from the action of such generators on a gas discharge, in particular, the study of the nature of ball lightning, which he was able to obtain in laboratory conditions. Kapitsa's generators formed the basis of devices that turned out to be absolutely necessary in modern work on mastering thermonuclear energy in tokamaks. For the implementation of these works, it turned out to be extremely important that Kapitsa was an amazingly “handy” experimental physicist.
