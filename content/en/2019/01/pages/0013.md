PETER

LEONIDOVICH

What achievements he considers most significant, Kapitsa said that for a scientist the most important work is always the work he is doing at the moment. “My work relates to thermonuclear fusion,” he added. And in his Nobel lecture, he took liberties and continued this topic. Contrary to the regulations of the Nobel Foundation, the lecture was not devoted to the work awarded the prize. It was called “Plasma and Thermonuclear Reaction.” Kapitsa said that he studied ultra-low temperatures a long time ago, in the days of his scientific youth. But now, on the contrary, he is concerned about the ultra-high temperatures that are necessary to initiate and maintain the thermonuclear reaction of hydrogen isotopes. The extremes have converged. (“Les extremes se touchent,” he quoted the famous French expression.) Moreover, liquid helium, for which the award was awarded, is also needed in this case to cool the windings of super-strong magnets that hold the hot plasma. And he ended the lecture with the words: “It is well known that at this time a controlled thermonuclear reaction is of great practical interest, since this process could most effectively solve the problem of the global energy crisis associated with the depletion of fossil raw materials, now used as a source of energy.” Today, more than 40 years later, these prophetic words are being put into practice by the global scientific community and the governments of the leading countries of the world, including Russia. Kapitsa's pedagogical activity was very important. In 1945, he was one of the main initiators of the creation of a new type of university for training physicists, where training would be carried out not only within the walls of the educational institution, but mostly in the laboratories of research institutes, where teachers should be leading scientists. And such a university was created. At first it was the secret (atomic-rocket) Faculty of Physics and Technology of Moscow State University, and in 1951 it became the Institute of Physics and Technology, the famous

Phystech. Kapitsa headed the department of general and experimental physics there. In the early years, lectures on this subject were given in turn by Kapitsa himself and Landau. The theorist Landau outlined the theoretical foundations of the next topic, and at the next lecture Kapitsa spoke about the experimental and historical foundations of this branch of physics. It was amazingly interesting. After the previous lesson, students rushed headlong through the corridors of the Physics and Technology Institute in order to have time to take seats closer to the wonderful lecturers. In the book “P.L. Kapitsa. Experiment. Theory. Practice" (Moscow: Nauka, 1974) contains articles and speeches by Kapitsa on a variety of topics. The book allows us to understand Kapitsa’s extraordinary gift for talking about the most complex problems of science in an intelligible and interesting way. His son, S.P. Kapitsa, received part of this gift from his father and took up the baton of teaching physics at the Physics and Technology Institute, becoming a professor of the department of general physics and its head. The famous English physicist Paul Dirac was a great friend of Kapitsa. He repeatedly came to the Soviet Union to see him and his other friend, I.E. Tamm. On his last visit in 1973, Dirac often visited Kapitsa at his dacha (I.E. Tamm had passed away). And when Kapitsa told another story from his life, and he was a wonderful storyteller, Dirac could not resist the emotional: “Kapitsa! What a wonderful life, I envy you.” Indeed, Kapitsa’s life is a vivid example of selfless service to science and fearlessness in the face of ignorance and authority. Even in the darkest times of Soviet isolationism, Kapitsa always defended the principles of internationalism in science. “I firmly believe in the internationality of science and believe that real science should be outside all political passions and struggles, no matter how they try to involve it there” (from a letter to Molotov dated May 7, 1935). Pyotr Leonidovich Kapitsa died of a heart attack on April 8, 1984, just short of his ninetieth birthday.
