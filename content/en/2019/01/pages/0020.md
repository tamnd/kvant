KVANT 2019/№1

When deducing all five types of parallelohedra, Fedorov assumed that the parallelohedron is necessarily centrally symmetric. Despite the arguments in favor of this assumption, Fedorov did not have strict proof. Unlike the two-dimensional case, proving the central symmetry of a three-dimensional parallelohedron is not so simple. G. Minkovsky, after Fedorov’s work on parallelohedra, became interested in this class of polyhedra, and of arbitrary dimension. An attempt to prove the central symmetry of a parallelohedron led Minkowski to the discovery of one of the most beautiful theorems in the theory of convex polyhedra - the theorem on the existence and uniqueness of a convex polyhedron with given directions of faces and their areas. From this difficult to prove theorem, Minkowski easily deduced the following first two properties of parallelohedra, to which the third was later added (B.N. Delaunay): 1) a parallelohedron is a centrally symmetric polyhedron; 2) all faces of the parallelohedron are centrally symmetrical polygons; 3) the projection of a parallelohedron along any of its edges onto a plane perpendicular to it is either a parallelogram or a centrally symmetric hexagon. Moreover, all three properties are true for parallelohedra of any dimension. Of course, for parallelohedra of dimension d t 4, properties 2) and 3) must be edited. So, in condition 2) it should be: “all faces of dimension d – 1 are centrally symmetric polyhedra”; and in condition 3) we are talking about the projection of a parallelohedron along any of its d 2-dimensional faces onto a plane perpendicular to it. Much later, in 1954, B.A. Venkov proved that these three conditions are not only necessary, but also sufficient: any convex polyhedron that satisfies conditions 1)–3) is a parallelohedron.

Voronoi parallelohedrons

How are parallelohedrons arranged in spaces of arbitrary dimensions? How to find all parallelohedra, or more precisely, all combinatorial types of parallelohedra? Voronoi’s latest memoir was dedicated to this problem. In it, Georgy Feodosievich constructed a theory of parallelohedrons of a special type, now called Voronoi parallelohedrons. In order to construct a Voronoi region, in principle, you need to connect a given point A with each point B / and draw a bisector perpendicular to the segment > AB @. The median perpendicular divides the plane into two half-planes. The half-plane containing point A consists of those points of the X plane that are located closer to A than to B. That part of the plane that belongs to all such half-planes (the intersection of half-planes) is the Voronoi region for point A. According to the definition, the Voronoi region is the intersection of an infinite number of half-planes. In reality, only a finite number of median perpendiculars corresponding to points B from / relatively close to A are involved in the construction of the Voronoi region.
