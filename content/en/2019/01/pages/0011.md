Friends and colleagues come to visit Kapitsa on Nikolina Gora; a “capital worker” who has become illegal works, although not regularly. His teaching activity at the Faculty of Physics and Technology of Moscow State University continues brilliantly. But in 1950, having found fault with a trivial matter, he was removed from heading the department at the Physics and Technology Institute and giving lectures there. And one of I.V. Kurchatov’s associates, the future president of the USSR Academy of Sciences A.P. Aleksandrov, agreed to become the director of the Institute of Physical Problems, to whom Kapitsa never forgave him for agreeing to this post. However, Aleksandrov retained all areas of research led by Kapitsa, and especially the theoretical department headed by L.D. Landau. Eight years pass like this. As if by magic, everything changed after the death of Stalin and the fall of Beria. Kapitsa again becomes director of the Institute of Physical Problems and editor-in-chief of the most authoritative scientific journal - the Journal of Experimental and Theoretical Physics. Along with I.E. Tamm, only more sharply, in the Academy of Sciences he is fighting for the restoration of biological science in the USSR, destroyed by the pseudoscientific activities of T.D. Lysenko and his followers. The full-fledged work of the “capitalist” is resumed, which becomes not only the center of physical science, but also the cultural center of Moscow, when at its meetings the scientific topic is replaced by performances by artists, writers, and poets. And “all of Moscow” tried to get into such “capitals.” In these cases, it was necessary to organize a radio broadcast from the meeting room to all rooms of the building - they were filled with guests.

KAPITSA

⟦column⟧

In 1974, on the days of his eightieth birthday, Kapitsa became twice Hero of Socialist Labor. And in 1978, he was finally awarded the long-deserved Nobel Prize “for basic research and discoveries in the field of low temperatures.” It is characteristic that, unusually generous in life, Kapitsa did not give a penny of the prize he received to the state. Kapitsa loved Russia; he never agreed to leave it, despite repeated proposals from many scientific centers around the world. But he actively disliked Russian governments: neither Stalin’s, nor Khrushchev’s, nor Brezhnev’s. He could not come to terms, for example, with the fact that in order to obtain any trivial materials or details he had to knock on thresholds, and, most importantly, lose months, whereas “in England it would have taken me two days,” as he writes in one of his letters to government officials. So, during the formation of his institute, Kapitsa turned to one of the People's Commissariats with a request for tools and measuring instruments. He was asked to fill out an application “for next year”... Kapitsa did not trust the telephone, firstly, because of possible wiretapping by authorities, and secondly, because of the difficulty of justifying his position due to the forced brevity of telephone conversations of that time (especially on the Kremlin “turntable”).
