P.L.Kapitsa at his desk

300 (!) letters, 50 of them to I.V. Stalin. Sometimes there were answers. The Kapitsa archive contains the following letter: “Comrade. Kapitsa! I received all your letters. There are a lot of instructive things in the letters - I think I’ll meet you someday and talk about it. As for L. Gumilevsky’s book “Russian Engineers”, it is very interesting and will be published soon. I. Stalin. April 6, 46." There were also responses with action. P.L. Kapitsa repeatedly stood up in the 1930s for scientists who were arrested or persecuted. On at least three occasions, Kapitsa's letters turned out to be their salvation. In 1936, the campaign of persecution that had started in the press against the outstanding mathematician Academician N.N. Luzin suddenly stopped. The arrested theoretical physicist V.A. Fok was released a few days later. The most difficult was Kapitsa's struggle for the liberation of L.D. Landau. He was arrested for participating in a group distributing leaflets with anti-Soviet content. And although his participation in this group was only formal, at the level of conversations, he was accused of spying for Germany. Demanding that he confirm such a monstrous accusation, Landau was subjected to particularly sophisticated torture by starvation. From the neighboring sanatorium they brought a tablespoon of unusually tasty food, and that was all for the whole day. Pizza wrote several dozen letters, vouched “for his exemplary behavior” and finally went to the Kremlin and declared that if Landau was not released, he would stop doing science. The next day Landau was released. In the 1970s, when the persecution of Academician A.D. Sakharov flared up, Kapitsa was the only one who openly rebelled against Sakharov’s expulsion from the Academy of Sciences. At a meeting of the Presidium of the Academy with the participation of the party leadership, he found the right words: “A similar shameful incident has already happened. In 1933, the Nazis expelled Albert Einstein from the Berlin Academy.” Probably, after this speech, many academicians felt deep down in their souls embarrassed for their non-resistance to superior pressure. Sakharov himself, Solzhenitsyn, and many other representatives of culture, whose activities were not approved by the authorities, repeatedly visited Kapitsa’s dacha. In general, Kapitsa’s humanitarian interests were very broad. He knew European cultural monuments well. Returning to Russia, it was only after the war that he was able to find time for an excursion along the “Golden Ring,” as such trips later became known. He was accompanied by art historians, and with them he took a thorough course in the history of ancient Russian culture. In those years, all the ancient monuments of Vladimir, Suzdal and other cities, and especially the great Church of the Intercession on the Nerl, were in disrepair. Shocked by the beauty of the ancient temples, Kapitsa turns to the government with more than just a letter. He called his appeal “Memorandum on the ancient monuments of the Vladimir region.” It was a detailed description of the desolation in which cultural monuments were located, and at the same time a detailed program of what needed to be done to restore and preserve these monuments. The news of the Nobel Prize award found Kapitsa at the academic sanatorium in Uzkoy, on the outskirts of Moscow, where he was vacationing with his wife.
