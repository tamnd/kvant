Customers said: “Why pay £1,000?” Kapitsa replied: “One pound for a hit, and 999 pounds for the fact that you had to know where to hit.” And suddenly everything changed. On one of his annual visits for summer holidays and lectures in Moscow, Leningrad and Kharkov in 1934, his foreign visa was canceled and he was deprived of his international passport. The formal reason for this was his consultations with “bourgeois” firms, including military-industrial ones. The wife leaves to join her sons who remain in Cambridge with their grandmother. And Kapitsa takes an irreconcilable position and declares that if he is not provided with the same scientific equipment that he organized in Cambridge, then he will leave physics and go to work as an ordinary engineer at the biological institute of the great I.P. Pavlov. Rutherford is enraged by this act of the Soviet government and initiates a campaign of condemnation in the press. The USSR government chickened out and issued a decree on the creation of a special institute for Kapitsa and the purchase of equipment from the Cambridge laboratory in England from Rutherford. Rutherford meets Kapitsa halfway, agreeing to sell the equipment, so to speak, at cost, and in addition sends two engineers to Moscow to set it up. In Moscow, on the site chosen by Kapitsa - on the Sparrow Hills, on the high bank of the Moscow River - a new academic Institute of Physical Problems is being built with the necessary services and a cottage for Kapitsa and his family. Kapitsa becomes the director of his institute under unusual conditions of complete independence in matters of managing the institute. At first there were only 15 scientific employees, but more than 100 engineers and technicians and only 3 management employees. The institute does not have a personnel department, Kapitsa himself gets to know everyone

For example, he fires three idle janitors and replaces them with one, but for triple pay, fortunately the so-called financial discipline does not concern him. He does not have a chief accountant, he himself is in charge of the distribution of funds, there is only an accountant-cashier. For the position of his assistant, he finds Olga Aleksandrovna Stetskaya, an employee of N.K. Krupskaya, who was included in all government offices and, moreover, had a prominent party leader as her husband. For many years, she freed Kapitsa from all the troublesome matters associated with various “authorities.” A.F. Ioffe gives Kapitsa a “royal” gift, giving one of the two glassblowing brothers Petushkov, extraordinary craftsmen in working with glass (and in experiments with liquid helium this was, oh, how necessary). Kapitsa’s main experimenter was Alexander Iosifovich Shalnikov, a future academician (1979), three times laureate of the State (Stalin) Prizes of the USSR, the author of many experimental discoveries that revealed the quantum essence of the phenomena of superfluidity, superconductivity, phonon gas in crystals, features of semiconductors and much more. And Pyotr Leonidovich Kapitsa, the leader and director of these works, became not only a classic of natural science, but also a scientist at the forefront of modern physics. For a theoretical understanding of his experimental achievements, Kapitsa invites and makes the head of the theoretical department of the institute a young, but already European-famous theoretical physicist Lev Davydovich Landau (1908–1968), a future Nobel Prize laureate “for pioneering theories of condensed matter and especially liquid helium.” An important role was played by the weekly seminar on physics organized by Kapitsa, which received the unofficial name “Kapishnik”.
