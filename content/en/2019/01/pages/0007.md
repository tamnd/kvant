When the construction of the Mondov laboratory for Kapitsa was completed, at its grand opening it turned out that the solid entrance key was the figure of a crocodile opening the lock, and in the lobby of the laboratory everyone was greeted by a bas-relief on which a crocodile was depicted. Rutherford remained silent, and almost everyone present approved of such liberty, especially Niels Bohr. Here is another feature of Kapitsa’s eccentricity. It was a great pleasure for him to drive wildly in a powerful car. His speed limit was 60 mph (about 110 km/h), while the legal maximum was 40 mph. When one day a frightened passenger, priest and historian Simpson, drew Kapitsa’s attention to the speedometer readings, he replied that this was a device specially converted for him and that it showed not English miles, but Russian kilometers. And before a dangerous turn he said: “Pray, Simpson, pray to God!” Sincere friendship connected P.L. Kapitsa with N.N. Semenov since their student days. Until their old age, they called each other “Petka” and “Kolka” both in letters and in live communication. After Kapitsa left for Cambridge, they did not see each other for several years. In 1926, Semenov was sent to England and France. In Cambridge, he meets his friend “Petka” and introduces him to his wife. And in Paris, Semenov’s wife introduces Kapitsa to her gymnasium friend Anna Alekseevna Krylova, the daughter of a prominent scientist, mathematician and shipbuilder A.N. Krylov. The civil war destroyed his family - two sons (Denikin officers) died, and his wife and daughter emigrated to France. At the age of 23, Anna had experienced a lot and was homesick in Paris for her homeland and the environment of Russian scientific culture that had surrounded her since childhood. Anna Alekseevna and Pyotr Leonidovich (Paris, 1927) Evna not only managed to create a well-to-do family “rear” for Kapitsa, but was also his main moral support in all the adversities, in all the injustices of life. Their son Sergei (1928–2012) was born in Cambridge, also a physicist, but who gained greater fame as a popularizer of science, the creator and for almost 40 years the permanent host of the famous popular science television program “The Obvious - the Incredible.” Today in Moscow, along with Academician Kapitsa Street, there is also Sergei Kapitsa Street. Three years later, the second son Andrei (1931–2011) is born, a geographer, researcher of the last major geographical discovery in the twentieth century - the existence of a huge subglacial lake in Antarctica, the initiator of the creation and first director of the Pacific Institute of Geography of the Far Eastern Scientific Center of the USSR Academy of Sciences. Kapitsa's scientific interests are increasingly focused on obtaining liquid helium and studying its properties. His engineering talent and skill attract the attention of many industrial companies - he is invited for consultations, including to military-industrial organizations. Consultations are generously paid. Kapitsa becomes a financially secure person. One of these consultations is associated with a famous, no, not an anecdote, but a story about a real event.
