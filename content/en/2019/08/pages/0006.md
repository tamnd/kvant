For example, it was predicted that at any temperature a completely black body should radiate an infinite amount of energy. This prediction even received a special name - ultraviolet catastrophe. The fact is that, according to the theory of those years, the emitted energy increases with the frequency of light. I understand that even now many may be perplexed: “It’s a terrible thing! They cannot explain how a hole emits energy. How much benefit science has already brought, what opportunities lie ahead, but they scare us with an ultraviolet catastrophe, as if it could harm someone.” I think many people, and not only people far from physics, reasoned this way or in a similar way at the turn of the 20th century. And Anna Akhmatova’s lines from “Poem without a Hero” haunt me:

It was not the calendar century that was approaching—the Real Twentieth Century.

After all, it was in 1900 that Max Planck realized that it is possible to reconcile observations of black body radiation with theory only if we assume that the atoms of a solid body do not obey the laws of Newtonian mechanics, the same mechanics that has explained so much, which has brought and will undoubtedly bring infinitely much benefit. According to Planck (in open contradiction with classical mechanics), the energy of a vibrating atom can only take on integer values ​​- energy quanta. An event that seemed to be on the periphery of the science of its time, about a quarter of a century later, the scientific community would call a revolutionary coup. It marked the birth of a new, quantum physics. Many active young physicists of that time enthusiastically devoted their talent to the new science. What motivated them? Let's not guess.
