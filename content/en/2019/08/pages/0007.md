SURPRISE, UNDERSTANDING, ADMIRATION

add doubt: “The highest accuracy, clarity and confidence - at the expense of completeness... Does the result of such a modest activity deserve the proud name of “picture of the world”?” Einstein’s answer is quite definite: “I think yes, because the general principles underlying the mental constructions of theoretical physics claim to be valid for all events occurring in nature. By purely logical deduction one could derive a picture from them, i.e. the theory of all natural phenomena, including life." This is followed by a caveat: “unless this process of deduction goes far beyond the creative possibilities of human thinking.” An important place in Einstein's article is occupied by the question of the role of intuition in creating an adequate picture of the world. I will cite just one phrase, which I consider very important for understanding the nature of knowledge: “It is not a logical path that leads to these laws, but only intuition based on insight into the essence of experience.” As the development of science shows, the limits of human thinking are expanding fantastically. And yet we cannot answer unambiguously whether a theory of all natural phenomena, including life, will be built or not. It seems to me that the process of knowledge will last as long as human civilization continues to exist. Unfortunately, it is not difficult to imagine the tragic outcome of the progress we so revered. Let us present what was said in high style in a much more prosaic manner. From a huge number of facts that were not understood by the end of the 19th century, Max Planck chose one - black body radiation. He tried to find an explanation within the framework of classical physics, failed and came to the conclusion: it is not always possible to use the laws of classical physics. The physics of Newton and Faraday–Maxwell received the epithet “classical” after the new, quantum physics arose and developed. At the turn of the 19th and 20th centuries it was all

physics, which, I repeat, has justified itself perfectly. Undoubtedly, it all began with surprise: why is it not possible to describe black body radiation in classical terms? I call for admiration at how timely Planck’s “seditious” idea was expressed. Ernest Rutherford found out that almost the entire mass of an atom is concentrated in its nucleus, and the idea of ​​the atom as a planetary system arose: the nucleus is the sun, the electrons are the planets. Niels Bohr realized that without involving Planck's idea of ​​quanta, it is impossible to explain the stability of atoms. Bohr's theory of the hydrogen atom is eclectic: quantum conditions were arbitrarily added to the well-known laws of motion of Newton's mechanics, and the formula for the hydrogen spectrum correctly described the experimental facts. The winners are not judged, but at what cost was the result obtained! In essence, the strict logic of all physics has been destroyed. Naturally, the search began for a way out of this situation. Now that the course of quantum mechanics is included in the standard set of subjects studied by the majority of young people receiving higher technical and natural science education, it is very difficult to imagine what not only intellectual, but also emotional stress it took to create the foundations of a new physics. One of Daniil Danin’s books describes such an episode. Werner Heisenberg, then a young scientist, discussed with Niels Bohr the logical difficulties of the new physics. The discussion went on for a long time. It seemed to both that there was no way out. Bohr came to the conclusion that the discussion should be stopped for a while, and, without warning his young colleague, he left, taking a vacation for himself. When Heisenberg found out that his teacher had left him, he burst into tears (!). The outcome of the episode is remarkable: Bohr returned from vacation with the principle of complementarity, and Heisenberg, in Bohr's absence, derived the uncertainty relation.
