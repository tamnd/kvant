Question 4. Why? People took a different path and created brightness amplifiers and night vision devices. But there are still external restrictions. In clean air, a person sees a horizon, which (if you stand on the surface of an ideal sphere) is about five kilometers away (and on the Moon?). Moreover, a person sees the horizon by climbing the highest mountain, and in this case it is more than three hundred kilometers away. In ideally clean air, due to the scattering of light by density fluctuations, the range of vision is limited to approximately the same value, but this is a coincidence. In other words, the scattering of light in the atmosphere, the very thing that makes the sky blue, at a distance of three hundred kilometers creates such a background that objects will not be visible in this haze. But if the thickness of the atmosphere were 30 times greater than it is now, we would not have astronomy, and the picture of the world would be different for a long time. It is precisely this – scattering by density fluctuations – that determines the minimum losses in an optical fiber, in light guides, the so-called Rayleigh losses. In general, losses in optical fibers are a relevant and actively researched area. For one simple reason: fiber optic lines carry most of everything—everything at all! – information that humanity sends.

In outer space there is a vast expanse of vision - the most distant stars that a person can see without the use of optical instruments are located at a distance of about ten thousand light years. But there is a fundamental difference between the Earth and Space in terms of the use of vision. What we see on Earth has made it possible to construct, albeit a limited, but correct picture of the world. Where a person saw a mountain, it was really difficult to climb, and where a person saw a river, there was water, fish and hungry crocodiles. But in order to understand at least the most general features of what we see in Space, it was necessary to create telescopes and other optical instruments. The main thing is that physics was required. What would happen if the range of vision was significantly shorter? For example, not kilometers, but meters - due to, say, less transparency of the atmosphere? The tactics for developing the earth's surface would change - no sailing on the seas, much less no flying. Man would begin to build his structures (both buildings and roads) sequentially and, if possible, continuously “creeping” civilization onto the surface of the Earth and only then building his structures upward. Society could develop, technology and science too, but the idea of ​​the planet as a whole, even if it arose, would be much later. And the idea of ​​the Cosmos would not have arisen at all - until the moment when the civilizational skin had grown to the boundaries of the atmosphere. Which, due to the limited strength of building materials, would generally be problematic. We see that the possibility of “far-sighting” significantly influences the appearance of civilization. We are lucky - the Sun radiates within the transparency range of the Earth's atmosphere. If it shined in that part of the ultraviolet or infrared range, in which the atmosphere is not transparent, we would live in darkness.
