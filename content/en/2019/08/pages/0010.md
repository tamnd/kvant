An increasing number of scientists are inclined to think that the explanation (description) of even the most mysterious phenomenon (the phenomenon of life) will not require the discovery of particles (not only electrons, protons, neutrons, but also atoms and molecules) of any specific properties that manifest themselves only when the particles take part in the work of a living organism. The last thesis, of course, is unprovable. But the history of science over the past more than 70 years, with its truly breathtaking rise, does not know a single fact that would contradict this statement. Does this mean that the role of future physicists is only to clarify the description of occurring phenomena and improve our understanding of the properties of bodies? Of course not! I just want to draw attention to where on our imaginary “globe” are the sources of new facts that can force us to change our ideas about what the world is built from. At the beginning of the 20th century, it became clear that trying to understand the structure of the world requires studying the movement of microscopic objects in regions of space a hundred million times smaller than one centimeter (this is the size of an atom). The discovery that each atom contains a compact nucleus of protons and neutrons has forced attention to spatial scales hundreds of thousands of times smaller than the atom. This is the work of nuclear physics, which studies the structure of atomic nuclei and the forces acting between protons and neutrons. Although the objects of nuclear physics are so miniature $( 10^{-} 14 - 10^{-} 13$ cm), its successes brought nuclear energy and unprecedented methods of killing to the world - atomic and thermonuclear (hydrogen) bombs. I will pass over these achievements of nuclear physics in silence. To my delight they add little. But there is something about thermonuclear physics that fascinates me - understanding the mechanism of the glow of the Sun and other stars.
