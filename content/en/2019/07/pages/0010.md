Then experimenters found anticopies of other elementary particles. The asymmetry of the Universe, which Sakharov thought about in the mid-60s, was that there are very few antiparticles in the Universe - compared to particles. And thank God for such asymmetry - otherwise every second meteorite would consist of antimatter. And such an antimeteorite, even if it contained only one gram, in contact with the substance of the Earth, would produce an explosion with the power of an atomic bomb. But physicists did not understand this will of God - it seemed self-evident that every particle and its antiparticle were created equal in their rights. This means, it would seem, matter and antimatter should be equally represented in the Universe. Astrophysicists began to look for signs of antimatter in space. Science fiction writers staged dramatic encounters between an earthly spaceship and an unearthly one and - quite possibly! – consisting of antimatter. Sakharov, however, took the observed asymmetry seriously and attributed it to the Universe as a whole, and not just to the Earth's cosmic environment. Then it was necessary to understand how the symmetry of microparticles can be combined with the asymmetry of the Universe. By that time, however, the symmetry of the microcosm had no longer become as simple as the right and left wings of a butterfly, mirror symmetrical (Fig.4). In other words, the butterfly will not change if, simultaneously with the rearrangement of the right and left, the black and white colors are swapped - the particles are swapped with antiparticles and, accordingly, each Charge is replaced by its opposite. This is CP symmetry. But it turned out to be not the last word in science. In 1964, experimenters discovered the first non-CP symmetric phenomenon. What could theorists say to this? For example, the fact that in everyday life people often confuse right and left and turn white into black. Theoretical physicists can do much more tricky things - change the direction of time to the opposite. By showing the film in reverse, you can put toothpaste squeezed out of a tube back on the screen. In real life, no one has seen this, and physicists call such a process asymmetric in time, or Tasymmetric. As for elementary particles, physicists have long believed that in the microcosm all phenomena are T-symmetric, like in billiards: if you film the collision of balls and run the film in the opposite direction, you will not notice anything strange on the screen. After the collapse of P-symmetry, physicists began to peer into other symmetries. They didn't need a rewind button to ask a question about T-symmetry in the microcosm; all they needed was a pen and paper. As a result, they established the most general law of symmetry of the microworld - the CPT law. To turn one wing of a CPT butterfly into another, you need to swap the right and left, the particle and its antiparticle (black and white), the past and the future (reverse time T) (Fig.6).
