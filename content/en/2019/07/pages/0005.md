The day before, each of them received a piece of paper in which he was asked if he knew: i that during the Second World War, Soviet and American soldiers fought against a common enemy - Nazism; i but that a decade and a half later, Soviet thermonuclear missiles were aimed at American cities, and American missiles were aimed at Russia; i that Soviet thermonuclear weapons were invented by the young physicist Andrei Sakharov and for this he was generously awarded by the Soviet government; i that, having realized the harmful effects of atmospheric nuclear testing on the health of mankind, he promoted a ban on such testing; i and realizing that lack of freedom threatens the life of humanity, he began to defend human rights as the basis of reliable peace between peoples; i that the Nobel Committee awarded his efforts with the Peace Prize, and the Soviet government with exile to the city of Gorky; i that the physicist Sakharov was most interested in how the Universe works; i and - for dessert for seventh graders - that Andrei Sakharov began studying at school in the seventh grade, and before that he studied at home.

From the handwritten heritage of Andrei Sakharov

It’s better to see once than to hear a hundred times, and to help 13-year-old Americans understand the Russian physicist-humanist, I took with me two of his small manuscripts. I hoped that they would help connect the incredible twists and turns of Sakharov's biography by showing how symmetry and asymmetry can be connected. First I showed a manuscript written partly in the international language of arithmetic (Fig.1).
