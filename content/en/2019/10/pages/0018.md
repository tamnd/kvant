Semi-metallic luster is similar to the luster of a tarnished metal surface and is found in opaque and translucent minerals (graphite, hematite, dark sphalerite, magnetite). The most widespread is non-metallic luster. It is characteristic of a number of transparent and translucent minerals. There are quite a large number of varieties of non-metallic luster: $\sum$ glass - reminiscent of the luster of the glass surface, this is the most common type of luster, about 70% of all minerals have it (quartz on the edges of crystals, calcite, dolomite, fluorite, feldspars); $\sum$ diamond – a very strong sparkling shine, often obscuring the natural color of the mineral (diamond, light sphalerite, cassiterite); $\sum$ greasy – close to glassy, ​​but somewhat duller luster, when the surface of the mineral seems to be covered with a film of fat (fractured quartz, nepheline, native sulfur); $\sum$ mother-of-pearl – similar to the shine of a mother-of-pearl shell with iridescent tints, characteristic of lamellar mineral aggregates (muscovite, gypsum, talc); $\sum$ silky – observed with a fine-fiber structure of minerals and resembles the shine of silk threads (asbestos, fibrous gypsum); $\sum$ waxy – dull, reminiscent of the shine of wax, characteristic of aggregates with a fairly rough surface (chalcedony, flint); $\sum$ matte shine - when minerals have practically no shine, found in finely dispersed earthy mineral aggregates (kaolinite, limonite, glauconite). In conclusion, we note that when we talk about optical properties, we always mean what is visible to the eye, i.e. a certain frequency of radiation and a certain - visible - size of the object. But there are other frequencies and other sizes of objects. Ultraviolet and infrared radiation are traditionally analyzed together with visible radiation, and as we move away from the rainbow and enter the region of X-ray radiation and millimeter radio waves, when substances begin to behave significantly differently, we find ourselves in another, no less interesting section of the textbook. As for the size of an object, substances consist of atoms and molecules, and we have the right to ask how a photon interacts with a single atom or molecule, and how interaction with a single atom results in interaction with a molecule and matter. Photons can either be absorbed or scattered, and scattering is significant at significantly higher energies (X-rays, gamma radiation). Absorption can occur through the transfer of energy either to electrons in an atom, or vibrations of atoms in a molecule, or to electron gas in conductors. And then - either the radiation of the atom that absorbed the photon (luminescence, reflection by dielectrics), or the vibrations of the electron gas and its radiation (reflection of light by metals), or the transition to heat (this is why metals reflect differently, and cats love to bask in the sun).
