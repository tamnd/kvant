And in another situation it is important to achieve extremely low absorption - this is also long-distance communication, only not through the air, but through glass. Optical fiber for transmitting a signal over a long distance is made of $SiO_{2}$, i.e. from quartz. The main parameter of optical fiber is the ability to transmit a signal with low distortion over a long distance. Distortion and loss depend on material, design and use. Specifically, the material requires low losses (i.e. absorption and dispersion) and dispersion. To reduce losses, you need to use the range of 1.2–1.7 μm and reduce impurities of Fe, Cr, Cu, Co, V, Mo, and OH groups. It was precisely the clarification of the causes of losses and the production of quartz with a low content of these impurities that made it possible to begin to actively use light guides. Since then, losses have been reduced by about a hundred times; today the record is 0.15 dB/km, and this is almost the theoretical limit. (You can look up the measurement of decibels per kilometer on the Internet.) The limit is “Rayleigh scattering,” the same thing that makes the sky blue, it gives 0.13 dB/km. But an attenuation of 0.13–0.15 dB/km means that over a kilometer piece of cable the signal loses 3–3.5% of its power. To make a light guide, you need a way to control the refractive index. And here we are lucky - there are impurities that reduce it (fluorine F, boron B) and increase it (aluminum Al, germanium Ge, phosphorus P), but do not cause absorption. Therefore, for the visible range, there is an optical fiber made from “ordinary” glasses: sodium-calcium-silicon $( Na_{2} O–CaO–SiO_{2} )$ or sodium-boron-silicon $( Na_{2} O–B_{2} O_{3} –SiO_{2} ).$ In the IR range, fluoride (for example, $ZrF_{4} –BaF_{2} )$ and chalcogenide $( As_{2} S_{3}, As_{2} Se_{3} )$ glasses, as well as crystalline materials based on TlCl, AgCl) are used.
