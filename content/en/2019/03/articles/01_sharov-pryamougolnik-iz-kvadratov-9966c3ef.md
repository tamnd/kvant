Rectangle of squares

F. SHAROV

IN EARLY TIMES, PEOPLE WERE DOING cutting tasks. Is it possible to cut a certain shape into some others (for example, into squares)? Is it possible to create another given figure (for example, a regular triangle) from a certain set of figures? In this article we will prove one of the classic results in this field of mathematics - Dehn's theorem. Theorem 1 (M. Dehn, 1903). If a rectangle is cut into squares (not necessarily equal), then the ratio of the lengths of its sides is rational. Dehn's theorem for good numbers

First, we prove the theorem for the special case when the sides of the rectangle being cut and the sides of all squares are good numbers. First of all, let's look at an example: rectangle 1 ¥ 2. Let's solve the following problem. Problem 1. Can a 1 ¥ 2 rectangle be cut into squares with rational sides? What about sides that are either rational or of the form b 2? What about sides that are arbitrary good numbers? $Решение.^{1}$ The answer to the first two questions is: no. The solution to the third question is given at the end of the journal. Let's answer the first question. Suppose that the rectangle 1 ¥ 2 is cut into squares with rational sides. Consider a side equal to 2. The sum of the sides of the squares adjacent to it must be equal to 2. The sum of rational numbers cannot be equal to the irrational number, therefore, such a cutting is impossible. Let's answer the second question. Suppose that the rectangle 1 ¥ 2 is cut into squares with sides of the form a or b 2. Its area is 2. The areas of the squares are either $a^{2}$ or $2b^{2}$.

Proof of Theorem 1. Let the rectangle $s_{0}$ ¥ $t_{0}$ be cut into squares, and $s_{0}$ and $t_{0}$ are incommensurable. By definition, its y-area is equal to y. This number is negative for y < 0. At the same time, the y-area of ​​any square is non-negative by Lemma 3. We have a contradiction with Lemma 4: the sum of non-negative numbers cannot equal negative. In conclusion, y-area is not just an Olympic trick. In mathematics there is an important concept of measure. The measures are, for example, the length of segments on a straight line; area (ordinary, not y-area) of figures on a plane; volume of bodies in space. Of the two main properties of a measure—additivity and nonnegativity—our y-area has only the first. If the property of non-negativity is not required, instead of the word "measure" is used

the term "charge". An example of a charge is the y-area.
