The enormous significance of Mourou and Strickland’s invention has now become clear, crowned with the 2018 Nobel Prize “for the method of generating high-intensity ultrashort optical pulses.” More precisely, it was only half of the Nobel Prize. The other half was awarded to Arthur Ashkin "for the invention of optical tweezers and their application in biological systems." It was the second prize awarded for the unusual use of light pressure exerted by a laser beam. After the creation of lasers, Ashkin began to work on the impact of a laser beam on microscopic objects where high radiation power is not needed. He soon discovered a phenomenon that was unusual at first glance - the pressure of light shifted microparticles not in the direction of the beam, but in a lateral direction towards the middle of this beam. No matter how narrow the laser beam may be from a macroscopic point of view, diffraction necessarily leads to a redistribution of the radiation intensity across the beam width. Something like a central maximum is formed along its axis, and the intensity at the edges of the beam is several tens of times less. This is a bit like the pattern of light diffraction by a slit. When approaching, they can be considered electric dipoles; a lateral (gradient) force acts, moving them towards the axis of the beam. It presses the particles towards an axis where they are in an equilibrium position. This position is especially stable when the laser beam in this place is narrowed and focused by a strong lens. The ray segment is no longer represented as a cylinder, but as a truncated cone. In this case, the resultant of the gradient forces has an axial direction and can balance the light pressure exerted by the beam on the surface of the microobject. An ideal laser trap arises with a stable position of the object at one point. With the very first low-power lasers at his disposal, Ashkin studied the behavior of objects where traps could easily be implemented. These were micron-sized plastic balls. As laser power increased, he studied the behavior of increasingly smaller particles. At first, he did not think at all about studying biological objects. Chance helped me get to them. Indoor flowers grew in the laboratory, the leaves of which were infected with a mosaic virus. It often affects leaves in gardens near Moscow, especially the leaves of black currants and phlox. A coating of tiny powder of various colors appears on the leaves. Ashkin studied this powder under a microscope and was convinced that the virus balls were only a few nanometers in size. These may have been the smallest microobjects that Ashkin could have had at this time. But they were so small that microscopes could not detect their movement in the laser beam. At some point in time, the viruses began to be attacked by larger biological objects - bacteria that fed on these viruses. It was their movement that Ashkin began to study. True, for this he had to replace the laser with a green wavelength with an infrared laser.
