The following more general fact encourages such research. Let's discard any conditions on the vertices and only require that all the faces of the polyhedron be regular polygons - anyway, except for prisms and antiprisms, we will get a finite number of different polyhedra! Such polyhedra are called regular-faced. A complete classification of regular-faced polyhedra is given in the article by V.A. Zalgaller “Convex polyhedra with regular faces” (Notes of scientific seminars LOMI, No. 2, 1967, p. 5–221). Zalgaller's article is available at http://mi.mathnet.ru/znsl1408 - despite its large volume and complexity, we strongly advise the reader to read it. The family of regular polyhedra includes the five Platonic solids, 13 Archimedean solids plus the pseudorhombocubooctahedron, an endless series of prisms and antiprisms, and 91 more polyhedra. These last polyhedra are called Johnson solids, named after the author of the article in which they were all first listed: N.W. Johnson. Convex polyhedra with regular faces (Canadian Journal of Mathematics, vol. 18, 1966, p. 169–200, https://doi.org/ 10.4153/CJM-1966-021-8). The pseudorhombocubooctahedron also belongs to the Johnson solids, if it is not classified as Archimedean; then there are 92 Johnson bodies. In his work, N. Johnson gives a list of all Johnson bodies, but does not prove that there are no others (this was done in the above-mentioned article by V.A. Zalgaller). We are interested in polyhedra that are semi-regular in the weak sense but are not such in the strong sense. It is clear that they are all Johnson bodies. This means that it is enough for us to go through all the Johnson bodies and select among them those in which the same polyhedra converge at each vertex. It is especially convenient to do this according to Johnson’s article, where for each polyhedron the types of its vertices are indicated, i.e. which polygons and in what quantity converge at each vertex.

Our search yields 6 new regular-faceted polyhedra, in which the same polygons converge at each vertex, but in a different order. Interestingly, they are all obtained from Archimedean polyhedra using the “rotating the cap” operation described at the beginning of the article. Let us list these six polyhedra. Firstly, by rotating the dome of the rhombicosidodecahedron, as O. Lazutchenko suggested to us, you can get as many as four polyhedra (Johnson has numbers 72–75). There were several polyhedra, because you can rotate not one, but two or even three domes. You just need to make sure that these domes do not have common edges: when turning, the dome “breaks” all the domes adjacent to it. If you rotate one dome, you get a twisted rhombicosidodecahedron (Fig.3). The two domes can be rotated in two ways - either one opposite the other, and then you get a twice oppositely twisted rhombicosidodecahedron (Fig.4); or two non-opposite ones, which gives a doubly obliquely twisted rhombicosidodecahedron (Fig.5). Finally, three non-overlapping domes can only be selected in one way; by rotating them, we get a thrice-twisted rhombicosidodecahedron (Fig.6). According to Zalgaller, the twisted versions of the rhombicosidodecahedron are designated $0_{6} 0_{14} 0_{6}$ or $0_{6} 0_{13} 2 0_{6} ; 0_{6} 0_{14} 0_{6} ; 20_{6} 0_{13} 0_{6} ; 30_{6} 0_{13}$.
