Donna Strickland was born in 1959 in Canada and became a physicist after graduating from McNester University in Canada. She wanted to work on high-power lasers and went to the USA, to the University of Michigan, to the laboratory of Gerard Mourou, where she became his graduate student. Mourou entrusted her with the most important area of ​​experimental work on chirping laser pulses, and already in 1985, in the second year of her graduate studies, a joint article by Mourou and Strickland was published, which became fundamental for all laser scientists and provided the basis for awarding the 2018 Nobel Prize to them, the authors of this work. Strickland defended her dissertation on this topic in 1989. Thus, she set a kind of “Nobel record” for awarding a prize for work done in graduate school. Donna Strickland continued her successful work on increasing the power of pulsed lasers at the University of Waterloo in Canada, where she became president of the Optical Society. At the time of the award, her position at the university corresponded to the position of associate professor (associate professor). But the very next day she became a full professor. “I am happy and honored,” she said, “to be one of the three great women.” Unusually youthful and slender, at the ceremony to receive the Nobel Prize signs, presented by the Swedish king Carl XIV Gustav, she wore a strict, but very elegant red dress with one strand of pearls. The delegation of teachers and students from her home university, who arrived in Stockholm and was invited to the awards ceremony, warmly welcomed their Nobel laureate, who became the pride of both their university and all of Canada. Donna Strickland became Canada's tenth Nobel laureate and the second Canadian physicist laureate.
