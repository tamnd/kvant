But even greater fame for magnetic resonance spectroscopy came from the use of the so-called gradient magnetic field. The first NMR and EPR researchers sought to create the most uniform magnetic field: after all, if the field strength differs in different parts of the object being studied, then the nuclei or electrons located in them will find themselves in different conditions and give signals at different frequencies. As a result, the narrow resonant line will be “smeared out” and distort its shape or become completely invisible. But if you create a non-uniform magnetic field that does not change at random, but, for example, uniformly and only in one direction (i.e., create a magnetic field that has a gradient of magnitude

ny strengths), then it is possible to distinguish signals from identical nuclei located in different parts of the sample under study along the field gradient. In other words, by seeing how much the resonance line of a nucleus has shifted, you can determine where this nucleus is located. This means that you can build a spatial picture of the object under study! By changing the direction of the field gradient, you can obtain a full-fledged three-dimensional image, and an image of its “insides” without violating the integrity of the object, i.e. without any incisions, insertion of probes, etc. This method is widely known as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), or NMR imaging, and has recently become one of the main important diagnostic methods in medicine. ESR tomography is a less common method, but is also used in medical research.
