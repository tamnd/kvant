...and quantum physics

Of course, Zavoisky was not the only one thinking about this task. Here it is worth recalling another direction in the development of physics. The atomic model proposed by Rutherford (Fig.1,b) gave rise not only to nuclear physics, but also to a new stage in the development of quantum physics. According to this model, the electrons in an atom revolve around a small central nucleus, like planets around the Sun (hence the name of the model - planetary). But unlike planets, electrons are charged particles. And a charged particle, in any motion other than uniform rectilinear motion, must generate electromagnetic radiation, as stated by the laws of classical electrodynamics, i.e. must radiate, which means lose energy. What happens to a planet or, it’s easier for us to imagine, to a satellite revolving around the planet and losing energy, for example, due to friction with its atmosphere? The satellite gradually descends and eventually falls to the surface of the planet. If the planet loses energy, it would have to fall into the Sun, and the electron would fall onto the nucleus. Why doesn't he fall? Two years later, in 1913, Niels Bohr suggested that electrons in atoms can only be in certain states, moving in so-called stationary orbits without any radiation. And they emit only when moving from one orbit to another. In other words, the energy of an electron, or more precisely, its angular momentum, can only take on a number of specific discrete values, i.e. quantized. Let us recall that in classical mechanics angular momentum is a measure of the rotational motion of a body, the greater the further its points are from the axis of rotation and the greater the tangential velocity they have.
