Compared to the nucleus, the electron is a much stronger magnet - its magnetic moment is thousands of times greater than that of the nucleus. And it was much easier to observe how the magnetic moment of an electron rotates in a weak magnetic field (while absorbing the energy of radio waves). In 1944, Zavoisky was able to detect resonant absorption of radio emission in paramagnetic crystals placed in a magnetic field (Fig.7). Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) was discovered.

Discovery Relay

Two years later, two American physicists, Edward Purcell and Felix Bloch, independently discovered the absorption of radio wave energy by hydrogen nuclei (NMR) in water and paraffin. These discoveries were immediately appreciated by physicists in Europe and the USA (in the USSR too, but with about a ten-year delay). A wave of research has begun in this area. One after another, more and more new types of magnetic resonances were discovered (absorption of radio emission by nuclei with a quadrupole electric moment, ferromagnetic and antiferromagnetic substances, etc.), more and more of their properties (for example, the hyperfine structure of EPR spectra due to magnetic interaction).

Altshuler theoretically predicted acoustic paramagnetic resonance - the resonant absorption of sound by a paramagnetic substance placed in a magnetic field. Perhaps one of the most important for NMR applications in chemistry (and beyond) was the discovery of the so-called chemical shift. The atomic nucleus “feels” the location of neighboring electrons in the molecule; as a result, the frequency at which it gives an NMR signal will be slightly different for two identical nuclei belonging to different molecules or even different atomic groups within the same molecule. This means that, having obtained the NMR spectrum of a substance, it is possible to determine which groups are part of its molecules, how many there are, and even how they are located relative to each other - i.e. determine the chemical structure of a molecule! Therefore, NMR has already become one of the most important analytical methods in chemistry since the late 1950s. It was simply irreplaceable in the study of complex organic molecules, and over time it became effectively used to study the spatial structure of proteins, along with the method of X-ray diffraction analysis (Fig.8; performed using a database of 3-dimensional protein structures).
