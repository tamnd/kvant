Hall's theorem

The following problem has become widely known in the popular mathematical literature. Problem about weddings. There are many boys, each of whom knows some girls. Under what conditions is it possible to marry all the young men at the same time so that each of them marries a girl he knows? The answer is: the problem is solvable if and only if any k boys from a given set are collectively acquainted with at least k girls. Note that the need here is obvious. Indeed, if for some k young men the number of potential brides is less than k, then these k young men will not be able to get married at the same time. Sufficiency is not so easy to prove! In order to formulate the problem of weddings in mathematical language, we present some definitions from graph theory [1]. The vertices of a graph are adjacent if they are connected by an edge. Edges of a graph are called adjacent if they have a common vertex, and non-adjacent otherwise. Pairwise nonadjacent edges of the graph form a matching. Sufficient condition

In this article we will talk about one (simple, but little-known) sufficient condition for the existence of a perfect matching in a bipartite graph. Theorem 2. Let in a non-empty (i.e., having at least one edge) bipartite graph $G V_{1}, V_{2}$ the degree of any vertex from part $V_{1}$ be no less than the degree of any vertex from part $V_{2}$. Then there is a perfect matching from $V_{1}$ to $V_{2}$ in this graph. Proof. Let q be a number that is not greater than the degree of any vertex in $V_{1}$ and not less than the degree of any vertex in $V_{2}$. Let us take arbitrary k vertices of the first part $b_{1}, b_{2}$, … $, b_{k}$ and adjacent vertices $g_{1}, g_{2}$, … $, g_{l}$ of the second part. Consider the subgraph G c of the original graph generated by these k + l vertices (G c includes the indicated vertices and all the edges between them that are present in the original graph). It is clear that in the graph G c the degree of any vertex from the first part is at least q, and from the second part no more than q. A graph is called regular if the degrees of all its vertices are equal. We call a bipartite graph semiregular if in each partition the degrees of all its vertices are equal to each other. Let us extract the following corollaries from Theorem 2. Corollary 1. In any non-empty regular bipartite graph there is a perfect matching. Corollary 2. In a nonempty semiregular bipartite graph $G V_{1}, V_{2}$, a perfect matching exists if and only if there are no more vertices in the partition $V_{1}$ than in $V_{2}$. Proof. The need is obvious. Adequacy. Let the degrees of the vertices in $V_{1}$ and $V_{2}$ be a and b respectively. By counting the number of edges in a bipartite graph in two ways, we get $a V_{1} b V_{2}$. Taking into account inequality $V_{1} d V_{2}$, we obtain that a t b. It remains to use Theorem 2.

Problems about magicians

There are a number of problems that can be easily solved using Theorem 2 and its corollaries. The plot of these tasks is as follows. The audience presents the magician's assistant with some randomly selected object (for example, several playing cards), after which the assistant somehow changes this object according to his choice. Next, the magician uses the new object to restore the original one. Let us construct a bipartite graph $G V_{1}, V_{2}$ as follows. Let the vertices from $V_{1}$ be all possible configurations that the audience can present to the magician’s assistant, and the vertices from $V_{2}$ be the configurations that the assistant can receive. If configuration b $V_{2}$ can be obtained from configuration a $V_{1}$, then the graph must contain an edge ab; there are no other edges in the graph. If in a given graph there is a perfect matching from $V_{1}$ to $V_{2}$, then the magician and his assistant, using it, will be able to perform the trick: the magician, based on configuration b, will uniquely determine configuration a.

Task 4. The spectator writes n numbers on the board from left to right. The magician's assistant covers one of the numbers with a red card, another with a blue card, and another with a green card. After this, the magician enters and calls the closed numbers. For what smallest n is such a trick possible? Answer: for n = 12. Solution. Let $V_{1}$ and $V_{2}$ be the sets of those “pictures” that can be seen by the assistant and the magician, respectively. Consider a bipartite graph $G V_{1}, V_{2}$, the edges of which describe possible transitions from pictures from $V_{1}$ to pictures from $V_{2}$. The graph is semiregular, $V_{1} 10^{n}, V_{2} n n 1 n 2$ • $10^{n} 3$. By Corollary 2, for a perfect matching from $V_{1}$ to $V_{2}$ to exist, it is necessary and sufficient that the share $V_{2}$ has no fewer vertices than $V_{1}$. Hence $n n 1 n 2 t t 10^{3}$, which is equivalent to the inequality n t 12. Thus, the smallest value of n is 12. We will show how to perform the trick practically with n t 12. We number the places where the first 12 digits are written from left to right: 1, 2, ..., 9, 0, 11, 12 (we do not number the remaining places, if any!). Let S be the sum of all digits recorded by the viewer. We will act for the assistant as follows. Let's cover with a red card the place whose number is the last digit of the number S, after which we'll mentally delete this place, and with a blue card we'll cover the place with a number equal to the digit that is hidden under the red card. Then, with a green card, we will cover the place whose number (calculated after “removing” the numbers under the red and blue cards) is hidden under the blue card. Now the magician will understand by the position of the green card which number is under the blue one, and by the position of the blue card - which number is under the red one. All numbers, except the one under the green card, are already known to the magician, and the position of the red card indicates the last digit of the sum of all the numbers written down by the spectator. Therefore, the magician will be able to determine the number under the green card.
