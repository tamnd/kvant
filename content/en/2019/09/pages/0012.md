If, as a result of geographical discoveries, the equator was clearly fixed, then until the end of the 19th century, the prime meridians, from which longitudes are measured, were different in different countries. Marinus of Tire, and after him Claudius Ptolemy and many geographers until the 19th century, used the Ferro meridian, passing through the Canary island of the same name, which was considered the westernmost point of the Old World. The Paris meridian passed through the center of Paris, and its markers can still be found on the streets. In Russia, the countdown started from the Pulkovo Meriadian, which passed through the Pulkovo Observatory near St. Petersburg. In Spain there were several different meridians, and the conflict that arose lies at the heart of A. To avoid such situations, at the end of the 19th century, a single prime meridian was adopted - Greenwich, passing through the Greenwich Observatory near London. In the mid-16th century, Mercator constructed a map that preserves angles. It is still widely used. On it, the horizontal $координата^{x}$ is also proportional to the longitude $M - M_{0}$. The equidistant map of Marin of Tire and the Mercator map are cylindrical, in which parallels and meridians turn into straight lines and are perpendicular to each other everywhere. The maps known from school textbooks are more complex and are pseudocylindrical. They take into account the fact that, to a more accurate approximation, the Earth's surface is an ellipsoid, and although the scale is always indicated on them, it only indicates how much the size of the ellipsoid is scaled before applying one or another map projection to it. In any case, measuring the lengths of lines on the earth's surface using maps can only be approximately, and good accuracy can only be achieved within small areas, as the examples we have given show. The strict mathematical criterion that a portion of a surface can be mapped onto a plane so that the lengths of all lines and the areas of all areas are preserved is due to Gauss. It consists in the fact that on a surface the Gaussian curvature K as a function of a point on the surface must vanish everywhere. This follows from Gauss's more general result on curvature, which he called "Theorema Egregium", which is Latin for "remarkable theorem".
