This projection and the corresponding Gauss-Kruger coordinates are used by military topographers. Gaussian curvature is quite difficult to calculate, although it can be immediately found for surfaces that are obtained from a sheet of paper by rolling it into a cylinder or cone or any other deformation in which the sheet does not tear: since they unfold into a flat sheet, their Gaussian curvature is zero everywhere. For a sphere of radius R, we will give another explanation for why any portion of it, no matter how small, does not map exactly onto a flat area. On a plane, the shortest line between any pair of distinct points is a straight line segment, and points that are distant from a given point X by a distance r form a circle of radius r with a center at point X. Let's see how the shortest lines on a sphere are constructed. You can try to establish this experimentally by fixing one end of the thread to the pole of the globe and, pulling the thread along its surface, consider all possible positions of its ends. If the length of the thread is short enough, then these will be exactly parallel, and the thread will run along the meridian. Mathematically, this means that points located at a distance r < S R from the pole, where R is the radius of the globe, form parallels - analogues of circles of radius r, and the shortest lines are sections of meridians, i.e. lines that are obtained when a spherical globe intersects a plane passing through its center and pole. We can rotate the sphere around its center so that the distances are preserved, and any given point on the sphere will go to any other given point. Therefore, while maintaining the distances, you can convert any point to a pole and apply the previous reasoning to it. Lines - great circles that are obtained when a sphere intersects with a plane passing through its center - are called geodesics (on a sphere).

This is a general concept that generalizes the concept of lines to arbitrary metric spaces with two important differences that appear for some spaces and, in particular, for the sphere. First, sections of geodetic lines have the shortest length among all the curves connecting their ends, but only if the ends are close enough to each other. For example, consider a great circle on a sphere: if the angular length of the arc of a geodesic segment is not greater than S, then it has the shortest length among all the lines connecting its ends, and if it is greater than S, then the shortest is another, complementary to it, section of the great circle. Secondly, for some pairs of points, the shortest geodesic segment connecting them is not unique: for example, opposite points of a sphere are connected by an infinite number of shortest geodesics of length $S^{R}$.
