D.I.MENDELEEV AND THE PERIODIC SYSTEM OF ELEMENTS

Another triumph of the periodic table was the discovery of the 118th element, which took its place in the group of noble gases. And he received a name that ends not in “-iy,” like all other transuranium elements, but in “-on,” as befits all elements of the eighth group, except helium. This name – “Oganesson” – was given in 2018 by an international committee in honor of the head of the work in Dubna, Academician of the Russian Academy of Sciences Yu.Ts. Oganesyan. This is the second time an element has been named in honor of a living scientist (the first was the American physicist Glenn Seaborg, who identified neptunium and plutonium in 1941). Among the names of transuranic elements, 2 are astronomical, 9 are geographical, and 15 are named after scientists. Thus, the 99th element is einsteinium, the 100th is fermium, and the 101st element deservedly bears the name “mendelevium”. There is no doubt about the role of the periodic table in the discovery of the elements radium and polonium, for which Marie Sklodowska-Curie received a second Nobel Prize in 1911, this time in chemistry. But the greatest triumph of the periodic system is its theoretical justification, made in 1926 by Wolfgang Pauli on the basis of the then newly created matrix representation of quantum mechanics. This justification became one of the first proofs of the validity of quantum mechanics, which, according to many scientists, is the greatest achievement of science in the twentieth century.

Dmitry Ivanovich Mendeleev was born on January 27 (February 8), 1834 in Tobolsk in the family of the director of the gymnasium and trustee of public schools of the Tobolsk province Ivan Pavlovich Mendeleev and Maria Dmitrievna Mendeleeva, née Koriilieva. There were 14 children in the family, but eight of them died in infancy. Dmitry was the youngest son, “the last one,” as he later called himself. Ivan Pavlovich went blind soon after the birth of his youngest son and, although Moscow surgeons were able to partially restore his vision, he did not return to work.

She independently completed the full gymnasium course and played a special role in the life of the family, in fact becoming the main family teacher. Maria Dmitrievna quickly realized that her youngest son had outstanding abilities, although in the gymnasium he was fond of mathematics and physics, and had no interest in humanitarian subjects. The boy's abilities and hard work allowed him to complete his gymnasium course at the age of 15. A year later, Maria Dmitrievna sold her property and went with her family, first to Moscow, and then to St. Petersburg, where at that time the level of natural science education was quite high, which her son strove for with all his soul. She manages to ensure that her son enters college early (due to his age), and a year later she dies. In the preface to one of the first scientific works, Dmitry Mendeleev writes: “This study is dedicated to the memory of the mother of her last child. She was able to raise him with her work and love, raising him by example, and in order to give a native of Siberia to science, she spent her last means and strength. Dying, she bequeathed: to avoid self-delusion, to insist in work and not in words, to patiently explain scientific truth, because she understood how, with the help of science, without violence, lovingly, but firmly, prejudices and errors are eliminated and freedom of further development, the common good and internal well-being are achieved. I consider my mother’s covenants sacred.” At the age of 21, Mendeleev graduated from the Faculty of Physics and Mathematics of the Main Pedagogical Institute in St. Petersburg with a gold medal and the title of “senior teacher.” For two years he worked first in Simferopol, and then in Odessa at the gymnasium at the Richelieu Lyceum as a teacher of physics, mathematics and natural sciences. During this time, he prepared and brilliantly defended his master’s thesis on chemical engineering at St. Petersburg University.
