A new time was then dawning in Russia - the era of the abolition of serfdom, the era of transformations and the organization of regional (zemstvo) administration. The government of Alexander II understood the need for this to train enlightened management personnel, and therefore the development of education and science. A sharp increase in university funding allowed Mendeleev to become a scholarship recipient for a two-year internship at scientific institutions in Germany. Abroad, Mendeleev not only studied the latest achievements of chemical science and technology. He was able to obtain funds to create a laboratory in which he studied the physicochemical properties of gases and liquids, in particular the dependence of the boiling point of liquids on pressure and the properties of saturated steam. Mendeleev showed that above a certain temperature, at no pressure, there is no long process of liquid boiling and, accordingly, there is no phenomenon of gradual liquefaction of gas. At certain temperatures and pressures, a simultaneous condensation of gas or expansion of a liquid occurs. These parameters were subsequently called “critical”, and the state of the substance itself was called the “critical state”. It turned out that producing liquefied gas by compression is only possible at temperatures below critical. Mendeleev's discovery formed the basis for all future technologies for producing liquefied gases. During his internship in Germany, Mendeleev had just begun this work. Returning to Russia, he was unable to find a suitable location and funding to continue the liquefaction of gases. After all, he was formally (according to his dissertation) a chemist, but he had not yet had time to properly prove himself in chemical science. And Mendeleev decides to postpone his scientific studies for a while. Based on his extensive knowledge of chemistry, he created the then urgently needed textbook “Organic Chemistry”, and also translated and

And the academic Demidov Prize received for them provided some material well-being. This prize, by the way, exists under a slightly changed name in our time and is awarded for outstanding scientific achievements. Among the recent winners of this prize are such outstanding scientists as physicists Zh.I. Alferov and V.A. Rubakov, mathematicians L.D. Faddeev and B.V. Raushenbakh, historian V.L. The Demidov Prize allowed Mendeleev to travel around Europe with his young wife, Feozva Nikitichnaya Lesheva, his fellow Tobolsk resident, the stepdaughter of the famous Tobolsk resident (or “Tobolak”, as the residents of this city prefer) Pyotr Pavlovich Ershov, the official author of “The Little Humpbacked Horse.” Mendeleev made full use of his honeymoon to communicate with European chemists and study all the new products of chemical science. Upon returning from a European trip, Mendeleev received a position as a full-time associate professor of organic chemistry at St. Petersburg University and at the same time a professorship at the St. Petersburg Institute of Technology. Two years after defending his doctoral dissertation, Mendeleev became a professor at St. Petersburg University in the department of technical chemistry. At this time, there was an urgent need to create a new textbook on inorganic chemistry, which would reflect the latest achievements of the rapidly developing chemical science. This idea captured Mendeleev. But in what order should the descriptions and chemical properties of the elements be presented? After all, they are so diverse. Having thoroughly studied the properties of all the then known elements, Mendeleev compiled a card index and all the time mentally shuffled this “deck”, trying to find patterns in the arrangement of elements.
