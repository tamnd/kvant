D.I.MENDELEEV AND THE PERIODIC SYSTEM OF ELEMENTS

knowledge of atomic weights and the presence of several possible valences for many elements. The periodicity in rows and columns was broken. But the very idea of ​​​​searching for periodicity when arranging elements in rows and columns was sound and promising. In Meyer’s homeland, in the town of Farel in Lower Saxony, near the mouth of the Elbe, there is a memorial with three sculptural portraits of Meyer, Mendeleev and Cannizzaro. The English chemist D. Newlands also noticed a certain pattern in the list of elements. He called this pattern the “rule of octaves.” At the beginning of Cannizzaro's list, valences were repeated every six elements, with the 2nd, 9th, and 16th elements having a valence of 1, and the 3rd, 10th, and 16th elements having a valence of 2. This was like a musical octave, with six other fundamental tones located between the C tones. True, after calcium, which occupied 17th place on the list, this rule lost its binding nature. Published in 1865, Newlands' work did not arouse interest among chemists and was even ridiculed at a meeting of the Chemical Society of London. Thus, four people were at the origins of the creation of the periodic system, but a Russian chemist is recognized as the creator of the periodic system of elements. So what did D.I. Mendeleev do? First of all, he was guided by a list not of 28 elements (Meyer) and not of 40 elements (Cannizzaro), but of 67 elements, boldly leaving in this list places for three elements, completely unknown at that time, which, in his opinion, must be located in certain places in the table according to their possible valence and possible atomic weight. This means that for the author himself, periodicity was already established by law, although he entitled his table “experience.” This reflected Mendeleev’s methodological (philosophical) conviction in the existence of cyclicity in the global laws of nature.

⟦column⟧

"Experience in a System of Elements" (1869)

In the first version of his table, Mendeleev, unlike Meyer, arranged elements with the same valence not vertically (in columns), but horizontally (in rows). This did not change the essence of the matter, and already in the second version of the table in 1870, he rotated the table by 90 degrees, and it took on a more familiar form to us. The column numbers began to correspond to the “main” valences of the element (from the first to the seventh) and were called groups, and the rows were called periods, which contained either 7 or 17 elements. The difference from modern values ​​- 8 groups (or 18, as is customary in the most modern versions of the periodic system) and 8 or 18 elements per period - is due to the fact that at that time the elements that are called noble today were not yet known at all.
