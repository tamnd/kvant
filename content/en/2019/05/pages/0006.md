And subsequently the discovery of other noble gases took place. It was very important that the periodic table predicted the existence of several then unknown chemical elements, which Mendeleev called eka-aluminum, eka-silicon and eka-boron. 6 years after Mendeleev’s work, the French chemist Lecoq de Boisbaudran discovered an element called gallium. And although Mendeleev’s interests at this time had already shifted to other areas of science, he continued to follow scientific publications on chemistry. Having read about the discovery of gallium, he immediately recognized it as his predicted eka-aluminum. Mendeleev's message about this in a letter to a French chemist created a real sensation among scientists. Moreover, Mendeleev’s predictions about the density and atomic weight of this element turned out to be even more accurate than the initially published experimental data. In dozens of European laboratories, chemists began feverishly searching for the remaining elements predicted by Mendeleev and testing known elements for questionable atomic weights and chemical properties. And a year later, the Swedish chemist Lars Nilsson discovered an element that fully corresponded to eca-boron described by Mendeleev. He named it scandium in honor of his homeland. During Mendeleev's lifetime, the element germanium (eka-silicon) was discovered and the discovery of the family of noble gases began. The table of the periodic system published by Mendeleev in the next edition of his “Fundamentals of Chemistry” in 1905 was much more similar to the modern one. This table finally received its modern form after the work on the analysis of the X-ray spectra of elements by Henry Moseley (1913). The Periodic Table of the Elements (1905) determines the position of the element in a particular group. Spectra associated with electronic transitions in the inner shells (K-, L- and M-electron shells) also play an important role. Much more sharply than in optical spectra, the number of an element in the periodic table appears in X-ray spectra. Today we know that this is the charge number of the atomic nucleus of an element. This allowed Moseley to refine the location of many rare earth elements in the periodic table and, in turn, predict the discovery of a number of then unknown elements. And in our time, when 26 transuranium elements have already been discovered at powerful accelerators in several world scientific centers (and primarily at the Joint Institute for Nuclear Research in Dubna near Moscow), each of them occupies its assigned “cell” in the periodic table according to the atomic weight and structure of electron shells, determined from X-ray spectra.
