Mendeleev himself created this legend, picturesquely describing how one day after a sleepless night, half asleep, the only possible arrangement of elements appeared to him and he immediately wrote it down on the first piece of paper he came across. Psychologists believe that it was not a dream, but an intermediate state between sleep and wakefulness, in which the brain works with particular activity. Mendeleev added that he did not see anything special in this, since he continuously thought about it for a long time before deciding on the final version of the table, where there were vacant places and where he boldly changed the then known atomic weights of some elements so that they would occupy the corresponding places in the rows and columns of the table. So, for example, despite the fact that the atomic weight of the element uranium was then considered equal to only 60 conventional units, Mendeleev “assigns” to uranium an atomic weight value 4 times greater (as it actually turned out to be) and places uranium in the very place of the table where it should be. Legal triumph and world recognition, especially after the discovery of the elements he predicted, did not prevent Mendeleev from continuing to work actively. But his scientific interests shifted to other areas. He again began studying the behavior of gases at various pressures and, for not very high pressures, rethought the law discovered in 1834 by the French engineer and physicist Paul Clapeyron and introduced the concept of the universal gas constant. Since then, this law has been named Mendeleev–Clapeyron and is called the equation of state of an ideal gas. But Mendeleev did not rest on this and began to investigate deviations from this law. He introduced the concept of “real gases” and qualitatively described deviations in the behavior of these gases from “ideality.” Mendeleev’s great achievements in physical chemistry, chemical technology and related

Here are just a few of them: the creation of a safe method for producing one of the variants of smokeless (pyroxylin) gunpowder, which ensured its widespread use in the world (“Mendeleev’s” gunpowder); development of the theory of solutions, in particular, determination of the most optimal ratio of components in a mixture of various liquids; study of surface tension of liquids and proof of its disappearance in a critical state of matter; study of the composition of oil and proof of its both biogenic and abiogenic origin; substantiation of the importance of many components of oil as valuable chemical products and the development of methods for extracting these products from oil (the famous phrase: “burning oil is like burning a stove with banknotes”). To this impressive “chemical” list one can add a number of other interests and achievements of D.I. Mendeleev. For example, metrological research, leadership of the Russian “Chamber of Weights and Measures” created by him; meteorological research, study of the earth's atmosphere and solar corona; participation in the creation of the world's first icebreakers for Arctic exploration; writing 25 articles on problems of industrial ecology in the encyclopedic dictionary of Brockhaus and Efron. But one of Mendeleev’s hobbies stands out from the crowd. For full observations of the solar corona during a total solar eclipse in the summer of 1887, Mendeleev is developing a design for a stratospheric balloon, the like of which did not exist in the world at that time (with a diameter of 20 m and a volume of more than $3000 м^{2} ).$). He does this together with the inventor and aeronaut S.K. Dzhevetsky. A pilot-aeronaut was supposed to fly with Mendeleev. But when it turns out that unexpectedly In the rainy weather, the balloon cannot be lifted by two people, Mendeleev decides that he will fly alone, and after the necessary instructions on how to control the balloon, he rises into the air. Unfortunately, the sun remains at the altitude.
