Explanation. There are three squares in a pentagon. It is impossible to cut a pentagon into three convex parts, each containing one square. The point in the center figure cannot belong to any of the parts, since each of the three segments going from it to one of the points of the squares will intersect the contour of another square, i.e. none of these segments will belong to a convex figure.

Application

Five houses. Let us prove that on a plane it is impossible to connect five houses with each other by non-intersecting paths so that each house is connected to each by a separate path. Let's say we managed to do this after all. Let's remove all the tracks and return them sequentially. First, we add four paths connecting the four houses in a cycle, we will get a “quadrangle” of paths. Let us now connect pairs of opposite vertices of this quadrilateral (draw “diagonals”). One dia-part, the boundary of which will also be a triangle (let's call this part an “infinite” triangle). The fifth house will be in one of these triangles and can only be connected to the three vertices of that triangle. A contradiction - there will be one more house that cannot be connected to the fifth. Three houses and three wells. Let us prove that on a plane it is impossible to connect three houses with three wells by non-intersecting paths so that each house is connected to each well by a separate path. Let's say we managed to do this after all. Let's remove all the tracks and return them sequentially. First, we will go around all the houses and wells in a closed loop. Since the paths do not intersect, it will be a “hexagon” of paths, with houses and wells alternating in it (Fig. 7).
