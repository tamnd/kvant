Previously, solder was found in the form of much thicker rods, and with them this experiment was even more effective, but it only required more time. I had to attach a horizontal rod at the beginning of a conversation or lecture and draw attention to it an hour and a half later - the metal rod, almost as thick as a finger, was bent a couple of centimeters! A very thin rod of solder, about a millimeter in diameter, bends very quickly under its own weight. The length in this version, of course, can be quite small, about 10–15 cm. If you have a solder bar of medium thickness and a length of thirty centimeters at hand, then the plasticity of the metal can be illustrated quite simply and quickly - just hang the bar on a thread by the middle or insert it into the most primitive

ABCD prism embedded in the wall; a load acts on its free end (engraving from Galileo’s “Conversations”)

A beautiful engraving reproduced in Galileo's Discourses shows a rather thick beam fixed at one end in a stone wall, while a certain load acts with its weight on the other end. By the way, Galileo wrote this last book of his largely because the guards from the Inquisition allowed him to study this topic, and by that time he was completely orphaned and blind. So it is likely that Galileo himself did not see this engraving. Today we can change this experience and show students and schoolchildren its simple and effective version. To do this, take a horizontal stick of lead solder and attach it to one end. If the stick is thin, with a diameter slightly less than three millimeters and a length of approximately 20–30 cm, then it bends by itself under its own weight. No additional weight is needed for the free end of the stick to drop a few centimeters in a quarter of an hour. This makes a great impression on the audience. We simply observe with our own eyes the plastic deformation of metal.

A stick of lead solder at the beginning of the experiment...

...and at the end of the experiment, a plate with a hole. In a few minutes it will be clearly visible how both ends of the twig have descended. Members of a circle of young technicians could easily supplement this simple experimental setup with, say, an electric bell, or a regulator of the length of the free part of a hanging rod to compare the deformation time.
