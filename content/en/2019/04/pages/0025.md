How is a circle different from a square?

S.VOLCHENKOV

AT FIRST GLANCE THE QUESTION SEEMS strange. Everyone knows what a circle is and what a square is. And everyone knows that these are different geometric objects. But in this article we will talk not so much about geometry, but about combinatorial geometry, where geometric objects are considered from a combinatorial point of view - as elements of finite sets. We will analyze three problems in which circles and squares, due to their seemingly insignificant differences, give rise to different answers. Problem 1 about circles. There are 5 circles on the plane, each two of which have a common point. Is it true that there will always be 3 circles that have a common point? Answer: true. Solution. Let us denote the circles (and their centers) by the letters $O_{1}, O_{2}, O_{3}, O_{4}, O_{5}$, respectively. Let's connect the centers to each other with segments. Some two of the drawn segments must intersect. This is a known fact (even if you try to connect the dots with broken lines) - try to prove it yourself or read it in the Appendix at the end of the article. Let the segments $O_{1} O_{2}$ and $O_{3} O_{4}$ intersect at point X (Fig.1). It remains to prove that this lemma is true.
