How is a circle different from a square?

S.VOLCHENKOV

AT FIRST GLANCE THE QUESTION SEEMS strange. Everyone knows what a circle is and what a square is. And everyone knows that these are different geometric objects. But in this article we will talk not so much about geometry, but about combinatorial geometry, where geometric objects are considered from a combinatorial point of view - as elements of finite sets. We will analyze three problems in which circles and squares, due to their seemingly insignificant differences, give rise to different answers. Problem 1 about circles. There are 5 circles on the plane, each two of which have a common point. Is it true that there will always be 3 circles that have a common point? Answer: true. Solution. Let us denote the circles (and their centers) by the letters $O_{1}, O_{2}, O_{3}, O_{4}, O_{5}$, respectively. Let's connect the centers to each other with segments. Some two of the drawn segments must intersect. This is a known fact (even if you try to connect the dots with broken lines) - try to prove it yourself or read it in the Appendix at the end of the article. Let the segments $O_{1} O_{2}$ and $O_{3} O_{4}$ intersect at point X (Fig.1). It remains to prove that this lemma is true.

Now let's go back to problem 3 about circles. It turns out that the partition of the polygon we need can be formed not by perpendicular bisectors, but by the so-called radical axes of circles. The radical axis of two non-intersecting circles is the locus of points, the tangents of which, drawn to two given circles, have equal lengths. It is easy to show that the radical axis of two circles of any radius with divergent centers is a straight line (see Appendix). If the circles do not intersect, they will be entirely on opposite sides of the radical axis. Let us then divide the points of the polygon (lying outside the circles) into areas consisting of points, the tangents of which to one circle are shorter than to the others. Similarly, we obtain that each circle lies entirely in one region and this region is a convex polygon.

Application

Five houses. Let us prove that on a plane it is impossible to connect five houses with each other by non-intersecting paths so that each house is connected to each by a separate path. Let's say we managed to do this after all. Let's remove all the tracks and return them sequentially. First, we add four paths connecting the four houses in a cycle, we will get a “quadrangle” of paths. Let us now connect pairs of opposite vertices of this quadrilateral (draw “diagonals”). One dia-part, the boundary of which will also be a triangle (let's call this part an “infinite” triangle). The fifth house will be in one of these triangles and can only be connected to the three vertices of that triangle. A contradiction - there will be one more house that cannot be connected to the fifth. Three houses and three wells.
