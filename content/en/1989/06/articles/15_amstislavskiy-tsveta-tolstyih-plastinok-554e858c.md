Thick plate colors

Candidate of Pedagogical Sciences
Y. E. AMSTISLAVSKY

Who among you has not admired the rainbow play of colors in soap films, the variety of brightly colored stripes of intricate shapes in oil films on sea asphalt (or on water) or other similar manifestations of white color interference in thin films? Is it possible to observe such a picture in laboratory conditions? How to explain it?

In general, the interference of light is discussed in some detail in the school physics course in the tenth grade. (In particular, the origin of the colors of thin films is explained there.) Here, very little will be said about the interference itself, only what is necessary for understanding the content of the article.

Light interference is the superposition of two (or more) light waves, in which regularly intersecting areas of increased and decreased light intensity appear in space. The interference pattern will be stable if the superimposed waves are consistent, or, as they say, coherent: the oscillation frequencies are the same, and the phase difference remains constant over time.

Before the advent of lasers, coherent light beams could only be produced by splitting beams coming from the same source. Thus, in the case of films (or interlayers), the interference pattern is created by rays reflected by the two-dimensionality of the film. In monochromatic light the picture appears

As experience shows (and calculations confirm this), the picture will be clear and bright only if the film thickness does not exceed tens of a micrometer (which is hundreds of times less than the average thickness of a human fiber!).

However, one special case is known when interference fringes are formed when the interlayer is very thick. This phenomenon was discovered and studied by Newton (and described in his “Optics”, published in 1704), but until relatively recently it was of only historical interest. And only the rapid development in the most recent years of interference methods in diffuse light breathed new life into the phenomenon discovered by Newton.

What is the essence of this phenomenon? However... everything is in order. Let's start with the experiments, and then we'll try to explain them.

How to see the color of the top layers?

One of the experiments on interference in diffuse (scattered) light can be tested with a so-called dusty mirror. To carry out the experiments, very modest equipment is required: a 4.5 V flashlight battery, a 3.5 V light bulb, a common household mirror (preferably thin) about 1 dm² in size and a piece of telephone wire.

To turn a light bulb into a suitable light source for experiments, do the following. Solder two wires to the light bulb circuit; during the experiments you will connect their opposite ends to the battery electrodes. Cut out a small strip of black wrapping paper, roll it up into a small bag, put it on the light bulb from the base side and glue it so that a small black lampshade is formed.

When preparing a mirror for experiments, it is necessary to apply a scattering coating to its surface. The easiest way to do this is to use diluted milk. Mix one spoon of milk with 4-5 spoons of water, then pour 0.5-1 teaspoon of the solution onto a mirror lying on the table and add even distribution of liquid over the entire surface of the mirror. After this, leave the mirror to dry for a few minutes.

If it is possible to supply a mirror with a metal coating (such mirrors are available in the school physics classroom), then a thin “airy” mirror can be made. To do this, apply a scattering coating to one of the surfaces of photographic plastic (for example, 9x12 cm in size) or a piece of window glass, washed from the emulsion. Then, from common terdan paper to the size of the mirror, cut out a rectangular frame-spacer 0.5-1 cm wide, place this frame on the mirror and cover it with a glass plate so that the milk film faces the mirror surface. As a result, you will get an “air” mirror, the thickness of which corresponds to the thickness of the paper sheet (spacer), i.e. ~0.1 mm.

When conducting experiments, the mirror is placed horizontally. In the case of a relatively thick mirror (3-6 mm thick), it should be placed directly on the floor. A thinner mirror (1-2 mm) can be placed on a chair. If experiments are performed with a thin “air” mirror (~0.1 mm), it is more convenient to place the mirror on the table. The best observations are random in a dark room.

Test 1. Connect the light bulb to the battery, take the battery in one hand, and the wires near the light bulb in the other. Place the light bulb between the eye and the mirror, close to the eye, so that the light beam is directed towards the mirror, and the light bulb itself and the eye are approximately at the same normal to the mirror. You will see a system of concentric colored interference wheels covering the central white

Test 2. Place the light bulbs approximately in the middle between the eye and the mirror and observe the change in the picture when the eye moves to the side. Explore the qualitative influence of the magnitude of the eye displacement, as well as the initial position of the light bulb, on the configuration of the “wheels,” their width and the color of the central area of ​​the picture.

Test 3. Bring the light bulbs directly to your eye. Shifting it slightly in the transverse direction, you will see a system of almost straight and fairly wide colored interface stripes with an image of a light source in the middle of the central white stripe. Place the space in a direction perpendicular to the direction of displacement of the light bulb.

It is interesting to observe the reorientation and deformation of the stripes when the light source moves along the circle around the eyes, and then along one of its diameters. To this end, first place the bulbs near the bridge of the nose, then move it to the eyebrow and then along the circle with the center in the pupil area, and then along the diameter “through the pupil.”

What is a complex diffuser?

As is known, the reflection of light from the interface between two media can be specular or diffuse. All definitions relate the size of the interface irregularities to the wavelength of the incident light.

If the irregularities are small compared to the wavelength, specular reflection occurs. When the irregularities are commensurate with the light wavelength or exceed it and the arrangement of the irregularities is random, diffuse reflection occurs. In this case, the interface between the media scatters light in all possible directions. This surface is called a diffuser.

Now consider an optical system of two flat and parallel diffusers I' and I'' (Fig. 3). Let us assume that these diffusers, consisting of a variety of chaotic distributed unequal scattering centers, are identical, i.e., the distribution and properties of the scattering particles of the second diffuser ideally repeat the distribution and properties of the particles of the first. In other words, each scattering center of the second diffuser has its own twin on the first, and the diffusers are located so that their identical centers are located opposite each other. Such a system is combined with a complex diffuser.

But we would like to pay attention to the process of evaporation of liquid from the walls of the bubble. You are probably familiar with the popular saying “bubbles in puddles mean prolonged rain.” Precisely because during heavy rain or when it is cloudy, the relative air humidity is close to 100%, the evaporation of water from the walls of the bubble is insignificant. This circumstance “extends” the life of large bubbles, which are rich in good summer juice. When the air humidity is low, the water quickly evaporates. In dry air, the thin layer of the wall of the water bubble would disappear almost instantly. You can carry out quantitative measurements of the rate of moisture evaporation yourself if you have a room hygrometer. All you have to do is drink water in a saucer and watch how quickly it evaporates. The result will depend on the readings of a hygrometer installed away from the saucer of water.
