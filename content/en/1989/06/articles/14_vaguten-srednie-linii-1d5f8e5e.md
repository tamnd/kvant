Center lines

V. N. VAGUTEN

This article discusses a whole series of theorems that cover the entire school geometry course - from the 6th to the 10th grade. It starts with the theorem about the midlines of a triangle and leads to interesting properties of the tetrahedron and other polyhedra.

By cutting the tetrahedron along this plane (Fig. 6, b), we obtain a parallelogram KLMN, two sides of which are parallel to the edge AC and equal to AC/2, and the other two are parallel to the edge BD and are equal to BD/2.

The same parallelogram—the “middle section” of the tetrahedron—can be constructed for other pairs of opposite edges. Each of these three parallelograms has a common diagonal. In this case, the midpoints of the diagonals coincide. So we get an interesting corollary:

Theorem 6. Three segments connecting the midpoints of opposite edges of the tetrahedron intersect at one point and divide it in half (Fig. 7).

Tetrahedron, octahedron, parallelepiped, cube

At the beginning of the article, we looked at a triangle divided by the middle lines into four identical triangles (see Fig. 1). Let's try to do the same construction for an arbitrary triangular paramide (tetrahedron). Let's cut the tetrahedron into parts as follows: through the middles of the three edges coming out of each vertex, we'll make a flat cut (Fig. 11, a). Then four identical small tetrahedra will be cut off from the tetrahedron. By analogy with a triangle, one would think that there would be another similar tetrahedron left in the middle. But this is not so: the polyhedron that remains from the large tetrahedron after removing the four small ones will have six vertices and eight faces - it is called an octahedron (Fig. 11, b). It is convenient to test this using a piece of cheese in the shape of a tetrahedron. The resulting octahedron has a center of symmetry, since the midpoints of the opposite edges of the tetrahedron intersect at a common point and bisect it.

One interesting construction is connected with a triangle divided by the middle lines into four triangles: we can consider this figure as the development of some tetrahedron.

Let's imagine an acute triangle cut out of paper. Bend it along the middle lines so that the vertices coincide at one point, and bend the edges of the paper converging at this point, we get a tetrahedron in which all four faces are equal triangles; its opposite edges are equal (Fig. 12). Such a tetrahedron is called half-right. Each of the three “middle sections” of this tetrahedron—parallelograms whose sides are parallel to the opposite edge and equal to their halves—will be a rhombus. Therefore, the diagonals of these parallelograms - three segments connecting the midpoints of opposite edges - are perpendicular to each other. Among the numerous properties of a half-right tetrahedron, we note the following: the sum of the angles converging at each of its vertices is equal to $180^\circ$ (these angles are respectively equal to the angles of the original triangle). In particular, if we start with a net in the shape of an equilateral triangle, we get an equal tetrahedron with all edges equal.

We saw at the very beginning that each triangle can be considered as a triangle formed by the midlines of a larger triangle. With an analogy in space, there is no such construction. But it turns out that any tetrahedron can be considered as

Through each edge of the tetrahedron we draw a plane parallel to the opposite edge. After this, what remains is the “core” - a tetrahedron, the edges of which are the diagonals of the faces of the parallelepiped.
