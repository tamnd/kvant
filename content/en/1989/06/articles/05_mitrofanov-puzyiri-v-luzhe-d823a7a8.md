BUBBLES
IN THE PUDDLE

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
A. V. MITROFANOV

You, of course, have observed these “tenacious” bubbles. They appear on the surface of reservoirs or puddles during a rainfall, when the water seems to “boil” from frequently falling large drops. In some cases, such bubbles also occur after rain if the tree branches continue to drip. Bubbles are surprisingly stable; you can not only form them, but also watch them float through the water. The lifetime of bubbles is several seconds, or even several tens of seconds. Eventually they burst.

All bubbles look approximately the same. The bubble shell is similar to a polysphere with a clearly visible rim near the base. Each bubble is like a round pronouncing hat with small dark brims. Our task is to figure out what kind of “hat” this is and where its “brims” came from.

We know that molecules on the surface of a liquid have a certain potential energy, called surface energy. The magnitude of this energy is proportional to the surface area, and the proportionality coefficient - different for each liquid - is called the surface tension coefficient. It is known that any system at equilibrium is in that state (from among those possible for it) in which

its energy is minimal. In relation to our bubbles, this means that the surface area of ​​the bubble should be as minimal as possible. A unique soap bubble has the shape of a sphere: after all, for a given volume of the body (air in the bubble), the sphere has a minimum surface area (rolls have little weight on the bubble, since it is very light). If you slightly deform the bubble, for example by blowing on it, then left to its own devices the bubble will again take the shape of a sphere.

On the surface of the water, bubbles are already different in shape: if the bubble is large, then it looks like a hemisphere, not a sphere. The bubble shell has a convex shape due to the excess pressure $\Delta p$ of air inside the bubble. Since the value $\Delta p$ is constant (the air is in equilibrium), the “cap” of the bubble is curved everywhere equally; in other words, the walls of the “cap” have the same curvature everywhere, i.e. they are part of the sphere. But how much of the sphere is above the water?

We will assume that the base of the bubble (its “bottom”, the role of which is played by the surface of the reservoir) is flat. This assumption is, of course, not always true, and the larger the bubble size, the closer it is to the truth (we will return to this issue later). It is easy to understand that such a bubble for a given volume of air has the shape of a hemisphere (the hemisphere in this case has a minimum area).

Now let's talk about the “rim” that surrounds the “cap” of the water bladder. What is it? It's not hard to guess, sorry.

It is known that water rises in a narrow capillary if it wets its walls. Energy is “profitable”. The smaller the radius of the capillary, the higher the liquid level in it. Anyone interested in calculations or physical assessments can join us in taking a closer look at the issue of capillary rise of water near a smooth vertical wall when it is completely wetted by liquid.

Let us estimate the maximum increase in water level $H$ near the boundaries of the body and determine the dependence of $H$ on the surface tension coefficient $\sigma$. We will use the dimensional method for this.

The equilibrium of a liquid during capillary rise is determined by surface tension and gravity. The position $H$ can depend only on $\sigma$, on the density of the liquid $\rho$ and the acceleration of gravity $g$. We will look for the function $H=f(\sigma, g, \rho)$ in the form

$$H = \text{const} \cdot \sigma^l \cdot g^m \cdot \rho^n. \quad (*)$$

(Of course, the physical quantities that define any phenomenon are not always related by a power law, but very often this is exactly the case.) To obtain in explicit form the dependence of $H$ on $\sigma, g$ and $\rho$, we need to find the values ​​of $l, m$ and $n$.

It is clear that the left and right sides, respectively (*), have the same dimension. Let's write down the dimensions of the quantities included in (*) -

$$[H] = L, \quad [\sigma] = MT^{-2}, \quad [g] = LT^{-2}, \quad [\rho] = ML^{-3}$$

(L, M, T - units of length, mass and time) - and write the corresponding equality

$$L = (MT^{-2})^l (LT^{-2})^m (ML^{-3})^n.$$

From here we obtain a system of linear equations for the unknowns $l, m$ and $n$:

$$l + n = 0, \quad l + m = 0, \quad m - 3n = 1.$$

The system has only one solution

$$l = 1/2, \quad m = -1/2 \text{ и } n = -1/2.$$

Thus,

$$H = \text{const} \cdot \sqrt{\frac{\sigma}{\rho g}}, \quad \text{или } H \sim \sqrt{\frac{\sigma}{\rho g}}.$$

It is impossible to determine the method of dimensions for an unknown constant. However, in physical formulas obtained by theoretical calculation, the numerical coefficients are, as a rule, close to unity; in any case, they do not affect the order of magnitude. The exact value of $H$ differs from our estimate by $\sqrt{2}$ times: $H = \sqrt{2\sigma/\rho g}$.

Now we can estimate how high the water rises near the vertical wall. Substituting the values ​​$\sigma$ and $\rho$ for water ($\sigma \approx 73 \cdot 10^{-3} \text{ H/m}, \rho \approx 10^3 \text{ kr/m}^3$), we get $H \approx 4$ mm. The water rises approximately the same amount at the base of the walls of the spherical part of the bubble. The curved surface of the water at the base of the bubble under proper lighting appears as a dark rim. The rim width of all large bubbles is of the same order as $H$, i.e. several millimeters.

An attentive reader will notice that the bubbles in the photograph are not exact hemispheres, but as if slightly submerged in water, and that the rim inside the bubble differs from the outside. (Under jamm, observing bubbles, it is very difficult to catch such a difference.) Why is this so? The fact is that, as we have already said, there is more air pressure under the shell of the bubble than outside. So inside the bubble

The excess air pressure inside the bubble is $\Delta p = 4\sigma/R$. In the water surrounding the bubble, such additional (to atmospheric) pressure is created at a certain depth $\Delta h$ below the surface of the water. It is clear that it is precisely this $\Delta h$ that the allowed bubble will “sag” (Fig. 2, a). From the equality $4\sigma/R = \rho g \cdot \Delta h$ we find $\Delta h$:

$$\Delta h = \frac{4\sigma}{\rho g R}.$$

For the bubbles that we formed, the values ​​of $\Delta h$ were in the range of 1–3 mm.

Thus, a large bubble has the appearance of a hemisphere, slightly submerged in liquid and has internal curved edges at the base. We will call large bubbles those with $R \gg H \approx \sqrt{\sigma/\rho g}$.

What do smaller bubbles look like? Try to figure out this difficult issue on your own. Let us only note that if we “make” a small bubble in water (with a radius of about 1 mm or less), then it will not float above the liquid level at all, but will be under water, as if glued to the water surface. Let's move on to the last question. Let's talk about the stability of the water bubble. We need to figure out why bubbles “live” for a long time only if it’s cloudy or if it’s still rain.

Some people will say that during the jedra there can be a lot of bubbles, which is why they seem to be “long-lived.” There is some truth in this statement. But if you follow a separate bubble, you can easily see that our question is not far-fetched. Bubbles in rainy or cloudy weather actually live much longer than, say, in dry sunny weather (in dry weather they “live” so little that no one even observes them).

There are many different reasons why bubbles break. The bubble shell can be torn on top, 《pierced》 by a large jedrink, etc. It may evaporate or become very thin and patchy due to water flowing down the walls of the bubble. The presence of so-called surfactants (for example, soap) in water can, on the contrary, extend the life of bubbles tens or hundreds of times.
