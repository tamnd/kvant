M1142. Table $m \times n$ filling in mn numbers so that in each row and in each column these numbers form an arithmetic progression. The sum of the four numbers at the nodes of the table is equal to s. What is the sum of all the numbers in the table?

Answer: $smn/4$.

Let $a_1, a_2, \dots, a_m$ be the numbers in the top line, $b_1, b_2, \dots, b_m$ the numbers in the bottom. Then $a_1 + a_2 + \dots + a_m = (a_1 + a_m)m/2, b_1 + b_2 + \dots + b_m = (b_1 + b_m)m/2$.

The sums of the numbers in the columns are $(a_1 + b_1)n/2, (a_2 + b_2)n/2, \dots, (a_m + b_m)n/2$. Adding these sums, we get the sum of all the numbers in the table:
$$(a_1 + b_1 + a_2 + b_2 + \dots + a_m + b_m)n/2 = (a_1 + a_m + b_1 + b_m)mn/4 = smn/4.$$
N. V. Vasiliev

M1143. The mass of each of the 101 mushrooms located in a circle is a natural number, and their total mass is 300 g. Prove that from this set one can choose one of several mushrooms located in a row with a total mass of 200 g.

Let us mark 300 points on the circle, dividing it into 300 equal parts ($1^\circ 12'$ each), and assign arcs of $m$ such parts to each mushroom of mass $m$; the ends of these arcs (located around the circle in the same order as the mushrooms) are called points, the remaining 300-101=199 points are called black. Let's consider all equilateral triangles inscribed in a circle, one of which has a red vertex. If each of them had two other vertices that were black, then there would be at least $2 \cdot 101 = 202$ black points in total. Therefore, it will find a triangle whose two vertices are red. The larger arc (at $240^\circ$) with ends at these red vertices corresponds to mushrooms with a total mass of 200 g, the smaller one ($210^\circ$) corresponds to mushrooms with a mass of 100 g.

M1144. Let $a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n$ be non-negative numbers. Which number is greater:

$$\sqrt{a_1^{1988} + a_2^{1988} + \dots + a_n^{1988}}$$

or

$$\sqrt{a_1^{1989} + a_2^{1989} + \dots + a_n^{1989}}$$?

Answer: The first number is greater if at least two of the numbers $a_i$ are not equal to 0 (which we will assume in the future).

Let us immediately prove more general statements: the multi-night function $f(\alpha) = (a_1^\alpha + \dots + a_n^\alpha)^{1/\alpha}$ decreases as $\alpha > 0$, i.e. $f(\alpha) > f(\beta)$ as $0 < \alpha < \beta$.

Without loss of generality, we can assume that $a_1$ is the most general number of $a_i$, $a_1 > 0$. Let us put $x_i = a_i/a_1$, then at $\alpha < \beta$

$$f(\alpha) = a_1(1 + x_2^\alpha + \dots + x_n^\alpha)^{1/\alpha} \geq a_1(1 + x_2^\beta + \dots + x_n^\beta)^{1/\beta} = f(\beta),$$

M1145*. From the point $P$ two tangents PB and PC are drawn to the circle, and $\angle BPC > 90^\circ$. Point A is taken less than arc BC. Prove that the area of ​​the triangle cut off from angle BPC by the tangent to the circle at point A does not exceed the area of ​​triangle ABC.

Fig. 1.

Fig. 2.

Let us denote the triangle cut off from angle BPC by KPL (Fig. 1), and the center of its incircle by I. Triangles ABC and ILK are similar. Indeed, each of the angles ABC, CAL and ACL is equal to half the angular value of the arc AC (the first angle is inscribed in the circle and rests on this arc, the other two are formed by the chord subtending this arc and the tangents at its ends). That's why

$$2\angle ILK = \angle PLK = \angle LAC + \angle LCA = 2\angle ABC,$$

i.e. $\angle ILK = \angle ABC$. Likewise, $\angle IKL = \angle ACB$. The similarity coefficient of triangles ILK and ABC is $KL/BC$, therefore, the ratio of their areas is $S_{ILK}/S_{ABC} = KL^2/BC^2$.

Let, further, $p$ be the semiperimeter, $S$ the area, $r$ the radius of the inscribed circle and $h$ the height omitted from the vertex $P$ of the triangle PKL (Fig. 2). Then

$$\frac{S_{PKL}}{S_{ILK}} = \frac{h}{r} = \frac{2S}{KL} \cdot \frac{p}{S} = \frac{2p}{KL} = \frac{2BP}{KL},$$

since $BP = CP$ and $BP + CP = AK + KP + PL + LA = 2p$ (we use here the equality of tangents to the circle drawn from the points $P$, $K$, $L$). So, $S_{PLK}/S_{ABC} = 2BP \cdot KL/BC^2$. It remains to prove that the last ratio is less than 1.

By the triangle inequality $2KL < KL + KP + PL = 2p = 2BP$, i.e. $2BP \cdot KL < 2BP^2$. But in the case when $\angle BPC > 90^\circ$, we have $2BP^2 < BC^2$, i.e. $2BP \cdot KL/BC^2 < 2BP^2/BC^2 < 1$, which is what needed to be proved.

This problem can also be solved using trigonometry. If $\alpha, \beta, \gamma$ are the angles at the vertices $A, B, C$ of triangle ABC, then $BC = KL$ cos $\beta$ cos $\gamma$; the necessary inequality is reduced to the form 2 cos $(\pi - \alpha)$ cos $\beta$ cos $\gamma > 1$ and follows from the fact that $\pi - \alpha = \beta + \gamma < \pi/4$.

When braking sharply, the wheels do not automatically move, but rather slide (this is the only way to quickly brake). Then from Newton's second law it follows

$$ma = F_{\text{тр}} = \mu mg, \quad \text{т. е. } a = \mu g,$$

where $\mu$ is the sliding friction coefficient.

There are two possible cases, shown in Figures 1 and 2, respectively. First case: acceleration $a_1$ is such that part of the bottom opens. When assessing, we will assume that the body is straight. Then the mass of concrete in the body before braking

$$M = \rho L H d,$$

where $\rho$ is the density of the solution, and $d$, $H$ and $L$ are the width, height and length of the body. As can be seen from figure 1, $H/l = tg \alpha = a_1/g$, $l = Hg/a_1 \leq L$ opens, i.e.

$$a_1 = \mu_1 g \geq \frac{H}{L} g.$$

Weight of concrete remaining in the body

$$m_1 = \rho l \frac{H}{2} d = \rho \frac{H^2 d}{2 a_1} g.$$

Thus, the proportion of remaining concrete

$$\eta_1 = \frac{m_1}{M} = \frac{\rho H^2 d}{2 a_1 \rho L H d} = \frac{H}{L} \frac{g}{2 a_1} = \frac{H}{L} \frac{1}{2 \mu_1}$$

at $\mu_1 \geq \frac{H}{L}$.

The second case (see Fig. 2): the acceleration $a_2$ is such that the tailgate is freed from the mortar to a depth $h = L tg \alpha = La_2/g$, and the depth of the mortar layer there is $H - h = H - La_2/g$. Weight of solution remaining in the body

$$m_2 = \rho L \left( H - L \frac{a_2}{2 g} \right) d,$$

and the proportion of the remaining solution

$$\eta_2 = \frac{m_2}{M} = \frac{\rho L \left( H - La_2/(2 g) \right) d}{\rho L H} = 1 - \frac{L}{H} \frac{a_2}{2 g} = 1 - \frac{L}{H} \frac{\mu_2}{2}$$

at $\mu_2 < \frac{H}{L}$.

Let's make numerical estimates. Let us put $H = 1$ m, $L = 5$ m, i.e. $H/L = 0,2$. Then at $\mu_1 \geq H/L = 0,2$, for example at $\mu_1 = 0,5$, we have

$$\eta_1 = \frac{H}{L} \frac{1}{2 \mu_1} = \frac{1}{5} = 20 \%.$$

At $\mu_2 < H/L = 0,2$, for example at $\mu_2 = 0,1$, we have

$$\eta_2 = 1 - \frac{L}{H} \frac{\mu_2}{2} = \frac{3}{4} = 75 \%.$$

If the braking is not sharp, that is, the wheels are shaking, then the braking is due to rolling friction. In this case $\mu \approx 0,02$ and

F1154. Two identical cubes of mass m each lie on the main horizontal table. One of the cups is glued to the table (see Fig.nok). A cube comes off the table if a horizontal force F is applied to it. Between the cubes there is an inexhaustible free spring of stiffness k. The loose cube was given speed v. At what speed will the cubes fly apart after a collision?

Let us denote the speeds of the cups after the collision by $v_1$ and $v_2$. From the law of conservation of energy we have

$$\frac{mv^2}{2} = \frac{mv_1^2}{2} + \frac{mv_2^2}{2}.$$

Immediately after the second cube is removed from the table, the system of two cups (between which there is an inexhaustible spring) becomes closed. Let us write down the law of conservation of momentum for it. Let us denote by u the speed of the first cube immediately after the second cube leaves the table. Then we get

$$mu = mv_2 - mv_1.$$

We find the speed u from the law of conservation of energy

$$\frac{mv^2}{2} = \frac{mu^2}{2} + \frac{kx^2}{2},$$

where $x = F/k$ is the magnitude of the spring force at the moment of separation.

Solving the system of equations (1)–(3), we find

$$v_1 = \frac{1}{2}(\sqrt{v^2 + F^2}/(km) - \sqrt{v^2 - F^2}/(km)),$$

$$v_2 = \frac{1}{2}(\sqrt{v^2 + F^2}/(km) + \sqrt{v^2 - F^2}/(km)).$$

It is interesting to consider some partial cases.

Let, for example, $mv^2/2 < F^2/(2k)$. Then the maximum rolling of the spring will not be enough to lift the second cube from the table and the first cube after the collision will move to the left from the initial speed v.

If $mv^2/2 = F^2/(2k)$, then after lying down the cubes will move in opposite directions with equal velocities equal to $v/\sqrt{2}$.

If $mv^2/2 \gg F^2/(2k)$ (the case of a very stiff spring, when $k \to \infty$), then $v_1 \to 0$, and $v_2 \to v$.

F1157. There are two translucent mirrors, each of which, as measured, transmits approximately 1/5 of the light flux, and reflects the rest of the light. If both mirrors stand in the path of a parallel beam of light so that their planes are perpendicular to the beam, then it would seem that they should transmit 1/25 of the incident light, whereas in fact the light is attenuated not by 25 times, but noticeably less (by about 10 times). The explanation is that the rays of light reflected by the second mirror return to the first mirror, are partially reflected from it and, falling on the second, increase the intensity of the transmitted beam.

Let $I$ be the intensity of the initial light beam (see figure; the incident light beam is shown inclined for clarity). Then the intensity of light passing through the first mirror will be equal to $i_0 = I/k$, where $k$ is the attenuation coefficient of the mirror ($k = 5$ according to the problem conditions). With successive reflections, the intensity of the light “captured” between the mirrors gradually decreases due to the partial energy of the mirrors, and after a sufficiently large number of reflections, almost all of the “captured” light will be released, almost equally to the right and left. Thus, the total intensity of transmitted light will be equal to

$$I' \approx \frac{i_0}{2} = \frac{I}{2k} = \frac{I}{10}.$$

The problem also allows for an exact solution. Let us write down how the intensity of the light “captured” between the mirrors changes during successive reflections:

$$i_1 = i_0 \left(1 - \frac{1}{k}\right)^2, \quad i_2 = i_1 \left(1 - \frac{1}{k}\right)^2 = i_0 \left(1 - \frac{1}{k}\right)^4,$$

With each fall on mirror 2, the light partially comes out to the right:

$$I_1 = \frac{i_0}{k}, \quad I_2 = \frac{i_1}{k}, \quad I_3 = \frac{i_2}{k},$$

Total transmitted light intensity

$$I' = I_1 + I_2 + I_3 + \dots = \frac{1}{k} \left(i_0 + i_1 + i_2 + \dots\right) = \frac{i_0}{k} \left(1 + \left(1 - \frac{1}{k}\right)^2 + \left(1 - \frac{1}{k}\right)^4 + \dots\right)$$

represents the sum of an infinite decreasing geometric progression with value $q = (1 - 1/k)^2$, so

$$I' = \frac{i_0}{k} \frac{1}{1 - q} = \frac{i_0}{k} \frac{k^2}{k^2 - (k - 1)^2} = \frac{i_0 k}{2k - 1} = \frac{I}{2k - 1} = \frac{I}{9}.$$

At $k = 5$ the exact solution differs by only 10% from the approximate one.
