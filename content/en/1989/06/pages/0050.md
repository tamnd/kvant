Such a spatial quadrilateral can be obtained by cutting out a quadrilateral ABCD from paper and bending it diagonally at a certain angle (Fig. 6, a). It is clear that the midlines KL and MN of triangles ABC and ADC remain their midlines and will be parallel to the segment AC and equal to AC/2. (Here we use the fact that the basic property of parallel lines remains true for space: if two lines KL and MN are parallel to a third line AC, then KL and MN lie in the same plane and are parallel to each other.)

Thus, points K, L, M, N are the vertices of the parallelogram; Thus, the segments KM and LN intersect and are divided in half by the intersection point.

Instead of a quadrilateral, we can talk about a tetrahedron - a triangular paramid ABCD: the midpoints K, L, M, N of its edges AB, AC, CD and DA always lie in the same plane. By cutting the tetrahedron along this plane (Fig. 6, b), we obtain a parallelogram KLMN, two sides of which are parallel to the edge AC and equal to AC/2, and the other two are parallel to the edge BD and are equal to BD/2.

The same parallelogram—the “middle section” of the tetrahedron—can be constructed for other pairs of opposite edges. Each of these three parallelograms has a common diagonal. In this case, the midpoints of the diagonals coincide. So we get an interesting corollary:

Theorem 6. Three segments connecting the midpoints of opposite edges of the tetrahedron intersect at one point and divide it in half (Fig. 7).

This and other facts discussed above are naturally explained in the language of mechanics - using the concept of the center of mass. Theorem 5 talks about one of the replacement points tetraedrika - the point of intersection of the medians; in Theorem 6 - about a replacement point for the four vertices of a tetrahedron. These points are the centers of mass of the corresponding tetrahedron. Let us first return to Theorem 5 on medians.
