F1157. There are two translucent mirrors, each of which, as measured, transmits approximately 1/5 of the light flux, and reflects the rest of the light. If both mirrors stand in the path of a parallel beam of light so that their planes are perpendicular to the beam, then it would seem that they should transmit 1/25 of the incident light, whereas in fact the light is attenuated not by 25 times, but noticeably less (by about 10 times). The explanation is that the rays of light reflected by the second mirror return to the first mirror, are partially reflected from it and, falling on the second, increase the intensity of the transmitted beam.

Let $I$ be the intensity of the initial light beam (see figure; the incident light beam is shown inclined for clarity). Then the intensity of light passing through the first mirror will be equal to $i_0 = I/k$, where $k$ is the attenuation coefficient of the mirror ($k = 5$ according to the problem conditions). With successive reflections, the intensity of the light “captured” between the mirrors gradually decreases due to the partial energy of the mirrors, and after a sufficiently large number of reflections, almost all of the “captured” light will be released, almost equally to the right and left. Thus, the total intensity of transmitted light will be equal to

$$I' \approx \frac{i_0}{2} = \frac{I}{2k} = \frac{I}{10}.$$

The problem also allows for an exact solution. Let us write down how the intensity of the light “captured” between the mirrors changes during successive reflections:

$$i_1 = i_0 \left(1 - \frac{1}{k}\right)^2, \quad i_2 = i_1 \left(1 - \frac{1}{k}\right)^2 = i_0 \left(1 - \frac{1}{k}\right)^4,$$

With each fall on mirror 2, the light partially comes out to the right:

$$I_1 = \frac{i_0}{k}, \quad I_2 = \frac{i_1}{k}, \quad I_3 = \frac{i_2}{k},$$

Total transmitted light intensity

$$I' = I_1 + I_2 + I_3 + \dots = \frac{1}{k} \left(i_0 + i_1 + i_2 + \dots\right) = \frac{i_0}{k} \left(1 + \left(1 - \frac{1}{k}\right)^2 + \left(1 - \frac{1}{k}\right)^4 + \dots\right)$$

represents the sum of an infinite decreasing geometric progression with value $q = (1 - 1/k)^2$, so

$$I' = \frac{i_0}{k} \frac{1}{1 - q} = \frac{i_0}{k} \frac{k^2}{k^2 - (k - 1)^2} = \frac{i_0 k}{2k - 1} = \frac{I}{2k - 1} = \frac{I}{9}.$$

At $k = 5$ the exact solution differs by only 10% from the approximate one.

V. V. Golubev
