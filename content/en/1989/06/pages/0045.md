POPP HAD A DOG...

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
S. L. TAVACHNIKOV

As a child, almost everyone had to hear the story of how

The priest had a dog, He loved her, She cut a piece of meat, He left her, He buried her in the ground, The inscription was written: “The priest had a dog...”

This simple poem is attractive because with each tuning it returns to itself again and again, like a snake swallowing its own tail. I will give several examples of such sentences, mathematical and non-mathematical, that say something about themselves.

There are twenty-eight letters in this phrase.

This phrase is a typical example of a sentence that talks about itself. Count the letters and you will see that this is the pure truth. Here's a more complicated example:

This sentence contains twenty words, twenty-six words and seventy-three letters.

Don't believe me? Check that this is also a “fair” offer.

I read the following example in one of the issues of the American magazine “Scientific American” for 1982 (some other examples are also from there) *. The magazine was then published only in English, and I had to work hard before the next “monster” was born.

In this phrase, the word “in” occurred twice, the word “this” occurred twice, the word “phrase” occurred twice, the word “occurs” occurred fourteen times, the word “occurs” occurred fourteen times, the word “occurs” occurred fourteen times, the word “occurred” six times, the word “one” occurred nine times, the word “fourteen” occurred three times, the word “three” occurred three times, and occurred twice the word “nine”, the word “seven” appeared twice, the word “six” appeared twice.

Ugh! This sentence is not at all easy to read. And yet it states the pure truth. Here's a task for you:

Come up with a tenth number, the first digit of which shows how many ones there are in this number, the second - how many twos there are in it, the third - how many threes, ..., tens - how many zeros.
