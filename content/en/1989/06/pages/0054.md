Thick plate colors

Candidate of Pedagogical Sciences
Y. E. AMSTISLAVSKY

Who among you has not admired the rainbow play of colors in soap films, the variety of brightly colored stripes of intricate shapes in oil films on sea asphalt (or on water) or other similar manifestations of white color interference in thin films? Is it possible to observe such a picture in laboratory conditions? How to explain it?

In general, the interference of light is discussed in some detail in the school physics course in the tenth grade. (In particular, the origin of the colors of thin films is explained there.) Here, very little will be said about the interference itself, only what is necessary for understanding the content of the article.

Light interference is the superposition of two (or more) light waves, in which regularly intersecting areas of increased and decreased light intensity appear in space. The interference pattern will be stable if the superimposed waves are consistent, or, as they say, coherent: the oscillation frequencies are the same, and the phase difference remains constant over time.

Before the advent of lasers, coherent light beams could only be produced by splitting beams coming from the same source.
