The excess air pressure inside the bubble is $\Delta p = 4\sigma/R$. In the water surrounding the bubble, such additional (to atmospheric) pressure is created at a certain depth $\Delta h$ below the surface of the water. It is clear that it is precisely this $\Delta h$ that the allowed bubble will “sag” (Fig. 2, a). From the equality $4\sigma/R = \rho g \cdot \Delta h$ we find $\Delta h$:

$$\Delta h = \frac{4\sigma}{\rho g R}.$$

For the bubbles that we formed, the values ​​of $\Delta h$ were in the range of 1–3 mm.

Thus, a large bubble has the appearance of a hemisphere, slightly submerged in liquid and has internal curved edges at the base. We will call large bubbles those with $R \gg H \approx \sqrt{\sigma/\rho g}$.

What do smaller bubbles look like? Try to figure out this difficult issue on your own. Let us only note that if we “make” a small bubble in water (with a radius of about 1 mm or less), then it will not float above the liquid level at all, but will be under water, as if glued to the water surface. The shape of the small bubble is spherical. figure 2 shows what a large bubble looks like, i.e. one with $R \gg \sqrt{\sigma/\rho g}$ (a), a medium-sized bubble with $R \approx \sqrt{\sigma/\rho g}$ (b), and a small bubble in the shape of a sphere with a radius $R \ll \sqrt{\sigma/\rho g}$ (b).

Consider why the shape of a bubble varies depending on its size—the larger the bubble, the more hemisphere-like it is. And why does jedra appear in the main bubble or medium size? Readers will find an explanation of this fact in Ya. E. Geguzin’s wonderful book “Bubbles,” published by the Nauka publishing house in 1985 in the “Library «Kvant»” series (issue 46).

Let's move on to the last question. Let's talk about the stability of the water bubble. We need to figure out why bubbles “live” for a long time only if it’s cloudy or if it’s still rain.

Some people will say that during the jedra there can be a lot of bubbles, which is why they seem to be “long-lived.” There is some truth in this statement. But if you follow a separate bubble, you can easily see that our question is not far-fetched. Bubbles in rainy or cloudy weather actually live much longer than, say, in dry sunny weather (in dry weather they “live” so little that no one even observes them).

There are many different reasons why bubbles break. The bubble shell can be torn on top, 《pierced》 by a large jedrink, etc. It may evaporate or become very thin and patchy due to water flowing down the walls of the bubble. The presence of so-called surfactants (for example, soap) in water can, on the contrary, extend the life of bubbles tens or hundreds of times. (Remember the foam on the river, which is not so easy to destroy.)
