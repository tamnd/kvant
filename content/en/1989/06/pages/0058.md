Without resorting to calculating the value of $\Delta$, we will try to draw some conclusions about the features of the interference pattern formed by a complex diffuser.

1) Let's start with the case when the source $S$ and the observation point $O$ are located on the same normal to the diffuser surface. At figure 3 it is easy to see that this arrangement is characterized by basic symmetry, with $\Delta$ monotonically increasing from zero as $r$ increases. Therefore, the interference pattern is a system of concentric colored and dark wheels with a central white spot and a source $S$ in the middle of it. A distinctive feature of interference in diffuse light is precisely that the interference pattern is formed against the background of the source and remains clear regardless of the thickness of the diffuser $2t$ (and even its location).

2) The rate of rise of $\Delta$ with the change of $r$ is very magnifying to the location of the observer's eye on the $SO$ axis. Let's introduce a point $S_1$, symmetrical to the exact source $S$, and ground it with a “mirror image” of the source in the diffuser. From figure 3 it is clear that if the point $O$ coincides with $S_1$ (i.e. $b=a$), for any $r$ the optical paths of both interfering rays turn out to be the same ($O_3C'' = SC''$ and $O_3C' = SC'$) and $\Delta=0$. This means that the zero white spot occupies the entire field of observation.
