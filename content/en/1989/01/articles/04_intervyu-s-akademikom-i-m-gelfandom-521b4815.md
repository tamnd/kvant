INTERVIEW WITH ACADEMICIAN
I. M. GELFANDOM

Gelfand's mathematical seminar, which has been meeting on Monday evenings at Moscow University for 45 years, is universally famous. Both first-year students and famous scientists study at the seminar. Several generations of outstanding mathematicians were educated by this seminar.

Israel Moiseevich is the founder of the All-Russian Correspondence Mathematical School and the chairman of its Scientific Council. The main task of this school is to help those schoolchildren who, unlike residents of Moscow, Leningrad and other large cities, are practically deprived of mathematical literature and contacts with scientists. Created 25 years ago, the All-Use Correspondence Mathematics School was the first school of its type in our country; Subsequently, other correspondence schools were opened following its example.

The meeting with Academician Gelfand was always planned as a general interview «Kvant». We prepared questions that we thought would be interesting to both our reader and our interlocutor. Israel Moiseevich, having looked through the list of questions, noted that the questions were very interesting, but he... did not consider himself competent to answer them.

- You understand, I do not consider myself to have the right to express my opinion to your young reader. Let me tell you how we do mathematics at an age that coincides with the age of most of the readers of Quantum - from 13 to 17 years old. I’m not sure that I now remember all the tasks that I was doing during this time, but I remembered the tasks that I’m going to talk about very well. Here is the story of I.M. Gelfand.

One of Graham Greene's novels is called "The loser takes all"). My mathematical biography was surprisingly happy, and for many years it was the fulfillment of this statement. How am I lucky? In short, firstly, that I did not study at the university (or any educational institution at all), and, secondly, that as a result of certain life difficulties that befell my family, I found myself in Moscow without parents, unemployed, at sixteen and a half years old.

I’ll try to understand the meaning of the phrase “the loser takes all” with the help of a story by another English writer, Somerset Mose. The main character of the story, a certain church servant, fails: during the certification of church personnel, it turns out that he is illiterate, and he is kicked out of work. He starts working as a seller of sirah, then buys out a table kiosk, then several other kiosks... and makes a brilliant commercial career, becoming the richest man in his city, its zer. And so they interview him - like you are interviewing me now - and he tells the journalist that he is illiterate. The needy journalist will exclaim: “What heights would you have reached if you were literate!” The answer immediately follows: “I would be a church minister.”

So in February 1930, at the age of 16 and a half, having arrived in Moscow to visit distant relatives, I was partly unemployed, took on any temporary work, and mostly sat in the Lenin Library and “acquired” the knowledge that I had not received at school and at an independent vocational school. In the library I met university students and began attending seminars. At the age of 18 I was already teaching, and at 19 I went to graduate school, and my further mathematical biography followed an ordinary, regular course, here it fits into the mainstream that is inculcated for mathematicians.

But I didn’t want to tell the reader «Kvant» about this part of my life, but about the previous one - about how I studied mathematics between the ages of 13 and 17. I want to talk about this for two reasons. Firstly, in my deep conviction, in the majority of future professional mathematicians, mathematical talent manifests itself precisely in the period from 13 to 16 years**), although, of course, there are fluctuations, from earlier development to much later development (from 20 to 30 and even 40 years), which is observed in some strong mathematicians. When I discovered that sinhus can be written algebraically in the form of a series, the barrier fell, mathematics became unified. And now I perceive the various branches of mathematics together with mathematical physics as a single whole.

$$F(n+1) - F(1) = S_0 + \frac{S_1}{2!} + \frac{S_2}{3!} + \dots,$$

where $S_0$ is the amount I’m interested in, and

Then I wrote down the system

$$F(n+1) - F(1) = S_0 + \frac{S_1}{2!} + \frac{S_2}{3!} + \frac{S_3}{4!} + \dots,$$

$$f(n+1) - f(1) = S_1 + \frac{S_2}{2!} + \frac{S_3}{3!} + \dots,$$

$$S_0 = \frac{1}{1}$$

I expanded the numerator of this “fraction” into the first column, obtaining

$$S_0 = B_0(F(n+1) - F(1)) + B_1(f(n+1) - f(1)) + B_2(f'(n+1) - f'(1)) + \dots,$$

(1)

where $B_0 = 1, B_1, B_2, \dots$ are numerical determinants of infinite order. The resulting expression is called the Euler-Maclaurin formula, but of course I didn’t know about it. To calculate it, it was necessary to find the coefficients $B_0, B_1, B_2, \dots$.

To do this, I applied considerations that today are called “functional”. Namely, taking advantage of the fact that the coefficients $B_0, B_1, \dots$ does not depend on $f$, I selected a function $f$ such that the left side of the system formed a geometric progression (which I knew how to sum). The function $f(x) = e^{\alpha x}$ is suitable for this, substituting it into formula (1), I got (do the calculations!)

$$B_0 + \alpha B_1 + \alpha^2 B_2 + \dots = \frac{\alpha}{e^{\alpha}-1},$$

i.e., he obtained the product of the function for the required numbers $B_0, B_1, B_2, \dots$ (they are called Bernoulli numbers, and the polynomial $S_0$ for $f(x) = x^p$ is called the Bernoulli polynomial).

7. To get acquainted with the determinants, let’s solve a system of linear equations:

$$\begin{cases} a_1x + b_1y = c_1, \\ a_2x + b_2y = c_2. \end{cases}$$

If $a_1b_2 - a_2b_1 \neq 0$, its solution gives the formulas:

$$x = \frac{c_1b_2 - c_2b_1}{a_1b_2 - a_2b_1}, y = \frac{a_1c_2 - a_2c_1}{a_1b_2 - a_2b_1}.$$

The quantity $a_1b_2 - a_2b_1$ in the denominator is denoted by $\begin{matrix} a_1 & b_1 \\ a_2 & b_2 \end{matrix}$ and is called the determinant. Numerals also contain determiners $\begin{matrix} c_1 & b_1 \\ c_2 & b_2 \end{matrix}$ and $\begin{matrix} a_1 & c_1 \\ a_2 & c_2 \end{matrix}$.

Similarly, when solving the system

$$\begin{cases} a_1x + b_1y + c_1z = d_1, \\ a_2x + b_2y + c_2z = d_2, \\ a_3x + b_3y + c_3z = d_3 \end{cases}$$

the denominator will contain third-order determinants. Determinant

$$\begin{matrix} a_1 & b_1 & c_1 \\ a_2 & b_2 & c_2 \\ a_3 & b_3 & c_3 \end{matrix}$$

equalized $a_1(b_2c_3 - b_3c_2) + a_2(b_3c_1 - b_1c_3) + a_3(b_1c_2 - b_2c_1)$. Note that there is a second-order determinant in parentheses. This notation of the determinant is called its decomposition in the first column.

It is possible to agree on which one-years of the variables $a_1, a_2, \dots, d_4$ the determinant of the fourth order consists of:

$$\begin{matrix} a_1 & b_1 & c_1 & d_1 \\ a_2 & b_2 & c_2 & d_2 \\ a_3 & b_3 & c_3 & d_3 \\ a_4 & b_4 & c_4 & d_4 \end{matrix}.$$

It is much more difficult to guess what the signs of these annuals are.

Square tables of numbers are called (square) matrices, and the corresponding determinants are called matrix determinants. It is interesting that now I.M. Gelfand is fascinated by the question: what should the determinant of a cubic matrix be equal to? When the answer is received, he will be found to tell about it on the pages «Kvant».

Solving the general problem of how the sum of powers of the roots of an equation is expressed through its coefficients, I. M. Gelfand proved Newton’s formulas:

$$p_k + a_1p_k-1 + a_2p_k-2 + \dots + a_k-1p_k = 0, \quad k \leq n, \quad p_k + a_1p_k-1 + a_2p_k-2 + \dots + a_np_k-n, \quad k > n.$$

Here $p_k$ denotes the sum of the $i$ powers of the roots $x_i^1 + x_i^2 + \dots + x_i^n$.

Newton's formulas can be considered as a system of linear equations for $p_i$ with coefficients $a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n$. If you solve this system, you will get the Variant formula, which directly expresses the sum of the degrees of the equations through its coefficients. In modern mathematics, this complexion has received far development.
