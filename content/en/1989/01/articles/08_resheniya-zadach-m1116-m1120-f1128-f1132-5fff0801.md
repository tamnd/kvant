Answer: a) 74; b) 6) $2S+2$.
A tract of size $m \times n$ cells contains $f=(m+1)(n+1)$ nodes. For a given area $mn=S$ the value

$$f=S+m+n+1=S+m+\frac{S}{m}+1$$

takes the most value $2S+2$ for $m=1$ or $m=S$ (for a rectangle of size $1 \times S$). For other $m$ ($1<m<S$), the value of $f$ is less than $2S+2$. This follows from inequality $m+\frac{S}{m}<S+1$, which is equivalent to the next inequality $(m-S)(m-1)<0$. Note that a more general statement is true: given $S$ (S is an integer or the resulting number), of all polygons with vertices at checkered paper nodes, the polygon that has no internal nodes contains the largest number of nodes. This follows from Pick's formula for cell $S$ of such polygons*: $S=i+\frac{r}{2}-1$, where $i$ is the number of nodes lying inside the polygon, and $r$ is the number of nodes on its boundary: the total number of nodes is $i+r \leq 2i+r=2S+2$. (Inequality turns into equality only at $i=0$.)

M1118. a) Prove that the equation $(x+y+z)^2=x^2+y^2+z^2$ (*) has infinite multisolutions in four numbers $(x, y, z)$ that do not have a common divisor greater than 1.
b) How many solutions are there for which $z=1988$?

a) Let us rewrite equation (*) in the form
$$xy+yz+zx=0.$$
Note that if one of the unknowns is 0, then some others are also 0; In what follows we will not consider such trivial solutions. Let $d$ be the most common divisor of $x$ and $y$, $x=da$, $y=ab$. then $z=-xy/(x+y)=-dab/(a+b)$, where $a, b$ and $a+b$ are pairwise coprime, so $d$ is divisible by $a+b:d=k(a+b)$. From here we obtain a general formula for nontrivial solutions:
$$x=ka(a+b), y=kb(a+b), z=-kab,$$
where $a, b, k$ are integers, and $a$ and $b$ are coprime. For $k=1$ we obtain all possible different solutions from numbers that do not have a common divisor greater than 1.
b) Answer: 16. The question boils down to how many ways can one represent 1988 as the product $-ab$, where $a$ and $b$ are coprime integers. Since 1988 = $4 \cdot 7 \cdot 71$, in the role of $a$ we can take the product of some numbers - 1, 4, 7, 71 (then $b$ will be the product of the rest; the product of the “empty set” can naturally be considered 1.)

F1129. A coil shaped like a narrow pole (Fig. 1) is put on a cylindrical permanent magnet in the region of its pole. If the path of the coil is such that it oscillates harmoniously along the axis OO' with an amplitude of $A=1$ mm (which is much smaller than the dimensions of the magnet and coil) and a frequency of $f=1000$ Hz, then an induced emf with an amplitude of $\mathcal{E}_0=5$ V is induced in it. What force will act on a stationary coil if a current of $I=200$ mA is passed through it?

When the coil moves along the axis of the magnet, the flux of magnetic induction through the coil changes. As a result, an induced emf $\mathcal{E}=N\Delta\Phi/\Delta t$ appears in it, where $N$ is the number of turns of the coil, $\Phi$ is the flux of magnetic induction through one turn.

To calculate $\Phi$ we proceed as follows. Let's stretch some surface onto a coil and count the number of magnetic field induction lines $\bar{B}$ intersecting this surface. In this case, it is convenient to use a surface in the form of a cylinder with a lid (Fig. 2). In this case, we will keep in mind that when mixing the coil, the cover remains in place, but the distance between it and the coil changes. Let the coil move down by $\Delta x$. Then a “belt” with an area of ​​$2\pi R\Delta x$ is added to the side surface of the cylinder, where $R$ is the radius of the coil. Corresponding to $\Phi$ is added $\Delta\Phi$ - magnetic flux through this “belt”:

$$\Delta\Phi=2\pi R\Delta x\cdot B_r,$$

where $B_r$ is the radial (i.e., perpendicular to the side surface of the cylinder) projection of the vector $\bar{B}$. Thus,

$$\mathcal{E}=N\cdot 2\pi R\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}B_r=N\cdot 2\pi RvB_r,$$

where $v$ is the coil speed. With harmonic oscillations, the velocity amplitude

$$v_0=\omega x_0=2\pi fA,$$

And

$$\mathcal{E}_0=N\cdot 2\pi R\cdot 2\pi fAB_r.$$

On the other hand, the required force is equal to

$$F=\sum\Delta F=\sum N\cdot I\Delta l\cdot B_r=N\cdot 2\pi RIB_r.$$

From the last two equalities we finally obtain

$$F=\frac{\mathcal{E}_0I}{2\pi fA}\approx 0,16\text{ H.}$$

The frequency of the reflected signal differs from the frequency of the emitter due to the Doppler effect. The maximum frequency shift will occur if the speed of the object reflecting radiovolts is directed exactly towards the radar (or away from it).

We will assume that the radar sends short radio pulses with a repetition frequency $f_0$, and the frequency of the pulses it receives is $f_1$. The time between arrivals at the radar antenna of the $n$th and $(n+1)$th pulses is equal to

$$T_n = \frac{2L_n}{v + c}, \quad T_{n+1} = \frac{2L_{n+1}}{v + c},$$
