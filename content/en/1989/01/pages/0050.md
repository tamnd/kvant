Let us introduce the parameter $\varepsilon$, equal to the ratio of the average interaction energy to the average kinetic energy: $\varepsilon=U/kT$. Now we can write down the conditions for the existence of gas, liquid and solid. If $\varepsilon\ll 1$, we have a gas (the molecules move quickly, with almost no interaction with each other). When $\varepsilon\gg 1$, the system is a solid (the molecules are “sandwiched” in their places). The intermediate case, when $\varepsilon\approx 1$, corresponds to a liquid. But within each of these very large classes of condition there is quite a lot of diversity.

Let us first focus on the solid body. From the condition $\varepsilon\gg 1$ it is clear that the state of a solid is determined mainly by the energetic interactions of molecules. As is known, any system presented to itself tends to occupy a position such that its potential energy is minimal (by potential energy here we must remember precisely the energy of interaction of molecules with each other). So, it turns out that the minimum energy corresponds to the state when the molecules are arranged strictly periodically. In other words, stable equilibrium corresponds not just to a solid body, but specifically to a crystal. This is the first, most studied type of solid. The properties of crystals are entirely determined by the type of crystal lattice. There are lattices composed of cups, hexagonal prisms, parallelepids, etc. When crystals are heated (for example, at atmospheric pressure), there is a temperature (attention! a very specific temperature) at which the crystal lattice becomes unstable. Swimming begins (it is clear that the swimming temperature must be determined from condition $\varepsilon\approx 1$, i.e. $kT_{\pi l}\approx U$).

Another type of solid will arise when, upon cooling, liquids lose their mobility before they have time to protrude into the crystal lattice. Now they would “want” to get organized, but they can’t. More precisely, they can, but for this they need a lot of time. We get a solid, but not a crystalline, but an amorphous body. A typical example of such bodies is glass. When heated, glass gradually softens and eventually turns into a liquid, but there is no specific melting point.

Whether cooling a given liquid produces a crystal or an amorphous body strongly depends on the cooling rate. For example, to obtain amorphous metals, the cooling rate must be enormous (molten metal is sprayed onto a surface cooled with liquid nitrogen). But this is not the only condition. For example, no matter how hard it melts, glycerin will not form a crystal (the reason for this is not entirely clear today).

If we are talking about bodies consisting of molecules of simple shape, then, apparently, there are no other possibilities. But fortunately, the world is not that simple. You know very well that there are limited (and not only limited) molecular properties of complex shapes. Substances built from such molecules can be in non-forming states that cannot be classified as either liquid or solid. Here are some examples.

A typical property of a liquid is its isotropy, i.e., the same properties in all directions. However, now it is now possible to use mechanical properties, the speed of propagation of various waves (elastic or electromagnetic), etc. So, about a hundred years ago, liquids that were not isotropic were discovered - the so-called anisotropic liquids.
