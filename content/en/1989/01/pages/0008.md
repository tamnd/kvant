I'll miss a big period. I will only mention a book on Davydov’s algebra with ingenious methods for solving maximum and minimum problems using elementary methods (i.e., without the use of differential calculus). For example, find the maximum of $ab$ if $a+b$ is given; find a rectangle of maximum area for a given period; find the maximum of the product of non-negative numbers $a_1a_2...a_n$, if their sum $a_1+a_2+...+a_n$ is given; squares are cut out of a square on a given side, and a box is made from the remaining part; How many squares should be cut to maximize the volume of the box?

I was greatly impressed by combinatorics and Newton’s biome, which I had been thinking about for a long time.

I lived in a small town with only one school. My mathematics teacher was a very kind, although seemingly stern man named Titarenko. He had a large Zaporozhye mustache. I have never met a better teacher, although I knew more than him, and he understood it. He loved me very much and encouraged me in every possible way. Encouraging is the most important thing for a teacher, isn't it?

The supply of books on mathematics was significant. I saw in advertisements that there were books on higher mathematics, and I imagined higher mathematics as something very interesting. My parents could not order these books - there was no money. But here I was lucky again. At the age of 15, I was taken to Odessa to have appendicitis operated on. I told my parents that I would not go to hospital until they bought me books on higher mathematics. The parents agreed and bought Belyaev’s higher mathematics course for colleges in Ukrainian. However, there was only enough money for the first part, with a presentation of differential calculus and analytical geometry on the plane.

I was lucky that I didn’t start with an in-depth university course. It was a very basic book. The level of Belyaev’s book can be judged by the introduction to it. It said, in particular, that there are three types of functions: analytical, defined by formulas; empirical, given by tables; correlational. I learned what correlation functions are many years later from a student studying probability theory.

On the third day after the operation, I took up this book and read it, interspersed with reading the novels of E. Zola, for nine days (then after such operations it was 12 days). During this time I read it.

From the book I got two great ideas. Firstly, any geometric problem on a plane and in space can be written down by formulas. However, I suspected this before. I also learned about the existence of such wonderful funcurds, such as the ellipse.

The second idea - it revolutionized my worldview - was that there is a formula for calculating sinhus: sin $x=x-\frac{x^3}{3!}+\frac{x^5}{5!}$ -...

Before this, I believed that there are two mathematics - algebraic and geometric, and that geometric mathematics belonged to the “transcendent” for algebraic. Take, for example, the formula for circularity - there is a “geometric” number l. Or, let's say, sinhus - it is determined purely geometrically.

When I discovered that sinhus can be written algebraically in the form of a series, the barrier fell, mathematics became unified. And now I perceive the various branches of mathematics together with mathematical physics as a single whole.

Of course, I was convinced that extreme problems are solved automatically, and although they lose their charm, you have in your hands a powerful apparatus for solving them.
