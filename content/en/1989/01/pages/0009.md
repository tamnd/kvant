⟦folio 7⟧

While studying differential calculus, I learned that there is also interral calculus related to areas and volumes. But I didn’t know what it consisted of: after all, I didn’t have the second volume of Belyaev’s course!

Here I want to remember one more task. When we studied the volumes of bodies of rotation at school the following fall, my classmate, the later famous mathematician D.P. Milman, drew my attention to the following problem: find the volume of a body formed by the rotation of a circle around a tangent

Presentation of an honorary doctorate degree from the University of Lyon to I. M. Gelfand. June 1984.

Noah. To solve this problem, I divided the circle into strips and calculated the sum of the differences in the volumes of the corresponding rotation cylinders. Thus I came to the need to find the sum

$$\cos \varphi + \cos 2\varphi + \cos 3\varphi + \dots + \cos n\varphi.$$

(*)

What happened next was, as always, a mixture of ingenuity and stupidity. I simplified the mismo of the elementary solution using general trigonometry, but used the formula $e^{i\varphi} = \cos \varphi + i \sin \varphi$ (this formula is called Euler's formula, but I didn't know that). I got this formula from the series for sine, cosine and $e^x$ that struck me deeply. Next, it remains to find the sum of the geometric progression $e^{i\varphi} + e^{2i\varphi} + \dots$ and get the sum (*) from it, which is what I did.

On this task, I got into the habit of thinking about the problem even after solving it. Namely: he moved the circle away from the straight line and realized that when rotated, a body is obtained that resembles a rubber circle, on which

*) $i$ — minimum unit ($i^2 = -1$); about complex numbers can be read, for example, in «Kvant» (1983, No. 2, p. 16).
