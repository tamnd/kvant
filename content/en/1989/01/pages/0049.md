But regarding reaction forces, we already know that they do not do any complete work. Therefore, for any virtual displacement, the total work done by external forces must also be equal to zero.

So, the equilibrium conditions of a mechanical system can be formulated in the form of the so-called principle of virtual (possible) displacements:

For the equilibrium of a mechanical system with ideal connections, it is necessary and sufficient that the sum of the work of all external forces acting on the system for any virtual displacements of the system is equal to zero.

Thanks to this principle, formulated by Johann Bernouli in 1717, to find equilibrium conditions there is no need to consider a large number of reaction forces. It is enough to select convenient virtual displacements, calculate the corresponding total work of only external forces and equate it to zero.

Let us illustrate the application of this principle with a simple example: we will find the elastic force of a spring from which a load of mass $m$ is suspended. Let's choose the load itself as the system. Two external forces are applied to it - the elastic force of the spring $T$ and the gravitational force of the load $mg$. Let's imagine that we have shifted the load in equilibrium by a small amount $\Delta x$ down. Then the work done by the gravitational force will be equal to

$$\Delta A_1 = mg\Delta x,$$

and the work done by the elastic force of the spring is

$$\Delta A_2 = -T\Delta x.$$

According to the principle of virtual movements, the sum of the work of both should be equal to zero:

$$\Delta A_1 + \Delta A_2 = mg\Delta x - T\Delta x = 0,$$

where do we get it from

$$T = mg.$$

This problem, of course, could be solved in the usual way - using the conditions of equilibrium of bodies. Moreover, in this case, both methods are the same in terms of complexity. However, in a number of cases, the method of virtual displacements leads to a faster and simpler solution, and sometimes even allows one to solve problems that, in principle, cannot be solved on the basis of general equilibrium conditions.

Note also that the method of virtual displacements can be successfully used in solving not only mechanical problems, but also problems of electronics and molecular physics.
