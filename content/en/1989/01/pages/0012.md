I went further and set myself a more general task: to express the sum of the $k$-th powers of the roots of the $n$-th degree algebraic equation through the coefficients of this equation. I solved this problem (the answer is called Newton's formula).

The second problem I was solving then arose when I discovered that cos $ix$ is a real number, or

$$\cos ix = 1 + \frac{x^2}{2!} + \frac{x^4}{4!} + \dots$$

I thought about this unexpected fact and proved the following promising theorem: every even real function takes real values ​​on the minimum axis.

To prove this, it was necessary to clarify what a “function” is. I thought about what to call a function, and accepted the following definition: a function is the sum of a convergent infinite degree of a series. After this, the proof of the theorem is almost obvious.

This task, apparently, was the last thing I thought about before my arrival in Moscow. I solved it in the summer of 1929, and in the next six months, which were very difficult for my family and for me, I had no time for mathematics.

The further period of my studies in Moscow, unlike what was described, was not a “pure experiment”. In Moscow I was already exposed to many different influences, and my development no longer followed its own path. During this period, as I already said, I studied on my own in the library named after. V.I. Lenin, surviving on official earnings. However, time was even a control in Leninka. I met some university mathematics students. One of them told me that my interest in expressions of the form $f(n+1)-f(n)$ is associated with a whole science called finite difference theory, and suggested that I need to read Nörlund’s book “Differenzenkalkül” on this topic. The book was in German, but I could understand it with the help of a dictionary.

I started going to university seminars, and then I found myself under heavy psychological pressure. I found that my style of doing math just wasn't working. New things have appeared in mathematics - new requirements regarding the rigor of proofs, great interest in the theory of functions of a real variable (TFV). (I note that today both this level of strictness and the TFDP are considered old-fashioned and outdated, but then...)

Then I realized that it is very important that a function does not have to be wrong, that a continuous function does not have to be differentiable, that a differentiable function does not have to be twice differentiable, etc.; that even if a function has arbitrary ones of all orders, then its Taylor series does not have to converge and that, even if it converges, its sum does not have to coincide with the value of the function! If this coincidence takes place, then such a function is called analytic, and this class of functions, the adherents of the TFDP argued, is so narrow that it is outside the main intervals of mathematics. And I only considered such functions!

Under the pressure of this point of view, I read a “modern, rigorous” textbook on analysis by Vallée Poussin, similar to those textbooks that are used today at the Moscow State University Mechanics, but better than them. Therefore, I have the understanding of a sympathetic first-year mechanite who is allowed to the beauties of mathematical analysis only after a one-year probationary period, a test of “rigorous justification” of the analysis.
