Images and displays

In order for an optical system to work, it is necessary that a beam of light rays emanating from an exact source (geometrically central) after passing through the system again turns into a central beam. Then an image of the source will appear in the center of the transformed beam. Moreover, an image can be created in two ways: either the rays of light themselves, leaving the optical system, are collected at one point - then they speak of a real image; it can be seen by placing a movie screen or photographic film at this point (Fig. 1, a); or the rays at the exit from the system form a diverging beam, but their continuations pass through one point - then the image is called

is minimal, although it is precisely what can be seen directly (Fig. 1, b). Finally, if the beam of rays entering the optical system is convergent (Fig. 1, c), then we speak of a minimum source. The higher the quality of the system, the more accurate the ray beam formula. Ideally, every exact source (real or minimal) A has an exact image $A'$ (also of one type or another). A mapping $p$ arises, which associates the point $A$ with the point $A'$. So we are set to find out how the device displays this.

Let's start with the simplest “optical system” - a flat mirror. There is hardly anything that needs to be explained here: it is very clear that the mapping $p$ is a symmetry with respect to the plane. This mapping can be considered for all points, including the “Through the Looking Glass” point: the minimal source in “Through the Looking Glass” creates a real image in front of the mirror (Fig. 2).

The second example is the refraction of light at a flat boundary of two media. Strictly speaking, no image is created here. In order for rays released from one point to form a central beam after refraction, they must be constant relative to tg $\varphi'$/tg $\varphi$, where $\varphi$ and $\varphi'$ are the angles made by the incident and refracted ray with the normal to the plane of separation of the media (see Fig. 3). But in reality, refraction occurs according to Sneglius’s law: sin $\varphi'$/sin $\varphi = k$. However, for rays almost perpendicular to the boundaries of the media, i.e. with small angles $\varphi$ and $\varphi'$, the sines in the last formula can be used with great accuracy

What about the display device $p$ for more complex optical systems? It turns out that this question can be answered almost without going into physical details. All we need is to proceed from the beginning of a point image of any accurate source.

For simplicity, we will propose that everything happens not in space, but on the $a$ plane - both the sources and their images are located on it.

In our examples, it could be any plane perpendicular to the surface of the mirror or the boundary of the media, and if we are considering a lens or a system of lenses that has an axis of symmetry, any plane passing through this axis. We will claim the thickness of our system; then it can be represented by a straight line $l$ on the plane $a$, upon meeting which the rays are refracted so that the central beam with center $A$ is transformed again into a central beam with center $A' = p(A)$ (Fig. 4, a).

Let's rotate the second copy along with all the images around the $l$ axis (Fig. 4, b) - we get the $a_1$ plane. Central projection and lens formula

Before reaping the fruits of our constructions in relation to lenses, we will make an important reservation. Rays released from one source are focused by a lens to a point on the lens when they pass down from the main optical axis (a straight line passing perpendicular to the lens through its center). We allow an idealization by considering the mapping $p$ on the entire plane. But the truth does not suffer from this, because all our reasoning can be repeated with the same success for any, no matter how small, area on the plane.

So, let’s apply our construction to the lens. Let $a$ be its main optical axis, $a_1$ the corresponding straight line in the $a_1$ plane, $P$ the center of the projection $p_1$. It is clear that all lines parallel to $a$, with this projection will turn into lines passing through the same point $F_1$ of line $a_1$ (Fig. 6) - a parallel projection of point $P$ onto the plane $a_1$ along line $a$. Consequently, a beam of rays parallel to the main axis, having passed the lens, converges at point $F'$ of the main axis (Fig. 7), corresponding to point $F_1$: $OF' = OF_1$, where $O$ is the center of the lens. Similarly, the rays emerging from the point $F$ - projection $P$ onto the plane $\alpha$ along the straight line $a_1$, having passed the lens, become parallel to the main axis. Points $F$ and $F_1$ are called the focal points of the lens. It can be proven that for a single lens system the focal lengths $f = OF$ and $f' = OF'$ are the same; for more complex systems they may be different.

Knowing the position of the foci, it is easy to construct an image of any point $A$ that does not lie on the main axis by following two rays originating from it - a ray parallel to the axis and a ray passing through the focus $F$ (Fig. 8). This construction is well known. Let us emphasize once again that we necessarily came to it, essentially based only on our “postulate of a dot image.” figure 8 shows how the image of point $B$ is constructed on the main axis. Here we take advantage of the fact that the image of the line $AB$, perpendicular to this axis, is the line $A'B'$, also perpendicular to it, a fact obvious from considering our central projection. Other properties of the image created by an ideal line are just as easily established using central projection.

Parallel projections, as is easy to understand, are expressed by linear functions $y=ax+b$. And since the composition of any number of linear and fractional-linear functions will again be a fractional-linear function $^*$), any projective transformation of a line is expressed in coordinates by a fractional-linear function.

From here, by the way, it follows that the design transformation of a straight line is completely determined by the images of three points. Indeed, using the coordinates of these points and their images, three equations are compiled for the coefficients $a$, $b$, $c$, $d$ corresponding to the linear fractional function. Thus, the coefficients will be specified up to multipliers, and the function itself will be specified uniquely. In the same way, knowing the coordinates of two points $A$, $B$ and their images $A'$, $B'$ under parallel projection, we could find the corresponding linear function, and with its help, the image $C'$ of any other point $C$. However, the point $C'$ can be constructed in a much simpler way: with a parallel projection, the relation in which the point divides the segment remains unchanged, or invariant, therefore $A'C':C'B'=AC:CB$. (Here and below we assume that the segments are directed; the equality $AC:CB=k$ means that $\vec{AC}=k\vec{CB}$.) The central projection does not preserve the relation $AC:CB$; the corral preserves the relation of two such relations. This quantity is called the double ratio of four points, denoted (ABCD) and is equal by definition

$$\left(ABCD\right)=\frac{AC}{CB}:\frac{AD}{DB}.$$

To prove the invariance of the double relation, consider the lines a, b, c, d passing through the common point P (Fig. 12) and an arbitrary line l intersecting them at points A, B, C, D. Let us check that $(ABCD) = A_1C_1/B_1C_1$, where A_1, B_1, C_1 are the intersection points of the line l_1, parallel to d, with a, b and c. Obviously, the ratios A_1C_1/B_1C_1 are the same for all such lines, so we can assume that l_1 passes through C, i.e. C_1 = C. From the similarity of triangles, which the reader can easily find for himself on figure 12, we have

$$\frac{AC}{AD} = \frac{A_1C}{PD}, \quad \frac{CB}{DB} = \frac{B_1C}{PD}.$$

Then

$$\frac{AC}{CB} : \frac{AD}{DB} = \frac{AC}{AD} : \frac{CB}{DB} = \frac{A_1C}{PD} : \frac{B_1C}{PD} = \frac{A_1C}{B_1C} = \frac{A_1C_1}{B_1C_1}.$$

So, for given lines a, b, c, d, the double relation $(ABCD)$ does not depend on the line l, which means it is preserved under the central projection.

Let us now know the images A', B' and C' of the points A, B, C under some transformation. Then the image D' of any other point D is found from the correspondence $(A'B'C'D') = (ABCD)$ or $A'D'/D'B' = (ABCD) \cdot (A'C'/C'B')$. If we write these equalities in coordinates, we will receive another confirmation that the projective transformation is expressed by a linear fractional function, and at the same time the method is found for this function. As an exercise, the reader is invited to check by direct calculation the invariance of the double relation with respect to linear functions and functions 1/x, and therefore any fractional linear function (since any fractional linear function is also a complex function!).

Let us return once again to the lens, the corresponding projective transformation p of its main optical axis and its analytical expression - the lens formula. It turns out that this formula can also be derived using a double relation. Let's imagine that an imaginary infinite distant point U is added to the main axis, the coordinate of which will be equal to ∞. Conditions that all straight lines parallel to the axis pass through this point. Then it should be considered as an image of a function F, and its own image will be the second function F'. So, we know where three points go when transforming p: the center of the lens - p(O) = O, the function - p(F) = U and the infinite distant point - p(U) = F'. the coordinates of the point O, F, F' and U are equal to 0, f, f' and ∞, respectively. If r and r' are the coordinates of point A and its image A', then

$$(OFUA) = \frac{\infty - 0}{f - \infty} : \frac{r - 0}{f - r} = -\frac{f - r}{r} = 1 - \frac{f}{r},$$

$$(OUF'A') = \frac{f' - 0}{\infty - f} : \frac{r' - 0}{\infty - r'} = \frac{f'}{r'}.$$

Equating these expressions (the double ratio is preserved!), we obtain the lens formula. It is easy to justify the validity of these trios: you just need to go to the limit in the equality $(OBCA) = (OB'C'A')$ as B→F, OC→∞. However, in the next section we see that the addition of an infinite distant point has a deep meaning, and attributing is not a purely formal acceptance, a border with mystification.
