Variations on a Theme of Euclid

Readers of «Kvant» probably remember how Euclid proves that there are many prime numbers of the infinite: if $p_1, \dots, p_n$ are primes, then the number $E = p_1 p_2 \dots p_n + 1$ is not divisible by any of the numbers $p_i$. Therefore, the environments of its divisors are new prime numbers.

Is it possible to apply this elegant argument to another problem—the problem of irreducible polynomials?

A Gochelen with integral coefficients is called irreducible if it cannot be represented as a product of polynomials with integral coefficients. For example, $x^3 - x^2 + x - 5$ is an irreducible polynomial, and $x^3 - x^2 + x - 6$ is a reducible one. The question arises: do irreducible polynomials of arbitrarily high degree exist?

In two Euclides it is easy to prove that there are infinitely many irreducible polynomials: if $p_1(x), \dots, p_n(x)$ are irreducible polynomials, then $E(x) = p_1(x) p_2(x) \dots p_n(x) + 1$ is not divisible by any of the polynomials $p_i(x)$. Therefore, the environments of its divisors are new irreducible polynomials.
