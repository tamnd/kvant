ANSWERS, ANSWERS...

If you are interested in questions from Ya. I. Perelman’s book “Do you know physics?” (see “Quant” No. 7, p. 44), you certainly wanted to find answers to them. Now we give you the opportunity to compare your answer with the author's.

1. On an uninhabited island. “Are there any trees growing on this tropical island?” - asks the author of a German book dedicated to the analysis of Edison's quiz. The question is separate, because there are no trees available for overturning the rock: this can be done literally with your bare hands.

Let's calculate the thickness of the rock, which is suspiciously not mentioned in the problem, and immediately clarify the matter. With a total rock mass of 3 tons and a granite density of 3 g/cm³, we report that the volume of the rock is 1 m³. And since the length of the rock is 30 m (100 feet), the height is about 5 m (15 feet), then its thickness is approximately 7 mm. Consequently, a thin wall, only 7 mm thick, rose on the island.

To knock down such a wall (unless it extends deep into the soil), it is enough to rest your hands or shoulder on it. Let us calculate the magnitude of the force required for this, denoting it by F. The point of application of this force is at the height of the person’s shoulders (1.5 m). The moment of force F relative to the “tipping axis” is equal to F×1.5 N·m. The leaning person is counteracted by the force of the gravity of the rock, applied at the center of its gravity and tending to pull the turning wall back, in position mode. The moment of this force relative to the same axis is equal to 3·10³×10×3.5·10⁻³ N·m. The magnitude of the force F is determined from the equality of the total moments: F = 70 N.

This means that by pushing against the wall with a force of only 70 N, a person will topple the rock.

2. In a hot air balloon. The ball will not remain alone. While a person is climbing the stairs, the balloon will descend. What happens here is the same thing that occurs when you walk along a light boat moored to the shore to get out onto the land: the boat retreats back under your feet. In the same way, a ladder, pushed by the feet of a person climbing it, will drag the arostat to the ground.

The transition to solving this problem from the point of view of the laws of mechanics, we must think like this. A ball with a ladder and a person on it represent an isolated system, the center of gravity of which cannot be moved by the action of internal forces. It will retain its position unchanged when a person climbs the stairs only when the ball itself goes down - otherwise the center of gravity would rise.

As for the magnitude of the ball’s movement, it is the same amount less than the height of the person’s lifting, as many times the ball is heavier than the person.

3. Plastic at the bottom of a container with liquid. In a vessel with water, wooden plastic placed on the bottom flares up because, of course, water penetrates under the plastic. It remains to be explained why water penetrates under wooden plastic, but does not penetrate under glass plastic.

It must be kept in mind that no matter how tightly the plastic fits to the bottom, there will inevitably remain a thin gap between them. At the edges of these tests of fused surfaces, water, wetting both wood and glass, forms a concavity towards the liquid-free layer; this concavity, like a concave meniscus, draws water into the gap between the plate and the bottom.

The situation is different in the case of mercury and glass plastic. Glass does not wet mercury, so the glass plastic and the glass bottom of the mercury form a bulge formed towards the liquid-free

4. Kolladan's task. Since the surface of the river is an inclined plane, one might think that moving a vessel upstream requires the same amount of work as sliding a body up an inclined plane. But one should not lose sight of the fact that the gravity of a ship floating in the water is balanced by the buoyant force of the water, so lifting the floating ship to a higher level does not require any force. The work of such a lift is zero; it should not be taken into account.

It is curious that of the students at the academy where the problem was proposed, only one gave the correct solution, who later became a famous railway engineer in France.

5. Water in an overturned glass. It is a mistake to believe that the glass contains only water and no air at all, since the paper is adjacent to the water closely. There is certainly air there. If there were no layer of air between two flat objects in contact, we could not lift a single thing from the table that rests on the table with a flat base: we would have to overcome atmospheric pressure. By covering the surface of the water with a piece of paper, we always have a thin layer of air between them.

Let's see what happens when the glass is turned upside down. Under heavy water, the paper protrudes slightly downward, so that some space under the bottom of the glass is freed up for the small amount of air that was between the water and the paper. This space is larger than the initial one; The air, therefore, becomes rarefied and its pressure drops.

Now the piece of paper is acted upon by: from the outside - full atmospheric pressure, from the inside - incomplete atmospheric pressure and flat water pressure. Both pressures, disturbed and internal, are balanced. Enough

Apply a small number of cilia to the piece of paper in order to overcome the force of application (determined by the surface tension of the liquid film) - and the piece of paper will fall off.

The protrusion of the paper by the weight of the water should be clean. When the space enclosed by the air increases by 0.01 part, the air pressure in the glass decreases by the same part. The insufficient hundredth fraction of atmospheric pressure is covered by the weight of 10 cm of water column. If the layer of air between the paper and the water was initially 0.1 mm thick, then its thickness will sufficiently increase by 0.01 × 0.1, i.e. by 0.001 mm (one micron), to explain the holding of the paper at the edges of the inverted glass. There is no point in trying to catch this protrusion of the piece of paper directly with the eye.

6. Siphon in the void. When asked whether a siphon can transfer liquid in a vacuum, the answer is usually a categorical “no.” I received this answer both when I graduated, and from high school teachers, and sometimes from higher school professors. Most school textbooks and a significant part of university textbooks unconditionally consider air pressure to be the only reason for the transfusion of liquid into sphons. Meanwhile, this is a physical prejudice.

How can we explain the work of the sfon without relying on atmospheric pressure? Let me quote the corresponding lines from my “Technical Physics” (1927):

“Since the right side of the liquid thread of the sfon is longer, and therefore heavier than the left one, it drags and solidifies the liquid all the time in the tube towards the long end. This is clearly encroached upon by comparison with a rope thrown over a block.”

7. Heating of the carpentry level. When asked about the problem, they will answer that the level of dust in warm weather is greater than in cold weather, since the gas contained in it expands under the influence of heat. They forget, however, that under these conditions the gas cannot expand: the liquid enclosed in the tube does this. All parts of the level heat up: the solid frame, the glass tube, the liquid, the gas in the bubble. The expansion of the frame and tube is very slight; the expansion of the liquid is greater than the expansion of the tube and therefore the bubble is reduced.

So, the level bubble in warm weather is smaller than in cold weather.

9. Why is snow white? Snow is white for the same reason that crushed glass and, in general, crushed transparent substances appear white. Crush the ice in a mortar or crush it with a knife and you will get a white powder. This color is due to the fact that rays of light, penetrating into small pieces of transparent ice, do not pass through them, but are reflected inside at the edges of the ice floes and air (total internal reflection) until they come out in an arbitrary direction.
