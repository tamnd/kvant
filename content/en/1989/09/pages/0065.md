Problem 3. During the Great Oppositions, the distance from Earth to Mars is minimal and is $r=56 \cdot 10^6$ km. What is the maximum distance from Earth to Mars at opposition?

Sweeping. In Problem 2 we silently treated orbits as if they were circular. Now this will no longer work - after all, in the assumption of circular orbits, where does the great opposition come from? However, the eccentricity of the earth's orbit is relatively small, and we will still consider it circular. But the orbit of Mars, of course, is no longer a circle.

The data that we will unpack: the rotation periods of the Earth $-T_0 \approx 365$ days and Mars $-T_M \approx 687$ days and the radius of the Earth’s orbit $R_0 \approx 1,5 \cdot 10^8$ km $=1$ a. e. (the value $R_0$ is used in astronomy as a unit of distance - this is 1 astronomical unit). First, from Kepler's III law we find the major axis of Mars' orbit:

$$2a_M=2R_0(T_M/T_0)^{2/3} \approx 3,05 \text{ а. е. } \approx 4,57 \cdot 10^8 \text{ км.}$$

And then it’s enough to look at figure 3 to understand that the required distance

$$L=2a_M-2R_0-l \approx 10^8 \text{ км.}$$

(Now, on the contrary, rounding the rough. The exact answer, i.e., the reference book data, is 97.5 $\cdot 10^6$ km.)

In this solution we assumed that the orbital planes of the Earth and Mars coincide. This is quite justified, since in reality the corresponding angle is less than $2^\circ$. In general, the entire planetary system is quite flat. The maximum inclination to the ecliptic (i.e., to the plane of the earth's orbit) is for the orbit of Pluto $-17^\circ$, noticeably less for Mercury $-7^\circ$, for the other planets it does not exceed $3,5^\circ$. This does not apply to comets. And that is exactly what we are doing now.
