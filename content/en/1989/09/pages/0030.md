No, they're wrong!

Let's answer together: guys, you are wrong! You, of course, solved the problem, but... completely different. Namely: you took two possible paths from point $A$ to point $B$ and determined under what conditions which path would be shorter. But besides these two paths, many other paths are possible (Fig. 3), going from point $A$ along the lateral surface to an arbitrary point $P$ on the circle of the upper base, and from point $P$ to point $B$ - along a straight line segment on the upper base. As you can see, the routes proposed by the schoolchildren represent only partial cases of the proposed path: for the first student, the point $P$ coincides with the point $M$, for the second it coincides with $B$, i.e. the schoolchildren proposed two “extreme” options. Well, the truth is undoubtedly somewhere in the middle.

Let's figure it out.

So, let's take the shortest path. If we denote the center of the upper base by $O$, and the angle $MOP$ by $\varphi$, then we can easily determine that the length of the arc $MP$ is equal to $R\varphi$, the length of the shortest line $AP$ is equal to $\sqrt{H^2 + R^2\varphi^2}$ (by the Pythagorean theorem); and the length of the segment $PB$ is equal to $2R\cos \frac{\varphi}{2}$. Thus, the path length is a function of $\varphi$ and is equal to

$$S = \sqrt{H^2 + R^2\varphi^2} + 2R\cos \frac{\varphi}{2}.$$

All that remains is to find the minimum of this function on the section $\varphi \in [0; \pi]$.

It is known how to do this. The minimum can be reached either at one of the ends of the interval, or somewhere in the middle. As for the ends, this part of the problem was considered by the schoolchildren themselves (their routes correspond to the values ​​$\varphi = 0$ and $\varphi = \pi$). How to find the minimum of a function inside an interval? Here, differential calculus provides a universal method. Let's take an arbitrary one:

$$S' = \frac{R^2\varphi}{\sqrt{H^2 + R^2\varphi^2}} - R\sin \frac{\varphi}{2}.$$

Those points of the interval $(0; \pi)$ at which this arbitrary value vanishes or is not defined (however, the latter is impossible in this case) are points suspected of extremum: if a function has local maxima or minima on this segment, then they can only be achieved at such points. Let’s equate an arbitrary one to zero:

$$\frac{R^2\varphi}{\sqrt{H^2 + R^2\varphi^2}} - R\sin \frac{\varphi}{2} = 0,$$

or, after simplification:

$$\frac{R\varphi}{\sqrt{H^2 + R^2\varphi^2}} = \sin \frac{\varphi}{2}.$$

It remains to find from here all $\varphi$ included in the interval $(0; \pi)$, determine for these $\varphi$ the values ​​$S$ and among all $S(\varphi)$ obtained in this way, as well as the end values ​​$S(0)$ and $S(\pi)$, select the most suitable one. And no problems!
