If on its way a powerful sound wave crosses the path of another wave (signal), it will “remember” this meeting, i.e. its characteristics will change. In other words, a powerful beam serves as a kind of applied antenna. Imagine: it is enough to increase the power of the sound emitted, for example, by the input, and we will get a receiving hydroacoustic antenna with a length of tens and even hundreds of meters. Such an antenna will be the area in which the acoustic beam is concentrated, that is, the space between the emitter and the receiver. Of course, nothing like this can be obtained for weak waves. It is known that two linear waves pass freely through each other, creating an interference pattern in the area of ​​intersection. Leaving the interference region, each wave behaves exactly the same as if there was no other wave. A powerful beam can serve not only as an application antenna, but also as a radiating antenna. Devices that emit sound using such antennas are called parametric radiators. What are these devices needed for?

It is known that the only type of radiation that can travel long distances underwater is sound. Without active connection it is impossible to explore the World Ocean and use its riches. But for directed radiation of low-frequency sound with a narrow beam, very large antennas with a reflecting surface (mirror) diameter of tens of meters are needed. The problems of making huge radiating antennas can be circumvented by using nonlinear wave interaction. To do this, an antenna of normal size must emit two powerful sound waves at the input with frequencies $f_1$ and $f_2$. These two waves will interact with each other until they die out, for example, at a distance of one kilometer from the emitter. As a result of the interaction of two waves, a signal is generated at a low (different) frequency $f_1 - f_2$, which attenuates much more weakly and can propagate over very long distances. But the most important thing is that this birth signal is not on the surface of the antenna (there are only signals with frequencies $f_1$ and $f_2$), but in the water column. Thus, the kilometer-long column of water in which the interaction occurs is a giant radiating antenna. It does not need to be built - it arises on its own.

Parametric emitters have found application in geophysics, medicine, and atmospheric research. But antennas of this type have proven to be especially useful for marine research. They made it possible to study with great accuracy the bottom topography and the properties of the soil in the bottom layers of the sea. “Parametrics” has found an interesting application in archaeology; with its help they tried to find the valuables taken by Napoleon from the Kremlin and abandoned by him during his flight in 1812 in the swampy, flooded lakes near Smolensk; Items belonging to polar expeditions of the first years of Arctic exploration were found...

Nowadays, mass industrial production of fish-based active locators has already been organized, which make it possible to detect accumulations of fish at the surface or at the bottom of the sea, at the mouths of rivers, in shallow waters - where conventional hydroacoustics are ineffective.

In this article we have only tried to identify some of the many effects occurring in powerful active fields.

Nonlinear actuation is a young science; it has been developing for a little more than a few years. There is plenty to keep a young researcher interested in the physics of nonlinear processes and its applications.
