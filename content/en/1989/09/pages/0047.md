6. Siphon in the void. When asked whether a siphon can transfer liquid in a vacuum, the answer is usually a categorical “no.” I received this answer both when I graduated, and from high school teachers, and sometimes from higher school professors. Most school textbooks and a significant part of university textbooks unconditionally consider air pressure to be the only reason for the transfusion of liquid into sphons. Meanwhile, this is a physical prejudice.

How can we explain the work of the sfon without relying on atmospheric pressure? Let me quote the corresponding lines from my “Technical Physics” (1927):

“Since the right side of the liquid thread of the sfon is longer, and therefore heavier than the left one, it drags and solidifies the liquid all the time in the tube towards the long end. This is clearly encroached upon by comparison with a rope thrown over a block.”

7. Heating of the carpentry level. When asked about the problem, they will answer that the level of dust in warm weather is greater than in cold weather, since the gas contained in it expands under the influence of heat. They forget, however, that under these conditions the gas cannot expand: the liquid enclosed in the tube does this. All parts of the level heat up: the solid frame, the glass tube, the liquid, the gas in the bubble. The expansion of the frame and tube is very slight; the expansion of the liquid is greater than the expansion of the tube and therefore the bubble is reduced.

So, the level bubble in warm weather is smaller than in cold weather.

9. Why is snow white? Snow is white for the same reason that crushed glass and, in general, crushed transparent substances appear white. Crush the ice in a mortar or crush it with a knife and you will get a white powder. This color is due to the fact that rays of light, penetrating into small pieces of transparent ice, do not pass through them, but are reflected inside at the edges of the ice floes and air (total internal reflection) until they come out in an arbitrary direction.
