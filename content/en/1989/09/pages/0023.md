A wave so that its shape is noticeably distorted is called the length of the gap formed. Like any nonlinear effect, profile distortion depends on the amplitude of the wave $p_0$ - the length of the discontinuity formed is inversely proportional to the amplitude, i.e. $l_p \sim 1/p_0$. The stronger the wave, the greater its amplitude, the faster the profile is distorted. If the attenuation is strong enough and occurs at distances $l_3$, less than $l_p$, the nonlinearity may be suppressed and not appear at all. Naturally, $l_3$, just like $l_p$, depends on the properties of the medium in which the sound propagates.

Now we can clarify the definition of a powerful acoustic wave: it is a wave with $l_p < l_3$. The ratio $l_3/l_p$ is called the acoustic Reynolds number and is denoted by Re.

When $Re > 10$, the wave is strong; at $Re \ll 1$ - weak. The Reynolds number is equal to $Re = \alpha p_0/f$, where $f$ is the frequency of the sound, and the coefficient $\alpha$ is some constant that characterizes the non-particle and viscosity properties of the medium (the way the medium “responds” to a powerful signal, the extent to which it distorts it). The values ​​of $\alpha$ are different for different environments; for water $\alpha \approx 300$ ($\Pi a \cdot c$)$^{-1}$. When detuning in water, a sound with a frequency of $f \sim 1$ MHz, the condition $Re > 10$ is satisfied for waves with a sound pressure amplitude of $p_0 > 3 \cdot 10^4$ Pa. Thus, a strong sonorous wave in water is a wave with intensity $I = p_0^2/2\rho c > \frac{(3 \cdot 10^4)^2}{2 \cdot 10^3 \cdot 1,5 \cdot 10^3} \cdot Bt/m^2 = 300 \cdot Bt/m^2$; the corresponding sound pressure level is $N > 180$ dB.
