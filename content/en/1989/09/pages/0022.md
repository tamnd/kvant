And the second question: what are nonlinear effects, what is necessary or needed in them, where can they find application?

Let's try to answer these questions. But first, let's remember what a sound wave is.

A sound wave is represented by areas of rolling and rarefaction propagating in a medium. At each point in the medium there will be a change in pressure caused by a change in the degree of rolling. This change in pressure to the average pressure that exists in the medium in the absence of a sound wave is called sound pressure.

A sound wave carries with it energy - the potential energy of deformation (if sound propagates in the atmosphere, then this is the energy of deformation of air) and the kinetic energy of moving particles (air). This energy flows in the direction in which the wave is propagating. The energy flow that passes per unit time through a unit area perpendicular to the direction of wave propagation characterizes the intensity of the sound wave.

It is clear that both intensity $I$ and sound pressure $p$ depend on the properties of the medium in which the sound propagates. We will not derive these dependencies; we will only present a formula connecting $p$, $I$ and the characteristics of the medium - density $\rho$ and the speed of sound in the medium $c$:

$$I = p_0^2/2\rho c$$

($p_0$ - sound pressure amplitude).

Now let’s move on to the question of what “strong” and “weak” sound is.

Listening to sound is pleasantly determined by the sound pressure level - a value that is related to the amplitude of the sound pressure and varies in decibels (dB):

$$N = 20 \lg(p/p_n), \text{ДБ.}$$

Here $p$ is the pressure whose level we are interested in, $p_n$ is the threshold sound pressure, conventionally assumed to be $2 \cdot 10^{-5}$ Pa. The pressure $p_n$ applied corresponds to the intensity $I_n = 10^{-12} \text{Bt/m}^2$ of a very weak sound that can be perceived by the human ear in the air at a fraction of 1000 Hz.

The higher the sound pressure level, the stronger the sound.

Subjective ideas about the strength of sound are associated with the concept of “loudness,” i.e., they are related to the range of frequencies perceived by the ear (see table). But what if the sound frequency lies outside this range, in the ultrasound region? It is in this situation, during experiments with ultrasound at frequencies of the order of 1 MHz, that it is easiest to observe nonlinear effects in laboratory conditions.

Thus, we have, as it were, closed the circle: it makes sense to call those active waves for which nonlinear effects become noticeable powerful.

Let us now turn to the nonlinear effects themselves. It is known that a general (linear) sound wave of small amplitude propagates in a medium without changing its shape. In this case, both the rolling regions and the rarefaction regions move in space at the same speed - this is the speed of sound in the medium. If a sound source generates (emits), for example, a sinusodal wave, its profile remains sinusodal at any distance from the source. In a powerful sound wave, the picture is the same: the regions where the ramp medium (sound pressure is positive) move at a speed greater than the speed of sound, and the rarefaction regions move at a speed less than the speed of sound in this medium. As a result, the wave profile is distorted - the front chips become larger, and the rear ones become flatter. A similar picture is observed for sea waves near the shore: smooth swell waves in shallow water change their shape, and in the surf zone, before collapsing, they approach the front front.
