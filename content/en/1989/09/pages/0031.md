How can this be? There is only one way out - to find some good option. First of all, we note: if a function has a local minimum at some point, this means that to the left of this point it kills, and to the right it increases, therefore its derivative on the left is negative, at the point itself it is zero, and on the right it is positive: in other words, the derivative in the lower case is zero). If it is strictly negative, then we can say for sure that there is no local minimum at this point (but, of course, there is a maximum).

Let's find the second derivative:

$$S'' = \frac{R^2 H^2}{(\sqrt{H^2 + R^2 \varphi^2})^3} - \frac{R}{2} \cos \frac{\varphi}{2}. \tag{2}$$

Let us find out its sign for those $\varphi$ for which the first derivative vanishes, i.e. for which equality (1) is true. But how to do this? A “cunning move” is proposed: let’s express the quantities $\sqrt{H^2 + R^2 \varphi^2}$ and $H^2$ from equation (1):

$$\sqrt{H^2 + R^2 \varphi^2} = \frac{R \varphi}{\sin \frac{\varphi}{2}},$$

$$H^2 = \left( \frac{R \varphi}{\sin \frac{\varphi}{2}} \right)^2 - R^2 \varphi^2 =$$

$$= R^2 \varphi^2 \left( \frac{1}{\sin^2 \frac{\varphi}{2}} - 1 \right) = R^2 \varphi^2 \frac{\cos^2 \frac{\varphi}{2}}{\sin^2 \frac{\varphi}{2}}.$$

Let's substitute these values ​​into (2):

$$S'' = \frac{R^2 \cdot R^2 \varphi^2 \cos^2 \frac{\varphi}{2}}{\sin^2 \frac{\varphi}{2}} - \frac{R}{2} \cos \frac{\varphi}{2} =$$

$$= \frac{R}{2 \varphi} \cos \frac{\varphi}{2} \cdot (2 \cos \frac{\varphi}{2} \sin \frac{\varphi}{2} - \varphi) =$$

$$= \frac{R}{2 \varphi} \cos \frac{\varphi}{2} (\sin \varphi - \varphi).$$

If $0 < \varphi < \pi$, then the set in front of the bracket is positive, and therefore $S''$ matches the sign of the expression $(\sin \varphi - \varphi)$. But, as you know, if $0 < \varphi < \pi$, then $\sin \varphi < \varphi$; whence it follows that $\sin \varphi - \varphi < 0$ at $\varphi \in (0; \pi)$. So, if the function $S$ has an extremum on this interval, then it can only be a maximum, but not a minimum.

But the schoolchildren are right!

Consequently, the minimum value $S$ is achieved at one of the ends of the segment $[0; \pi]$, and, therefore, the students have the correct answer for perfect. That is, they are right.

So much for you! But what we objected to them is also true! Paradox?

No, more like a happy accident. In this problem, the students were simply lucky: the wrong solution led to the correct answer. It must be said that such cases are not so rare: probably everyone remembers something of this kind from their practice. In general, this statics was written to remind you just once: even when using the most advanced and reliable methods (such as the sweep method when finding the shortest path), you need to be careful and critical of your reasoning. Otherwise, you can get into trouble.
