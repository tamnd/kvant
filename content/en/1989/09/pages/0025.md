⟦folio 23⟧

Let's try to estimate what maximum intensity can be transmitted in water over a distance of 100 m by a sound wave with a frequency of 1 MHz:

$$I_{\text{преД}} = \frac{p^2_{\text{преД}}(x=100 \text{ м})}{2c\rho} = \frac{8f^2}{c\rho\alpha^2} e^{-2x/l_3},$$

and substituting $c \approx 1,5 \cdot 10^3$ m/s, $\rho \approx 10^3$ kg/m$^3$, $\alpha \approx 300$ (Pa s)$^{-1}$, $l_3 \approx 50$ m, we get $I_{\text{преД}} \approx 1$ W/m$^2$. Thus, under conditions suitable for the generation of powerful ultrasonic radiation (in water), at a distance of about 100 m from the source, very little energy, on the order of one joule, can be transmitted to an applied antenna with an area of ​​one square meter in one second. This energy is enough to light a flashlight, but obviously not enough to damage the ship or injure the sperm whale.

How, then, did the many technical applications of ultrasound that we mentioned earlier—hole drilling, ultrasonic cleaning, etc.—come about? The fact is that these operations are carried out at relatively short distances from the emitter, where nonlinear attenuation has not yet had time to noticeably weaken the powerful wave and efficiency has not yet occurred.

The teacher may ask: how to explain the strong impact of shock waves on the transition? It is known that explosive shock waves can destroy structures located quite far from the explosion site. But a shock wave is a highly nonlinear formation, and therefore attenuation due to nonlinear effects should occur even faster than for weak waves observed in problems of nonlinear acoustics.

The thing is that a single pulse signal (Fig. 2) behaves differently than a periodic one (compare figure 1 and 2). Its peak value decreases with distance from the source according to the law

$$p_0(x) = \frac{p_0}{\sqrt{1+x/l_p}}.$$

For large initial amplitudes $p_0$ the unity under the root of the denominator can be neglected; in these cases, the amplitude of a single signal at the observation point, say at the transmission, depends on the amplitude at the point of radiation (explosion) and the distance as

$$p_0(x) = p_0 \sqrt{l_p/x} \sim \sqrt{p_0/x}.$$

For us, the dependence on the initial amplitude $p_0$ is important. We see that with a strong manifestation of nonlinear effects in the case of a shock wave, the maximum value of $p_0(x)$ is not limited at some limiting level, but only depends more slowly on its initial value (proportional to $\sqrt{p_0}$, and not $p_0$ as in the linear case). This means that by increasing the amplitude of the wave (for example, the force of the explosion), we can achieve greater pressure on the continuity at a given (even large) distance and thereby destroy it.

Until now we have been talking about the change in the shape of a powerful acoustic wave and the decrease in its amplitude as it propagates through a medium. But we said nothing about the most important thing - how its spectral composition changes. This issue is very important for the application.
