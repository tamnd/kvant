⟦folio 22⟧

All that remains is to overcome technical difficulties. However, there are fundamental difficulties that make the idea of ​​​​creating ultrasonic weapons hopeless. It turns out that for any given distance there is a limiting value of the intensity of the sound wave, reaching an integer, and this intensity is lower, the greater the distance to the integer. And the point is not at all in the usual attenuation of acoustic waves during the passage of an absorbing medium, which occurs according to the law $p_x = p_0 e^{-x/l_3}$.

Typically, the attenuation for acoustic waves is less, the higher the sound frequency, $-l_3 \sim f^{-2}$, i.e., the attenuation effect is noticeably heard with increasing frequency. However, we can choose the frequency in such a way that the total (linear) attenuation at the distances we need will be negligible.*) Now imagine that in some places in space $(x=0)$ we specify (generate) a wave with precisely such a frequency and with a large amplitude, such that nonlinear effects will significantly affect the surface of the wave. The change in the shape of one period of a wave during its propagation is shown in figure 1.

We see that as long as the wave is at distances $x \leq l_p$, it does not decay at all. But at $x > l_p$ nonlinear attenuation begins. The amplitude of the wave decreases with distance from the source as

$$p_0(x > l_p) = \frac{p_0}{1 + x/l_p}$$

*) For a signal with a frequency of 1 MHz in water $l_3 \approx 50$ m, and $l_p$ for ultrasound with a sufficiently large amplitude can be only 10 cm.

— the faster, the greater the initial amplitude value $p_0$. For very large values ​​of the initial amplitude, the unit in the sign can be neglected in comparison with other terms; the amplitude is killed by the proportional $1/x$, and, since $l_p \sim 1/p_0$, the rate of decrease does not depend on the magnitude of the initial signal $p_0$. This process continues until (up to such distances from the source) the wave is attenuated so strongly that the non-linear effects “stop”, and after that the wave goes into a linear mode of propagation. Further attenuation occurs according to the laws of linear acoustics, i.e. it is a weaker horizon and does not depend on the magnitude of the initial disturbance.

It is possible to obtain an expression for the limiting (maximum) value of the amplitude of a sinusoidal wave (at the entrance to the medium) under the combined action of both nonlinear effects (attenuation due to the formation of a circular wave front) and general linear attenuation, characterized by the attenuation coefficient $1/l_3$:

$$\lim_{p_0 \to \infty} p_0(x > l_p) = p_{\text{пред}}(x) = \frac{4f}{\alpha} e^{-x/l_3}.$$

Once again we draw attention to the fact that the amplitude of the signal $p_0(x)$ at a distance $x \gg l_p$, as follows from the above expression, does not depend on the magnitude (amplitude) of the signal $p_0$ to the emitter. There is a finite limit of $p_0(x)$ at $p_0(x = 0) \to \infty$. No matter how powerful the emitter we take, no matter how large or amplitude of the original signal, for each given distance $x$ it is impossible to obtain a signal greater than $p_{\text{пред}}(x)$.
