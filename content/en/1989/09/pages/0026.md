⟦folio 24⟧

Let us remind the reader what the spectrum of a signal is. Apparently, the word “spectrum” is associated with a picture of the optical emission spectra of atoms, containing a set of multi-colored stripes. Each atom is characterized by its own “spectral portrait”. Thus, in the spectrum of sodium, a yellow line stands out at a light wavelength of 0.59 microns. No matter how we transform a wave of a given spectral composition (i.e., with a known set of linear absorption) in a linear light medium—pass it through light filters, scattering media, amplifiers, etc.—new frequencies (or, what is the same thing, new spectral lines) will not arise. Nonlinear signal conversion using nonlinear optics methods is another matter. It is known that the infrared beam of a powerful laser, passing through a specially selected crystal, can change its color to green. The frequency of the light wave doubles.

A similar effect of a multiple increase in frequency, or, as they also say, generation of higher harmonics, is also important for the physics of powerful acoustic waves. When we considered harmonic waveform distortion (see Fig. 1), we actually already encountered this phenomenon. Indeed, the spectrum of the signal depicted in figure 1 consists of a set of equal frequencies - the fundamental frequency of the emitted signal $f$ - this frequency corresponds to the original undistorted sinusoidal signal - and high harmonics $nf$ ($n=2, 3, 4, \dots$), which appeared when the wave propagated in a nonlinear medium. In other words, distortion of the sine wave shape leads to the appearance of high harmonics in the spectrum. The amplitude of the second harmonic ($n=2$) increases in proportion to the distance traveled by the wave. It can become quite large and can therefore be changed quite precisely. On the other hand, at a fixed distance between the emitter and receiver of sound, the amplitude of the second harmonic depends on the elastic properties of the medium, or, as physicists and material scientists say, on nonlinear models of the medium. Our readers are probably familiar with Young's model - a quantity that determines the elastic deformation of a solid under the action of applied mechanical stress (remember Hooke's law). Young's modulus is a linear model, since in Hooke's law the deformation of a body is directly proportional to stress (depends linearly on it). With the refinement of Hooke's law in the region of high stresses, when the deformation cannot be considered elastic - the material becomes “flat”, “flows” and even collapses - the dependence of the deformation on stress is already characterized by nonlinear moduli of the medium.

So, by changing the amplitude of the second harmonic of a wave passing through such a medium, we thereby change the nonlinear models of the medium and, therefore, can judge the laminarity, strength and other important characteristics of matter.

Now we have managed to understand the meaning of one of the most important applications of nonlinear dynamics. When studying the properties of solid materials, they are generally subjected to heavy loads. Using special presses, pressures of up to ten thousand atmospheres are created. Instead of this cumbersome and expensive equipment, a simpler method can sometimes be used. A sound emitter is glued to the end of the rod made of the material under study, with the help of which a very powerful wave can be excited in the rod. At the other end of the rod, a nonlinear signal is recorded (for example, the amplitude of the second harmonic), containing the necessary information about the properties of the material.
