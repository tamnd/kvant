⟦folio 27⟧

ANT
ON A CAN

I. F. AKULICH

Are the students right?

Two schoolchildren were asked to solve the following problem:
The tin can has the shape of a rectangular steep cylinder of radius R and height H. There is an ant on the circumference of one of the bases (at point A at Fig. 1). He wants to move along the surface of the jar to the point B most distant from him on the circle of another base (symmetrical to point A relative to the center of the jar). How should it go so that the path length is minimal?

- But this is a very simple task! — the first schoolboy said confidently. — We need to unwrap the surface of the cylinder onto a plane. To determine this, we will assume that the ant crawls first along the side surface, and then along the upper base (it is possible that a symmetrical path will be marked out: first along the lower base, and then along the side surface, the length of which is the same). Expand the jar (Fig. 2, a), we immediately find that the shortest path is first along the patterns AM, then along the diameter MB. The length of this path is obviously $S_{\min} = H + 2R$.

“Wait a minute,” objected another schoolboy, “but it’s much more convenient to unfold the can differently.” Let's discard the bottom completely, and turn the side surface into a rectangle (Fig. 2, b). Then the shortest path is the segment connecting points A and B; its length is $S_{\min} = \sqrt{H^2 + \pi^2 R^2}$. And on the bank, the ant’s path will be a section of a helix line.

The students argued, but then found a way out: they need to compare the two results obtained and which one is smaller will be the desired route. First they determined under what conditions the two paths would be equal in length; for this they simply equated them:

$$H + 2R = \sqrt{H^2 + \pi^2 R^2},$$

then converted:

$$(H + 2R)^2 = H^2 + \pi^2 R^2,$$
$$H^2 + 4HR + 4R^2 = H^2 + \pi^2 R^2,$$
$$4H = (\pi^2 - 4)R,$$
$$\frac{H}{R} = \frac{\pi^2}{4} - 1 \approx 1,467.$$
