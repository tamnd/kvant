The Greeks could reveal a myriad and even a myriad of myriads of myriads (i.e., the number $10^{12}$), but they could not write down this number. However, the Greeks did not have to deal with such large numbers. But still, one of the greatest Greek mathematicians, Archimedes, wondered what to call very large numbers?

In his essay “The Calculus of Grains of Sand” (“Psammit”), he set himself the goal of showing that there are “numbers greater than the number of grains of sand that can be combined ... in a space equal to the volume ... of the whole world.” The meaning of this statement becomes if, if we take into account that Archimedes, in relation to the structure of the world, adhered to the views of his older contemporary, the outstanding Greek astronomer Archistarchus of Samos. According to Archistarchus, the world is a sphere, in the center of which is the Sun (!), and the boundary of which is the “sphere of fixed stars”. The Earth revolves around the Sun in a circular orbit, the diameter of which is related to the diameter of the Earth in the same way as the diameter of the sphere of the fixed stars is related to the diameter of the Earth's orbit. Archimedes counts very little grain of sand: a poppy seed, according to his assumption, contains a myriad of grains of sand.

What did Archimedes call very large numbers? He called the ordinary unit the “unit of first numbers,” and the myriad of myriads of such units, i.e., the number 10,000,000, the “unit of second numbers.” He called the myriad of myriads of second numbers “the unit of third numbers,” and so he counted up to the myriad of myriads of “myriad-myriad numbers.” This is a huge number. Suffice it to say that we would write it as ones followed by 800,000,000 zeros! But the great scientist did not stop here either. He declared all the numbers he named to be “numbers of the first period”, and a myriad of myriads of myriads of numbers - a “unit of the second period”. So he must have a Myridomirid period. Of course, he could go further, calling the number following the one he named the unit of the first epoch or era - the sonorous Greek language gave him great opportunities.
