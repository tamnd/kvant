LOOKING AT THE THERMOMETER...

Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
M. I. KAGANOV

One morning I felt that the quartile was colder than usual. Looking at the thermometer, I saw: in reality, instead of the usual 20 °C, it was 19 °C. After complaining about the instability of public services, he got ready and went to work. On the subway, my mind turned to the thermometer reading, and an error arose that something was wrong here...

Temperature is a measure of the thermal movement of molecules. The average energy of thermal motion of molecules (say, a gas in the air filling a room) is equal to $\frac{3}{2} kT$, where $k \approx 1,4 \times 10^{-16}$ erg/deg is Boltzmann’s constant, and the temperature, however, must be changed not in degrees Celsius, but on an absolute scale - the Kelvin scale, offset relative to the Celsius scale by $-273,16^\circ$. In other words, the temperature in my quartile was about 300 K. And I felt a temperature change $\Delta T$ of the order of $\frac{1}{300} T$, i.e. I was mistaken that the energy of thermal motion of molecules changed by 0.3%! Moreover, without any complex instruments, using a simple real thermometer, I checked my error - I measured the fact of a change of 0.3% in the energy of thermal motion of molecules... I even had a proud thought about evolution, which created the collision of sensory mechanisms of temperature error. It is well known that temperature is an important parameter for living organisms: a change in human body temperature by 1 degree is a sign of illness, and the range of permissible changes in body temperature is less than 10 degrees. Naturally, a living organism needs to mistake the temperature very accurately... But how?

The same real thermometer served as a clue to me. Therefore, let's deal with him first. How do we manage to change the temperature change by 1 degree? and with a medical thermometer - by 0.1 degrees? The meter serves as a change in the volume of liquid (for determination - mercury). When the temperature increases or decreases, its volume $V$ changes to $\Delta V$, and

$$\frac{\Delta V}{V} = \alpha \cdot \Delta T.$$

The factor $\alpha$ is called the coefficient of thermal expansion. In order of magnitude it is $10^{-3} - 10^{-4} K^{-1}$. It is possible to “see” $\Delta V/V \sim 10^{-4}$ only with the help of a simple technique - “driving” mercury into a thin capillary. Then $\Delta V = s \cdot \Delta l$, where $s$ is the area of ​​the second of the capillary, and $\Delta l = \frac{V}{s} \alpha \cdot \Delta T$ is the change in the height of the mercury column. By making $s$ small enough, the required resolution can be achieved. The capillary serves as an amplifier.
