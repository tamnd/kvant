Having dealt with the thermometer, let's move on to a living organism. What makes him successful? The determining role of temperature in the life of important processes is due to the fact that the speed $W$ of most chemical reactions (without which life would be impossible) depends on temperature very sharply - according to an exponential law:

$$W \sim e^{-U/kT}.$$

The value $U$, called the activation energy, is different for each reaction, but, as a rule, $U$ significantly exceeds $kT$. Without going too deep and without looking at reference books, I reasoned like this. Chemical reactions are always a rearrangement of electronic states. For example, the combination of Na and Cl atoms into a NaCl molecule occurs as follows: the Na atom separates an electron from the Cl atom. The Na$^+$ and Cl$^-$ ions are attracted to each other and create a NaCl molecule, but the electrons in the NaCl molecule are distributed around the nuclei differently than in the Na and Cl atoms. To change the energy of electrons in an atom, a special energy scale was created - electron-volt, and 1 eV = 1.6·10$^{-12}$ erg. So, the characteristic value of the activation energy $U$ is about 1 eV.

The relative change in the rate of chemical reaction $\Delta W/W$ when the temperature $T$ changes by values ​​$\Delta T$ is$^*$):

$$\frac{\Delta W}{W} \approx \frac{U}{kT} \frac{\Delta T}{T}.$$

At $\Delta T/T \approx 1/300$ the relative change in the rate of the chemical reaction is $\Delta W/W \approx 1/10$, i.e., quite noticeable.

*) $W(T+\Delta T)$ proportional

$$e^{-\frac{U}{k(T+\Delta T)}} = e^{-\frac{U}{kT(1+\Delta T/T)}} \approx e^{-\frac{U}{kT}} \left(1-\frac{\Delta T}{T}\right),$$

pose

$$W(T+\Delta T) = W(T) \cdot e^{\frac{U}{kT} \Delta T/T} \approx W(T) \left(1+\frac{U}{kT} \frac{\Delta T}{T}\right),$$

if $U \cdot \Delta T/kT^2 \ll 1$. By writing out approximate equalities, I assume the teacher's ability to handle small quantities.
