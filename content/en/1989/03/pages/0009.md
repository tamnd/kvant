So, to complete the calculation, we must be able to calculate the quantities $\beta$, $x$ and $x^2(t)$. Let's start with the last one. Since the anharmonic term $\beta x^2$ is a small correction (it had to be included in the expression for the force only because without it the answer turned out to be equal to zero), we can assume that the potential energy of motion is $x \cdot x^2(t)/2$, and the total energy is equal to

$$E = \frac{M(x')^2}{2} + \frac{xx^2}{2}.$$

But on average, the kinetic $(M(x')^2/2)$ and potential $(xx^2/2)$ energies are equal. That's why

$$\bar{x}^2 = \frac{1}{\alpha} \bar{E}.$$

And the average energy of colbatal motion is $kT^*$). So,

$$\frac{\Delta V}{V} = \frac{3\beta}{dx^2} kT.$$

All that remains is to learn how to calculate the set $3\beta/x^2$. Of course, this is precisely the most difficult part of the task. But we will simplify it extremely (although even with this method of presentation you will have to simply believe the author at one point).

Let us deal with pons whose charges are $+e$ and $-e$ and the distance between them is $r$. There is an electrical attraction force between the pons equal to $e^2/r^2$. But this force cannot be the only one - under the influence of such a force the ions would fall on each other. When they get too close to each other, they echo, and the law of the echo can be clarified using the equation of quantum mechanics.

And here we come to the place where the reader will have to trust the authors with energies $^3/2kT$ for a particle in a gas: the particle is free, that is, its potential energy is zero, therefore, for each degree of freedom there is “accounted for” $^4/2kT$, and not $kT$. There are three degrees of freedom. But we have already taken into account three degrees of freedom.

$$F = \frac{e^2}{r^2} - \frac{A}{r^{10}}.$$

At $r=d$ the force must be zero. Therefore $A=d^8e^2$, and finally

$$F = \frac{e^2}{r^2} - \frac{e^2d^8}{r^{10}}.$$

Substituting $r=d+x$ here, we expand $F$ in powers of $x$, limiting ourselves to the first two terms. The coefficient at $x$ gives us the value $x$, and at $x^2$ the value $\beta$. Please do these calculations yourself and you will be convinced that

$$\frac{\Delta V}{V} = \frac{3 \cdot 52}{64} \frac{kdT}{e^2},$$

i.e. $\alpha = \frac{3 \cdot 52}{64} \frac{kd}{e^2}$.

The distance between the atoms $d$ is approximately equal to the size of the atom—a $d \approx a \approx 3 \cdot 10^{-8}$ cm = 3 Å—and substituting the values ​​$k$, $e$ and $d$, we get

$$\alpha \approx 5 \cdot 10^{-5} \text{ град}^{-1}.$$

After the reduction, you will be convinced that the assessment we received (under simplified consideration) is not bad at all.

Well, that's all, please. We only note that we could have guessed the order of magnitude of the thermal expansion coefficient $a$. Take a look at the last formula for $\Delta V/V$. It shows that the dimensionless ratio $\Delta V/V$ is approximately equal to the ratio of thermal energy (per particle) $kT$ to the binding energy between particles (here $\sim e^2/d$). Hence the conclusion: the more tightly bound the molecules in the body, the lower their coefficient of thermal expansion. This pattern is easy to understand from the table, which shows melting temperatures for solids or boiling temperatures for liquids with values ​​$a$. But, of course, such a statement is not a law of nature. There may be exceptions to this...
