Namely: using a conductor with current in a magnetic field, an electric field will appear inside the conductor, directed perpendicular to the direction of the current and the magnetic field. This remarkable effect was discovered and studied by the American physicist Edwin Hall in 1879 and now bears his name.

To understand why we called the Hall effect remarkable, let's look at it in a little more detail. Let us first calculate the potential difference $\Delta \varphi_X$ (it is called the Hall potential difference), which is processed between the side surfaces of the conductor. As we have already said, the charge filling process will stop when the Lorentz force $F_\pi = evB$ is balanced by the electrostatic force $F_{\perp} = eE_\perp$:

$$evB = eE_\perp,$$

here $v$ is the average speed of the electric direction, $B$ is the magnetic field induction, $E_\perp$ is the direction of the transverse electric field. From here we get

$$\Delta \varphi_X = E_\perp d = vBd,$$

where $d$ is the thickness of the conductor. The average speed of charge direction can be expressed through the current strength $I$, the concentration of free electrons in the conductor $n$ and its cross-sectional area $S$:

$$I = envS, \quad v = \frac{I}{enS}.$$
