LOOKING AT THE THERMOMETER...

Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
M. I. KAGANOV

One morning I felt that the quartile was colder than usual. Looking at the thermometer, I saw: in reality, instead of the usual 20 °C, it was 19 °C. After complaining about the instability of public services, he got ready and went to work. On the subway, my mind turned to the thermometer reading, and an error arose that something was wrong here...

Temperature is a measure of the thermal movement of molecules. The average energy of thermal motion of molecules (say, a gas in the air filling a room) is equal to $\frac{3}{2} kT$, where $k \approx 1,4 \times 10^{-16}$ erg/deg is Boltzmann’s constant, and the temperature, however, must be changed not in degrees Celsius, but on an absolute scale - the Kelvin scale, offset relative to the Celsius scale by $-273,16^\circ$. In other words, the temperature in my quartile was about 300 K. And I felt a temperature change $\Delta T$ of the order of $\frac{1}{300} T$, i.e. I was mistaken that the energy of thermal motion of molecules changed by 0.3%! Moreover, without any complex instruments, using a simple real thermometer, I checked my error - I measured the fact of a change of 0.3% in the energy of thermal motion of molecules... I even had a proud thought about evolution, which created the collision of sensory mechanisms of temperature error. It is well known that temperature is an important parameter for living organisms: a change in human body temperature by 1 degree is a sign of illness, and the range of permissible changes in body temperature is less than 10 degrees. Naturally, a living organism needs to mistake the temperature very accurately... But how?

The same real thermometer served as a clue to me. Therefore, let's deal with him first. How do we manage to change the temperature change by 1 degree? and with a medical thermometer - by 0.1 degrees? The meter serves as a change in the volume of liquid (for determination - mercury). When the temperature increases or decreases, its volume $V$ changes to $\Delta V$, and

$$\frac{\Delta V}{V} = \alpha \cdot \Delta T.$$

The factor $\alpha$ is called the coefficient of thermal expansion. In order of magnitude it is $10^{-3} - 10^{-4} K^{-1}$. It is possible to “see” $\Delta V/V \sim 10^{-4}$ only with the help of a simple technique - “driving” mercury into a thin capillary. Then $\Delta V = s \cdot \Delta l$, where $s$ is the area of ​​the second of the capillary, and $\Delta l = \frac{V}{s} \alpha \cdot \Delta T$ is the change in the height of the mercury column. By making $s$ small enough, the required resolution can be achieved. The capillary serves as an amplifier. If $V \sim 1$ cm$^3$, then in order to obtain $\Delta l \sim 1$ mm=0.1 cm at $\alpha \cdot \Delta T \sim 10^{-4}$, you must have a capillary with a flat

Having dealt with the thermometer, let's move on to a living organism. What makes him successful? The determining role of temperature in the life of important processes is due to the fact that the speed $W$ of most chemical reactions (without which life would be impossible) depends on temperature very sharply - according to an exponential law:

$$W \sim e^{-U/kT}.$$

The value $U$, called the activation energy, is different for each reaction, but, as a rule, $U$ significantly exceeds $kT$. Without going too deep and without looking at reference books, I reasoned like this. Chemical reactions are always a rearrangement of electronic states. For example, the combination of Na and Cl atoms into a NaCl molecule occurs as follows: the Na atom separates an electron from the Cl atom. The Na$^+$ and Cl$^-$ ions are attracted to each other and create a NaCl molecule, but the electrons in the NaCl molecule are distributed around the nuclei differently than in the Na and Cl atoms. To change the energy of electrons in an atom, a special energy scale was created - electron-volt, and 1 eV = 1.6·10$^{-12}$ erg. So, the characteristic value of the activation energy $U$ is about 1 eV.

The relative change in the rate of chemical reaction $\Delta W/W$ when the temperature $T$ changes by values ​​$\Delta T$ is$^*$):

$$\frac{\Delta W}{W} \approx \frac{U}{kT} \frac{\Delta T}{T}.$$

At $\Delta T/T \approx 1/300$ the relative change in the rate of the chemical reaction is $\Delta W/W \approx 1/10$, i.e., quite noticeable.

*) $W(T+\Delta T)$ proportional

$$e^{-\frac{U}{k(T+\Delta T)}} = e^{-\frac{U}{kT(1+\Delta T/T)}} \approx e^{-\frac{U}{kT}} \left(1-\frac{\Delta T}{T}\right),$$

pose

$$W(T+\Delta T) = W(T) \cdot e^{\frac{U}{kT} \Delta T/T} \approx W(T) \left(1+\frac{U}{kT} \frac{\Delta T}{T}\right),$$

if $U \cdot \Delta T/kT^2 \ll 1$. By writing out approximate equalities, I assume the teacher's ability to handle small quantities.

It has been developed in detail that such a calculation can be completed with the necessary accuracy (we say “in principle”). But it is absolutely clear that calculation is possible and there is no reason to expect that we are faced with a fundamentally insoluble problem.

All physical quantities mentioned in this note, the values ​​of which the author either copied or took from a reference book, can be obtained as a result of calculation from first principles. We will try to show this using the example of two quantities: activation energy $U$ and coefficient of thermal expansion $a$. At the same time, we will not get to the basics, considering that the size of the atom $a$ depends can be expressed through the world constants listed above. For example, the size of a hydrogen atom is $-a_H = \hbar^2/m_e e^2$. You can read about this in any popular book on quantum mechanics.

Let's start with the activation energy $U$. Since we do not intend to build a theory of rates of chemical reactions, but only want to demonstrate how the quantities of interest to us are expressed through world constants, we will limit ourselves to calculating the energy of induction of a hydrogen atom $U_{нон}$, i.e. we will answer the question of how much energy must be spent to tear an electron away from a proton.

Electron energy in a hydrogen atom -

$$E = m_e v^2 / 2 - e^2 / a,$$

But

$$m_e v^2 / a = e^2 / a^2,$$

and therefore

$$E = -e^2 / 2a$$

(I hope these equalities are clear?). Substituting the value $a = a_H$, we get:

$$U_{нон} = \frac{m_e e^4}{2\hbar^2} \approx 13,6 \text{ эВ.}$$

The energy that must be spent on rearranging electronic states is, as a rule, less than $U_{нон}$. Therefore, when assessing the relative change in the rate of chemical reactions $U \sim 1$ eV.

Calculating the coefficient of thermal expansion is more difficult. It requires knowledge of the structure of the body that is experiencing expansion. We will have to limit ourselves to the simplest approach, taking into account the main thing - that warm expansion is the result of the dependence of the average equilibrium distance between particles on temperature.

So, two particles in a body are located at a distance $d + x(t)$ from each other, and $d$ is the distance between them when these particles are at rest (at absolute zero temperature), and $x(t)$ is the instantaneous (at time $t$) deviation of the particle from the equilibrium position. The force $F$ acting on the particle resembles the elastic force acting on a weight attached to a circle - it is proportional to the deviation of the particle from the equilibrium position: $F = -\chi \cdot x(t)$. But the average force $F$ (over a sufficiently large period of time) should be equal to zero $^*$). And this means that $x(t) = 0$, i.e., the average distance between particles is $d$ and does not depend on the number of atoms and, therefore, on temperature. This result in scientific jargon sounds like this: the harmonic approximation cannot describe the warm expansion of bodies.

We brought this structure to scientific approval so that the words “approximation” appeared. The fact is that the expression for force that we used is approximate. Let's try to clarify it - take into account the terms that are nonlinear in the mixing $x(t)$:

$$F = -\chi \cdot x(t) + \beta \cdot x^2(t) + \dots$$

The theory based on this formula or similar formulas is called anharmonic, and the coefficient $\beta$ is called the anharmonic coefficient. From this formula it follows that $\bar{x} = \frac{\beta}{\chi} \bar{x}^2$, i.e.

$$\frac{\Delta V}{V} = \frac{(d + \bar{x})^3 - d^3}{d^3} \approx \frac{3\bar{x}}{d} = \frac{3\beta}{d\chi} \bar{x}^2$$

*) If the average force $F$ were different from zero, then the particles of the body would have to mix somewhere under its influence.

(coefficient 3 appeared due to the fact that the body can expand in those directions). In this formula, $V$ is the volume of the body at $T=0$ K. So, to complete the calculation, we must be able to calculate the quantities $\beta$, $x$ and $x^2(t)$. Let's start with the last one. Since the anharmonic term $\beta x^2$ is a small correction (it had to be included in the expression for the force only because without it the answer turned out to be equal to zero), we can assume that the potential energy of motion is $x \cdot x^2(t)/2$, and the total energy is equal to

$$E = \frac{M(x')^2}{2} + \frac{xx^2}{2}.$$

But on average, the kinetic $(M(x')^2/2)$ and potential $(xx^2/2)$ energies are equal. That's why

$$\bar{x}^2 = \frac{1}{\alpha} \bar{E}.$$

And the average energy of colbatal motion is $kT^*$). So,

$$\frac{\Delta V}{V} = \frac{3\beta}{dx^2} kT.$$

All that remains is to learn how to calculate the set $3\beta/x^2$. Of course, this is precisely the most difficult part of the task. But we will simplify it extremely (although even with this method of presentation you will have to simply believe the author at one point).

Let us deal with pons whose charges are $+e$ and $-e$ and the distance between them is $r$. There is an electrical attraction force between the pons equal to $e^2/r^2$. But this force cannot be the only one - under the influence of such a force the ions would fall on each other. When they get too close to each other, they echo, and the law of the echo can be clarified using the equation of quantum mechanics.

And here we come to the place where the reader will have to trust the authors with energies $^3/2kT$ for a particle in a gas: the particle is free, that is, its potential energy is zero, therefore, for each degree of freedom there is “accounted for” $^4/2kT$, and not $kT$. There are three degrees of freedom. But we have already taken into account three degrees of freedom.

$$F = \frac{e^2}{r^2} - \frac{A}{r^{10}}.$$

At $r=d$ the force must be zero. Therefore $A=d^8e^2$, and finally

$$F = \frac{e^2}{r^2} - \frac{e^2d^8}{r^{10}}.$$

Substituting $r=d+x$ here, we expand $F$ in powers of $x$, limiting ourselves to the first two terms. The coefficient at $x$ gives us the value $x$, and at $x^2$ the value $\beta$. Please do these calculations yourself and you will be convinced that

$$\frac{\Delta V}{V} = \frac{3 \cdot 52}{64} \frac{kdT}{e^2},$$

i.e. $\alpha = \frac{3 \cdot 52}{64} \frac{kd}{e^2}$.

The distance between the atoms $d$ is approximately equal to the size of the atom—a $d \approx a \approx 3 \cdot 10^{-8}$ cm = 3 Å—and substituting the values ​​$k$, $e$ and $d$, we get

$$\alpha \approx 5 \cdot 10^{-5} \text{ град}^{-1}.$$

After the reduction, you will be convinced that the assessment we received (under simplified consideration) is not bad at all.

Well, that's all, please. We only note that we could have guessed the order of magnitude of the thermal expansion coefficient $a$. Take a look at the last formula for $\Delta V/V$. It shows that the dimensionless ratio $\Delta V/V$ is approximately equal to the ratio of thermal energy (per particle) $kT$ to the binding energy between particles (here $\sim e^2/d$). Hence the conclusion: the more tightly bound the molecules in the body, the lower their coefficient of thermal expansion. This pattern is easy to understand from the table, which shows melting temperatures for solids or boiling temperatures for liquids with values ​​$a$. But, of course, such a statement is not a law of nature. There may be exceptions to this...
