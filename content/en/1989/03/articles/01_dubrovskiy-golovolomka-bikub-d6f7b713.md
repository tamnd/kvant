⟦folio 4⟧

Take your starting, good Rubik’s cube, if it’s still there, warm the dust from it and fasten 9 pairs of small cubes with adhesive tape, as shown in figure 1. You will get a completely different puzzle - the “bicube”, which we learned about from the book “Relatives of the Magic Cube” by V. Hinze, published in the GDR. The main difference between the bicube is that each double block “locks” one rake. Which grabs will remain free depends on the “configuration” of the bicube, i.e. the location of the blocks (binary and single) without taking into account their colors. For example, in the initial state (configuration 0, Fig. 1), 3 rake are movable - yellow, shining and red. We will consider a simplified version of the bicube, in which only these robbers are allowed to heal - this is already quite interesting. It is interesting that this variant is equivalent to Raoul Rab’s flat puzzle “slut” (Fig. 2), consisting of 10 wing triangles arranged in the form of three intersecting circles and moved by doctors of these circles. Like a regular cube, the bicub is randomly tangled and then tried to return to its original state.

In any bicube configuration, the choice of possible repetitions is very impossible. This avoids coupling between configurations.

Zinim is a relatively simple graph (Fig. 3). Its peaks have different configurations, and each colored hand means a repeat of the same color at 90° along the clock. For example, to turn configuration 1 (Fig. 4) into 0, you need to sequentially rotate the yellow and blue faces 90° counterclockwise in accordance with the graph. The blue loop, “called” to the vertex of graph a, shows that, in accordance with the value of the configuration, the shining rake is free, but with all its repetitions we find ourselves in “dead ends,” i.e., the remaining edges are locked. This is how 5 more configurations are already arranged, depicted by white circles. Of all the black circles, you can also get into dead ends, but for simplicity they are not shown in the graph.

As can be seen from the graph, for a bicube “all roads lead to configuration 1, 2, 3 or 0” - you just need to avoid backtracking and dead ends. Therefore, configuration 0 is achieved almost automatically. It turns out that all the permutations of blocks possible in this configuration (and there are 60 of them) can be obtained by combining two 8-move operations, resulting in the graph by traversing 0→1→2→0 and 0→3→1→0 along the blue and red arrows. Once you understand how these operations work, you can learn how to completely assemble a bicub.
