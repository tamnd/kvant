Introduction

What to do?

Statement of the problem
and necessary simplifications

Let us formulate the conditions of the problem:

A man is standing on the street. Suddenly it starts to rain. The person goes to the nearest shelter, located at a distance l from him. At what speed should he beat in order to get as little wet as possible?

Fig. 2.

The task has been set, but in this form, please, we cannot solve it. Indeed, a person is a creature of complex shape, and when he moves, he also continuously changes his shape (rearranges his legs, waves his arms...). So making an accurate calculation is an incredibly difficult task. Therefore, there is nothing left but to solve its approximation, and therefore we reformulate the problem:

A suitable paralleled object whose face areas are $S_B$, $S_B$ and $S_J$ (from the words “side”, “top” and “front”) moves with speed and perpedicular to the face $S_J$. At the same time, it is raining, each drop of which has a speed v (Fig. 1) (the vector v is not necessarily directed vertically downwards - the rain can also be oblique). The number of raindrops per unit volume is k. The question is: how many drops N will fall on the paralleled object during the time that it moves a distance l, and at what value of speed i will this value N be the smallest?

Let's get started with the solution

Let us introduce a coordinate system $Oxyz$ in space: axis $Oz$ is directed vertically downwards, axis $Ox$ is directed in the direction of vector $\bar{u}$, and axis $Oy$ is perpedicular to the plane $Oxz$, so that the projection of the velocity vector of falling raindrops v onto axis $Oy$ is non-negative (Fig. 2). Since the vector v is given, we can assume that the projections of the vector v on the coordinate axes are known. Let's call them $v_x$, $v_y$ and $v_z$. What can be said about these projections? We will solve the problem in the reference system associated with the parallelepid, i.e. we will consider the parallelepid motionless: then we must be计 on this reference system will receive a speed $\bar{w} = \bar{v} - \bar{u}$. The projections of the velocity vector $\bar{w}$ on the coordinate axes will be $w_x = v_x - u, w_y = v_y, w_z = v_z$ (u is the length of the vector $\bar{u}$). It is required to determine how many drops $N$ will fall on the parallelepid in time $\tau = l/u$ and at what $u$ the value $N$ will be minimal.

It is clear that in time $\tau$ all the drops will fall onto the parallelepid, from here from the sides of the parallelepid to a distance no greater than $\tau \cdot |\bar{w}|$, in the direction opposite to the vector $\bar{w}$, i.e. all the drops located inside the volume of the body outlined in red lines on figure 3. How to find the volume of this body? It is easy to notice that this body consists of three prisms, the areas of the bases of which are equal to $S_\pi, S_B$ and $S_B$, and the heights are the abscille values ​​of the projections of the vector $\tau \cdot \bar{w}$ on the axes $Ox, Oy$ and $Oz$, respectively. Therefore, the volume of this body is equal to

$$\tau(|w_x|S_\pi + |w_y|S_B + |w_z|S_B) = \tau(|v_x - u|S_\pi + v_y S_B + v_z S_B),$$

Let's continue with the solution

Since the quantities $k$ and $l$ are constant, for convenience we will operate with the quantities

$$\psi = \frac{N}{kl} = \frac{|v_x - u|S_\pi + v_y S_B + v_z S_B}{u}.$$

Let's consider two cases:

1. $v_x \leq 0$ (oncoming death).

In this case, $v_x - u < 0$, and the function $\psi$ takes the form:

$$\psi = \frac{(u - v_x)S_\pi + v_y S_B + v_z S_B}{u} = S_\pi + \frac{-v_x S_\pi + v_y S_B + v_z S_B}{u}.$$

Since $v_x \leq 0$, the numerator of the fraction is positive, and the function $\psi(u)$ kills on the interval $(0; +\infty)$. The graph of function $\psi$ versus $u$ is shown in figure 4. We see that although $\psi$ kills as $u$ increases, it is still always $\psi > S_\pi$, and $\psi \rightarrow S_\pi$ at $u \rightarrow \infty$. But then another fact, quite unexpected at first glance, is revealed: since $\psi > S_{\pi}$ for any $u$, $N = kl\psi$ is always greater than $klS_{\pi}$. This means that no matter how fast you run (even fly at the speed of a bullet), the “minimum dose” should still be equal to $klS_{\pi}$, guaranteed. 2. $v_x > 0$ (passing rain).

There are two intervals to consider here:

1) $0 < u \leq v_x$. Then $|v_x - u| = v_x - u$, and

$$\psi = \frac{v_x S_{\pi} + v_y S_B + v_z S_B}{u} - S_{\pi}.$$

This function kills on the interval $(0; v_x]$ and reaches a minimum at $u = v_x$:

$$\psi |_{u = v_x} = \frac{v_y S_B + v_z S_B}{v_x}.$$

2) $u > v_x$. Then $|v_x - u| = u - v_x$, and

$$\psi = \frac{-v_x S_{\pi} + v_y S_B + v_z S_B}{u} + S_{\pi}.$$

Here it is no longer possible to immediately say how $\psi$ behaves as $u$ increases. It depends on the numerator of the fraction $A = -v_x S_{\pi} + v_y S_B + v_z S_B$. If $A > 0$, then $\psi(u)$ is a killing function on the segment $(v_x; +\infty)$, if $A < 0$ is increasing on this interval, and if $A = 0$, then $\psi = S_{\pi}$ is const on this interval.

The dependence graphs $\psi(u)$ for all $u \in (0; +\infty)$ are shown on figure 5. It can be seen that in the section from 0 to $v_x$ the curves have the same appearance for all three cases $(A > 0, A = 0$ and $A < 0)$, and after the break at $u = v_x$ they are different.

Conclusions

Having analyzed the calculation results and graphs in Figures 4 and 5, we can thoroughly answer the question of how to run away from the board.

So, if the board is counter, then you need to run to the steal as quickly as possible. If the board is downwind, then first you need to mentally estimate the values ​​of $A = -v_x S_{\pi} + v_y S_B + v_z S_B$. If it turns out that $A > 0$, then in this case it is advisable to hurry up. If $A = 0$, you can move at any speed exceeding $v_x$, you will get wet equally. Well, if $A < 0$ - you need to run strictly at a speed equal to $v_x$ - then you will get the least wet.

If, say, $v_y = 0$ (the case of a tailwind), then the inequality $A < 0$ is equivalent to the inequality $v_x S_{\pi} > v_z S_B$. Since for a tall, thin person $S_{\pi}$ is significantly larger than $S_B$, this inequality can hold for relatively small, “pedestrian” $v_x$. Such a person, if he moves towards stealing so that the trajectories of the drops seem vertical to him, has a chance to generally stay dry. This is called “slipping between the droplets.”
