BRAIDS AND KNOTS

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
A. V. SOSINSKY

Braiding hair and tying knots - is this really mathematics?

An earnest student clearly takes away from school the following point of view: mathematics deals with abstract concepts, and everyday objects, like braids and knots, have nothing to do with mathematics.

But that's not true. Mathematicians in our time deal not only with emerging problems - number theory, calculations of space flights, the study of poetic meters - but also with earthly, everyday issues: economic calculations and even... the theory of queues.

And braid theories. This is a realizing and living science that arose in the 20s of our century; is not yet completed and has not exhausted its applications. And in beauty, the theory of braids is not inferior to the classical, but frozen in its magnitude, mathematics of antiquity and the 17th century, which is only studied in school.

Our story begins with examples of braids (Fig. 1). You can imagine a braid like this: n nails are driven into the upper and lower edges of a vertical board (n can equal 1, 2, 3,...) - each of the nails on the upper base is connected by a thread to one of the nails on the bottom; the threads do not intersect in pairs and must always go down (the thread has no right to turn around or start going up: the figures on figure 2, for example, are not braids).

Two braids are considered equivalent (i.e., the same) if one can be turned into an exact copy of the other by moving the threads (without breaking or gluing) so that each point of each thread moves only in the horizontal plane. Such a movement is shown in figure 3.

At figure 1 at the top at the beginning of each thread, the artist indicated its serial number. Below again the number of each thread is indicated - but here the numbers are not required and are in order: each braid corresponds to a permutation of the numbers of its threads. Thus, the braids $K_1$, $K_4$, $K_5$ and figure 1 correspond to permutations

$$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 3 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 1 & 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 4 \\ 2 & 4 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Fig. 1. Examples of braids of three and four strands: $K_1$ - “maiden braids”; $K_2$ - trival braid; $K_3$ — ?; $K_4$ - dyed braid; $K_5$ - cyclic braid.
