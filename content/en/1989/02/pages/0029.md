For example, water as such (liquid) can be heated or external pressure applied to it, or both at the same time (all this, of course, within certain limits), and in this case the phase state of the system in the state will not change - water remains water. The same is true in the case of ice or steam. It can be concluded that a single-phase system has two degrees of freedom.

The situation is completely different in two-phase equilibrium. There is an equation connecting both external parameters. In the case of liquid-vapor equilibrium, we have already written it down: $q_\pi(p, T) = q_\pi(p, T)$. Therefore, you can freely change only one parameter, and the other must be “adjusted” to it in accordance with the equation. If we begin to change both parameters independently of each other, we will “jump” from the linear phase equilibrium and one of the phases will disappear—the phase state of the system will change. Therefore, the state of two-phase equilibrium has only one degree of freedom.

Let us now move on to three-phase equilibrium by adding ice to water and steam. Let us assume that the phases in the vessel are located as shown in figure 3. We take into account that the number of molecules leaving ice per unit time through a unit area will also be a function of pressure and temperature - $q_\pi = q_\pi(p, T)$, and we write down the phase condition:

$$C = 3 - \Phi (C > 0).$$
