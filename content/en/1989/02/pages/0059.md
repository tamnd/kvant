What might it depend on? Obviously, from the refractive index of the liquid $n$ (we assume that the effect surrounding the lens in its optical parameters does not differ from vacuum) and from the radii of curvature of its surfaces $r_1$ and $r_2$. As calculations show (you can easily reproduce them, for which you only need the law of light refraction and a little geometry), the focal length of a thin lens is determined by the expression:

$$\frac{1}{F} = (n - 1) \left( \frac{1}{r_1} - \frac{1}{r_2} \right),$$

moreover, for a surface convex along the bow, $r > 0$, and for a thin wave, $r < 0$. In our case $r_1 \to \infty$, and $r_2 = -R$, therefore from equalities (1) and (2) we have

$$n = R/d.$$

Thus, to determine the refractive index of a liquid, it is necessary to change the lens two distances from the screen $\theta$ to the mirror $3$, at which clear images $S'$ and $S''$ are formed on the screen, and find the ratio of the larger distance to the smaller one.

Routine 3. Let us now replace the liquid lens $\lambda_j$ with a glass lens $\lambda_1$, and the wave mirror $\theta$ with a small flat mirror $\delta_1$ (Fig. 3; for clarity, the lens is slightly raised above the mirror). By moving the screen illuminator $\theta$ in height, a bright image $S'$ is added to it next to the source $S$. It's easy to see that this will happen when the screen aligns with the focal plane of the lens. Therefore for

$$\frac{1}{F} = (n - 1) \left( \frac{1}{r_1} - \frac{1}{r_2} \right),$$

To limit the focal length $F_1$ of the lens, it is enough to change the distance from the screen in its specified position to the central section of the lens. (Note that a similar method, known as the autolimation method, is used to establish the initial orientation of the telescope required in a number of optical schemes.)

Routine 4. Let us now bring the illuminator-screen $\theta$ closer to the lens $\lambda_1$. At a certain distance $d_1$, which for a biconvex symmetrical lens is about 0.5 $F_1$, a clear image appears on the screen again - $S''$, although not as bright as $S'$. It is created due to reflection, albeit relatively weak, from the lower polished surface of the lens, which plays the role of a wave mirror with a low reflection coefficient ($\varphi \approx 0,05$). It is not difficult to verify this by taking the lens from the mirror and crossing it directly onto the table (or onto a book, or onto a dark surface): the image $S''$ is preserved and even becomes more contrasting.

The nature of the image $S''$ is the same as in the case of a liquid lens in the second optet, with the difference that the liquid lens is replaced by a glass one and the first surface of the lens along the bow can be convex, and the reflection coefficient from the lower surface of the lens is small. Therefore, we apply formula (1) to image $S''$. By measuring $F_1$ (by image position $S'$), $d_1$ (by image position $S''$) and using formula (1), we can calculate the radius of curvature $r$ of the lower lens surface $\lambda_1$ along the bow:

$$r_1 = \frac{F_1 d_1}{F_1 - d_1}.$$

If the surfaces of the lens have different curvatures, then it is necessary to change two values ​​$d_1$ and $d_2$ for the case when the lens lies on one and the other of its surfaces, and calculate $r_1$ and $r_2$.
