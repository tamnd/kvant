Fig. 4. Multiplication of braids: the top of the second braid is applied to the bottom of the first and the corresponding threads are glued together.

It turns out that any braid can be represented as a product of the elementary braids $S_1$, $S_2$, ... and their inverses. For example, it is obvious that

$$K_1 = S_1 S_2^{-1} S_1 S_2^{-1} S_1 S_2^{-1} S_1 S_2^{-1}.$$

Next

$$K_3 = S_2 S_1 S_3^{-1} S_1^{-1} S_3 S_2^{-1} S_1 S_3 S_1^{-1} S_3^{-1};$$

this becomes obvious if, by properly moving the threads, we slightly lower the 4 double points located on the right side of the braid $K_3$ (Fig. 7).

Exercise 1. Imagine the braids $K_4$, $K_5$ (see Fig. 1) as a product of the elementary braids $S_1$, $S_2$, $S_3$ and their inverses.

In braid theory, as in analytical geometry, algebraic notation allows geometric reasoning to replace perfection with mechanical calculations based on the following points:

I. Trival relations

$$S_i S_i^{-1} = S_i^{-1} S_i = 1,$$

$$S_i \cdot 1 = 1 \cdot S_i = S_i (i=1, 2,..., n-1).$$

II. Long distance commuting

$$S_i S_j = S_j S_i$$ at $|i-j| \geq 2$

($i, j=1, 2,..., n-1$).

III. Plexus relationships

$$S_i S_{i+1} S_i = S_{i+1} S_i S_{i+1}$$ ($i=1, 2,..., n-2$).
