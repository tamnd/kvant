Among the braids at figure 1, the painted braid $K_4$ stands out; it is called so not at all because the artist painted its legs in different colors: any braid that corresponds to an identical permutation is called dyed

$$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & \dots & n \\ 1 & 2 & 3 & \dots & n \end{pmatrix},$$

i.e., a braid that preserves the order of thread numbers. A trivial braid, all of whose threads are vertical, is a special case of a dyed braid.

By the way, figure 1 shows not one, but two trivial braids. Two? — Yes, exactly two: the braid $K_3$ is trivial, or it easily turns (see Fig. 3) ​​into a braid of four vertical threads.

Among all the braids, except for the dyed ones, it is worth highlighting the cyclic braids that are in a sense opposite to them: these are braids that rearrange all the thread numbers in a single cycle, as the braid $K_5$ does:

$$1 \rightarrow 2 \rightarrow 4 \rightarrow 3 \rightarrow 1.$$

Braids are one of the simplest geometric objects that can easily be “algebraized”: braids with the same number of threads can be multiplied. This is done quite simply (see Fig. 4): you need to attach one circle, gluing the corresponding threads, and remove the studs that have become unnecessary (the lower studs of the first braid and the upper ones of the second). Such multiplication has a number of properties of general multiplication of numbers: the associative law is satisfied

$$K_1(K_2K_3)=(K_1K_2)K_3,$$

ests are similar - the trivial braid $K_2=1$, for which

$$1 \cdot K=K \cdot 1=K.$$

There is also an analogue of division: each braid $K$ has a reverse braid $K^{-1}$; for her

$$K^{-1} \cdot K=K \cdot K^{-1}=1.$$

The last fact is not very clear, and we invite the reader to figure out how to construct a braid inverse to this one. Well, if it doesn’t work out, you can look at the answer on the next page (Fig. 5). Braid multiplication, however, is not commutative: $K \cdot L$ is not necessarily equal to $L \cdot K$ (an example is given below).

The resulting algebraic object—it is called a braid group of $n$ strands and is denoted $B_n$—is not very simple, but it has been thoroughly studied. Let's start studying the group of braids. To do this, you will need elementary braids $S_1, S_2, \dots, S_{n-1}$ of $n$ threads (Fig. 6).
