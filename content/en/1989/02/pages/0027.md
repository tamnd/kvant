In what follows we will assume that the phase boundaries are always flat. Taking these remarks into account, the state of the system is characterized by pressure and temperature values ​​common to all phases.

First of all, it is desirable to find out under what external conditions the phase transformation occurs. To determine this, let’s focus on the evaporation of water. If we fix normal atmospheric pressure, then water in our state begins to evaporate at 100 °C. At lower temperatures, the piston in the vessel will “lie” on the water, and there will be no steam in the system.

By changing the pressure, we will also change the evaporation temperature. You can summarize all the data on the phase transformation into a table of two columns: one indicates the pressure, and the other indicates the evaporation temperature. But it is even more convenient to display the table data graphically as a dependence of temperature on pressure (Fig. 1). In the same way, it is possible to present in the form of graphs data on two other phase transformations that can occur in the system - the transformations water ← ice and ice ← steam. As a result, we get a diagram like the one in figure 2.

Let me emphasize one important point. The lines on the diagram separate the areas of stable existence of the corresponding phases from each other. This does not mean that each phase “lives” only in its own area and cannot “merge” into someone else’s. Of course it can. We regularly observe this, say, in the spring, when the rivers are still full of ice even at positive temperatures. But this state is not stable - the ice in the river is melting. This process does not occur instantly, but at some finite speed. After a few days (if frost does not strike again), the ice begins to grow in weight until the end. But under the same conditions, water will never become ice again.

If the state of a system ceases over time, it is said to have reached equilibrium. The diagram at figure 2 represents only the equilibrium state of the system.

Such diagrams are called phase diagrams. They are very convenient to use: knowing the temperature and pressure, we can immediately determine which phases of the system will be in equilibrium. Let us determine the value of pressure and temperature on the diagram that corresponds to the point. If it falls into the region of stable existence of steam, ice or liquid, then the system will be in equilibrium with a state of one corresponding phase. If the point is on the line of the diagram, the system in equilibrium will be a state of two phases. Which ones exactly? It is absolutely obvious: if, for example, this is the dividing line between the “water” and “ice” regions, then it is made of ice and water. Finally, with a single combination of pressure and temperature, all three phases will be in equilibrium.
