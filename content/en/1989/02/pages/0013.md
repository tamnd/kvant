let's pour in. We only note that with their help, the geometric theory of braids turns into a computational science, in which all specific questions can, in principle, be solved by a computer.

A skeptical reader will ask: so what? Why do you need to be able to solve these “specific issues”?

I'll try to answer. The fact is that braid theory, as already mentioned, has many applications both in mathematics and beyond. I have focused here on only one application that is especially valuable - the application to the theory of knots.

Let's first look at a few examples of nodes (Fig. 9). A knot is a closed line in space, smooth or broken, which can be twisted and intertwined as desired. It is useful to imagine that the knot is made of a thin, flexible and extensible thread; we consider that two knots are the same (equivalent) if one can be turned into an exact copy of the other by moving, bending, stretching and skimming this thread in space without breaking it. Among the knots, a trivial knot stands out (actually, not a knot at all) - a common, insignificant circle: $Y_0$ on figure 9. However, this figure shows two images of a trivial knot: the knot $Y_7$ is also trivial - it is easy to untangle, turning it into a large circle (do this mentally or with a pencil and eraser!). Moreover, at figure 9, in addition to $Y_0$ and $Y_7$, there is another pair of equivalent nodes.

Exercise 5. Find two non-trivial equivalent nodes on figure 9.

It is more convenient for mathematicians to have a precise definition of the equivalence of nodes, rather than a visual description like the one we gave above. Let's give this definition. To do this, we assume that the node is not a smoothly bending curve, but a broken one. Then an elementary operation is the replacement of one link of a broken line - say, the broken line $AB$ on figure 10 - with a two-line broken line $ACB$ (and the reverse transition from $ACB$ to $AB$), provided that the node does not contain internal points of the triangle $ABC$. Two nodes will be equivalent if one can go from one to the other using a finite sequence of operation elements (Fig. 10). Having looked at this figure, you can easily understand that this definition of equivalence is actually obtueed by the above visual description.
