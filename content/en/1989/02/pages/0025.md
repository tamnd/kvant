At 0 °C, pieces of ice appear in the beams. An initially homogeneous system—liquid water—splits into two parts. They are called two different phases of the chemical compound H₂O. The same compound can also exist in the form of a third phase - water vapor.

Liquid, crystal and vapor are the most common examples of different phases of one substance. However, the phases do not necessarily differ from each other precisely in their state of aggregation. Two solid phases of carbon are well known: graphite and diamond. They have different crystal lattices, and this leads to a colossal difference in their properties. Phases can differ from each other in their chemical composition, magnetic characteristics (magnetic and non-magnetic), electrical conductivity (normal and superconducting), and other properties.

Now we will try to introduce the concept of phase more strictly. The basis is a laconic definition by Academician M.A. Leontovich: “In thermodynamics, a phase is any homogeneous system, that is, a body whose physical properties are the same at all points.” There are a few subtleties to note here.

Firstly, when we talk about a “body,” we always mean that it consists of a large number of micro-essentials - atoms or molecules. As a rule, the measure of “large quantity” is Avogadro’s number - 6·10²³.

“Homogeneity”, which appears in the definition, must also be understood at the level of a “large number” of particles, and not individual atoms or molecules. Air is homogeneous and is an unambiguous system, although it consists of different molecules.
