It is simply amazing how many interesting questions can arise when observing the most common phenomena. Well, for example... Why do ice and water, without transforming into each other, exist only at 0 °C? The temperature changes slightly and either the ice begins to melt or the water begins to freeze. For us, this is, of course, a happy circumstance, because otherwise slush and ice would happen much more often. And yet: why is this so? Of course, he won’t answer right away, but the first question causes a lot of confusion from others. Serving memory suggests that water also has a third “guise” - water vapor. So, can water, steam and ice exist in one state at the same time without turning into each other? Most likely, you know the answer to this question. But any element from the periodic table is possible. Almost everyone can exist in liquid, solid, and gaseous states. And at the same time, in the same state?

As you may have guessed, in this article we will try to answer these and many other questions. To do this, let's try to trace the narrow tropics of the amazing conclusions that were discovered more than 100 years ago by the great American physicist Josiah Willard Gibbs.

What is a phase?

The word "phase" has many meanings. We are interested in Gibbs's practice. To understand what the Gibbs phase is, let's teach with examples.

At 0 °C, pieces of ice appear in the beams. An initially homogeneous system—liquid water—splits into two parts. They are called two different phases of the chemical compound H₂O. The same compound can also exist in the form of a third phase - water vapor.

Liquid, crystal and vapor are the most common examples of different phases of one substance. However, the phases do not necessarily differ from each other precisely in their state of aggregation. Two solid phases of carbon are well known: graphite and diamond. They have different crystal lattices, and this leads to a colossal difference in their properties. Phases can differ from each other in their chemical composition, magnetic characteristics (magnetic and non-magnetic), electrical conductivity (normal and superconducting), and other properties.

Now we will try to introduce the concept of phase more strictly. The basis is a laconic definition by Academician M.A. Leontovich: “In thermodynamics, a phase is any homogeneous system, that is, a body whose physical properties are the same at all points.” There are a few subtleties to note here.

Firstly, when we talk about a “body,” we always mean that it consists of a large number of micro-essentials - atoms or molecules. As a rule, the measure of “large quantity” is Avogadro’s number - 6·10²³.

“Homogeneity”, which appears in the definition, must also be understood at the level of a “large number” of particles, and not individual atoms or molecules. Air is homogeneous and is an unambiguous system, although it consists of different molecules.

In a multiphase system, homogeneity is lost, as was the case with ice and water. Between different phases

One system generally has a clearly defined interface. Let's give another example here. Take a container with sunflower oil and add water into it. The liquids will not mix. Water in oil forms its own separate phase. The interface between the two phases will not be visible.

The heterogeneity of the system and the interface are not always visible in a non-instrumental way, and only with the help of a microscope can we be convinced of its multiphase nature.

When external conditions change (temperature, pressure, electric or magnetic fields), the phases can transform into each other (this is quite clear from the example of water, steam and ice). These processes are called phase transformations.

Introduction to Phase Diagrams

Let's start studying phase transformations with the same pure water. To do this, let us enclose it in a sealed vessel with a piston, where its state can be easily controlled by changing the external pressure on the piston and heating or cooling the vessel. It is important to understand that we do not have any other ways to influence the phase state of water in a vessel without opening it.*) Therefore, all “events” associated with phase transformations in a closed vessel should be a function of only p and T.

Let us pay attention to one circumstance: if a constant temperature T is maintained in the external environment, then after some time the same temperature is established in all phases of the system. This position is confirmed by our everyday experience - when there is contact, the temperatures of different bodies are equalized.

The same is true for pressure, but with one caveat: the pressure in different phases is the same and equal to the external one if all the interfaces between the phases are flat.*) Then the equality of pressure follows from the mechanical equilibrium of the interface. If the interface has a finite radius of curvature, then surface tension forces must also be taken into account. However, in

In what follows we will assume that the phase boundaries are always flat. Taking these remarks into account, the state of the system is characterized by pressure and temperature values ​​common to all phases.

First of all, it is desirable to find out under what external conditions the phase transformation occurs. To determine this, let’s focus on the evaporation of water. If we fix normal atmospheric pressure, then water in our state begins to evaporate at 100 °C. At lower temperatures, the piston in the vessel will “lie” on the water, and there will be no steam in the system.

By changing the pressure, we will also change the evaporation temperature. You can summarize all the data on the phase transformation into a table of two columns: one indicates the pressure, and the other indicates the evaporation temperature. But it is even more convenient to display the table data graphically as a dependence of temperature on pressure (Fig. 1). In the same way, it is possible to present in the form of graphs data on two other phase transformations that can occur in the system - the transformations water ← ice and ice ← steam. As a result, we get a diagram like the one in figure 2.

Let me emphasize one important point. The lines on the diagram separate the areas of stable existence of the corresponding phases from each other. This does not mean that each phase “lives” only in its own area and cannot “merge” into someone else’s. Of course it can. We regularly observe this, say, in the spring, when the rivers are still full of ice even at positive temperatures. But this state is not stable - the ice in the river is melting. This process does not occur instantly, but at some finite speed. After a few days (if frost does not strike again), the ice begins to grow in weight until the end. But under the same conditions, water will never become ice again.

If the state of a system ceases over time, it is said to have reached equilibrium. The diagram at figure 2 represents only the equilibrium state of the system.

Such diagrams are called phase diagrams. They are very convenient to use: knowing the temperature and pressure, we can immediately determine which phases of the system will be in equilibrium. Let us determine the value of pressure and temperature on the diagram that corresponds to the point. If it falls into the region of stable existence of steam, ice or liquid, then the system will be in equilibrium with a state of one corresponding phase. If the point is on the line of the diagram, the system in equilibrium will be a state of two phases. Which ones exactly? It is absolutely obvious: if, for example, this is the dividing line between the “water” and “ice” regions, then it is made of ice and water. Finally, with a single combination of pressure and temperature, all three phases will be in equilibrium.

The phase rule has always been considered one of Gibbs' most significant achievements. The strict derivation of the rule is quite complicated. We will present it in an essentially forgiven form.

Let's start our consideration with the same water in a closed sauce.*) Let us assume that for the given values ​​of $p$ and $T$ the liquid phase and vapor are in equilibrium. Molecules $H_2O$ in vapor and in liquid are in constant tell motion. As a result, some of them leave their “native” phase and enter the neighboring one. The average number of molecules that fly away per unit time through a unit area from a liquid is denoted by $q_{ж}$. What could this number depend on? As already noted, the state of affairs in a closed sauce depends only on the set equilibrium temperature of 100 °C.

Now we need to introduce a new concept - the number of degrees of freedom. This is how thermodynamics commonly refers to the degree of external parameters that, within certain limits, can be changed independently of each other without changing the phase state of the system. In the case we are studying, there are only two external parameters - pressure and

Phase rule for multicomponent systems

Pure water, pure bismuth, or any other pure substance is relatively rare. Significant value comes precisely up to various multi-component systems. For example, with rasters. The simplest example of a solution is water, which consists of two components - sugar and water, each of which is a type of molecule.

To describe the state of the solution, it is necessary, in addition to temperature and pressure, to indicate the relative content of all components, i.e. their concentration. It is clear that in a system consisting of K components, the value (K - 1) of concentration should be indicated.

For phases in equilibrium, temperature and pressure are common (identical) parameters. But equality of concentration does not follow. Moreover, it is easy to give an example of the opposite sense. If you put too much sugar in tea, it will not be placed even with long and intensive work with a spoon. A two-phase equilibrium will be established between the liquid trail tea and the solid sugar lying at the bottom. It is clear that the concentrations of the components in each of the two phases are completely different.

Taking this circumstance into account, let's try to describe the situation mathematically.

Let us assume that in a system of K components the phases are in equilibrium. Now the equilibrium conditions should be written down for each component:

$$q^{(1)}_I = q^{(1)}_{II} = q^{(1)}_{III} = \dots = q^{(1)}_{\Phi},$$

$$q^{(2)}_I = q^{(2)}_{II} = q^{(2)}_{III} = \dots = q^{(2)}_{\Phi},$$

$$q^{(K)}_I = q^{(K)}_{II} = q^{(K)}_{III} = \dots = q^{(K)}_{\Phi}$$

(the lower index indicates the phase number, and the upper index indicates the component number). This system of equality is a non-representational generalization of equilibrium conditions in a one-component system.

Each of the values ​​of $q$ must depend on the general pressure and temperature - on $p$ and $T$ - and on (K-1) concentration in its phase. Thus, the total number of parameters that must be specified to describe the equilibrium is $2 + (K-1)\Phi$. Now let's clean up the total number of the equation in the last system. For each component it is equal to (Ф - 1), and for the total components K. So, we get K(Ф - 1).
