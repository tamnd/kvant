F. P. Grosu from Chisinau studies the solution in natural numbers of the equation $x^n + y^m = z^k$. It turns out that if $k \geq n > m$, $x \geq y$ and $k/n = p$ are a natural number, then there are no solutions. Proof. Because $x \geq y$, $x^m \geq y^m$ and $x^n + x^m \geq z^k$. Since $(x+1)^n > x^n + x^m$, $(x+1)^n > z^k$. Therefore, $x > z^p - 1$. On the other hand, $x = \sqrt[n]{z^k - y^m} < z^p$. So $z^p > x > z^p - 1$, so $x$ cannot be an integer.

The following statement is proved in a similar way:

if the equation system

$$\begin{cases} x^n + y^m = a \\ x^m + y^n = b \end{cases}$$

with $n > m \geq 1$, $a > b$ has a natural solution, then this solution has the form

$$x = \sqrt[n]{a}, y = \sqrt[m]{a - \sqrt[n]{a}} = \sqrt[n]{b - \sqrt[n]{a}}^m,$$

where square brackets indicate the integer part of the number. For example,

$$\begin{cases} x^3 + y^2 = 134 \\ x^2 + y^3 = 52 \end{cases}$$

V. E. Koshakov from Leningrad offers the following game with numbers. Let's take an odd natural number $N$ and represent it as the sum of two terms. For each partition $N$ into a sum of the form $p+q$, where $p < q$, we construct a new partition of the form $(2p) + (q-p)$. What happens if you continue this process? For example, splitting the number $17 = 1 + 16$ results in an integer

$$1 + 16 \rightarrow 2 + 15 \rightarrow 4 + 13 \rightarrow 8 + 9 \rightarrow 16 + 1.$$

After this splitting is repeated. The author calls such chains nets. Here's another number 17 loop:

$$3 + 14 \rightarrow 6 + 11 \rightarrow 5 + 12 \rightarrow 7 + 10 \rightarrow 3 + 14.$$

The loops written out contain all partitions of the number 17 into the sum of two terms. Please note that both loops are the same length. This is no coincidence: all loops of a prime number have equal lengths. Try to prove this statement yourself.

V. E. Koshakov continued a large number of calculations and discovered numbers that had a lot of loops. For example, the number 65537 has 2048 loops. Think about what will happen if you replace the number with the number in a certain loop.

"Fluffy Stone"
and own parts of the resonator

In the vicinity of Baku, where the stone desert of Kobystan - “Country of Ravines” - begins, there is an amazing stone. He became so famous that he even has his own name - Nagaradash, i.e. “Stone tambourine” (nagara is an Azerbaijani percussion musical instrument, dash is a stone). This stone block has remarkable acoustic properties. If you hit it with a stone (and everyone who is nearby continues this with great pleasure), then such a loud and clear sound is released that it seems as if they hit a good-sounding bell...

Let's try to describe the acoustic properties of the “pyosh stone” in the language of physics. It is clear that it is premature in this case to talk about the frequency at which the stone sounds. Let's see how part of the sound vibrations $f$ is related to the dimensions of the “tambourine” $l$.

Upon impact, a short-term deformation of the stone will occur, and sound waves will propagate in all directions from the point of impact. They run at a certain speed $v$, which does not depend on the size of the stone. But the wave propagates continuously only in a homogeneous boundless medium, and all objects have boundaries where one substance comes into contact with another (in our case, a stone with an impact). Having reached the boundaries, the wave partially penetrates into another medium (sound from the stone penetrates the impact, subsequently reaching our ears), and is partially reflected back. The greater the difference in the speed of sound in two media, the more effective its reflection will be. In granite it is $v \approx 4 \cdot 10^3$ m/s, and in impact it is $v \approx 3,3 \cdot 10^2$ m/s, so only a small part of the sound energy goes out into impact, and the main part of it must be “banned” inside the stone. (The sound could also come out into rocky soil, but a “bulky stone” lying on other stones has a weak “acoustic connection” with the soil - it comes into contact with it only at a few points.)

Such objects, within which vibrational energy is quickly used, are called resonators (from the Latin word resonо - I sound in response, I respond). If the oscillations in the resonator are weakly damped, the resonator is said to have a high quality factor.

However, let's return to what happens inside the “puosh stone” after the impact. Sound waves are repeatedly reflected from the walls and interfering with each other, either absorbed or mutually weakened. An increase in oscillations occurs when a wave, having experienced one or more reflections, returns to a given point in the same phase from which its path began. Considering that the time it takes for the wave to travel back and forth along the sounding object is approximately $2l/v$, we obtain the condition for amplification of oscillations: $2l/v \approx nT=n/f$ ($n=1, 2, 3, ...$ is an integer). From this follows the formula for frequencies, which are called the natural frequencies of the resonator:

$$f_n \approx \frac{v}{2l} n.$$

With $n=1$ we get the naimization, or, as we say, the main part $f_1 \approx v/2l$.

The question may arise: why is the equality in formula (1) approximate and not exact? This is easy to understand if we remember that we denoted the “dimensions” of the resonator with the letter $l$.

$$f_1 \approx 0.33 \frac{v}{R}.$$

It is clear that in this case $f_1$ differs little from the approximation of this approximation (1), if we count $l=R$ and $n=1$ in the latter.

The Earth's atmosphere is also a resonator

The majority of the entire mass of atmospheric air is contained in a layer with a thickness of ~6 km, and if you rise to a height of 30 km, then ~99% of the entire atmosphere remains visible. Therefore, we can conditionally talk about the “border” of the atmosphere. Let's imagine that in some place the “border”, under the influence of certain forces, rose a little, after which the force ceased to act. The force of gravity will return the “hump” that has arisen back, but the one that has arisen, moving by inertia, will sink even lower, and a “hollow” will appear in place of the “hump.” Fluctuations in the thickness of the atmosphere spread in all directions from the location of the disturbance in the form of waves similar to those observed on the surface of the ocean. But since there is no sharp boundary of the atmosphere, wave-like vibrations cover its entire thickness, they are called internal granite waves (IGW)*.

Global electromagnetic resonances

Under the influence of solar radiation in the upper layers of the atmosphere, atoms and molecules are ionized, free charges appear, and the electrical conductivity of the air, starting from a height of 50-60 km, increases greatly. This is where the ionosphere begins, which at large distances from the Earth “closes” with interplanetary space. The lower layers of the atmosphere, several tens of kilometers thick, have very low electromagnetic waves (radio waves). What are the natural frequencies of this spherical resonance? The answer is expressed using formula (2), but with the speed of sound $v$ replaced by the speed of light $c=3 \cdot 10^8$ m/s.
