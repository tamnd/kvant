Laser refractometer

Candidate of Physical Sciences
D. D. KHRISTOSOV
(Plovdiv, NRB)

With the advent of lasers, many optical experiments and demonstrations have become simpler and more visual. This applies, for example, to experiments on diffraction of monochromatic light.

The device we want to pair you with is called a laser refractometer. With its help, it is easy and quick to change the speed of light in any liquid medium and thereby determine an important physical characteristic of this medium - the refractive index.

A laser refractometer is easy to make in a school physics lab. The most important element of the device, schematically depicted in figure, is the final laser (L). For example, you can take a milliliter helium-neon laser, which is now the most common. You also need a diffraction grating (P). The bath (B) for the liquid should be made from some kind of material, for example plaster glass, and given a trapezoidal shape. The role of the photodetector in a laser refractometer is performed directly by the eye of the observer.

The diffraction grating can be mounted on the small side wall of the bath, then the diffraction pattern will be conveniently observed on the large side wall-screen (E). To make the observed picture clear and absent, it is better to make the screen wall matte, and to avoid unwanted reflections, it would be good to blacken the internal non-parallel walls of the bathtub.

Now about the experiment itself.

The speed of light c in vacuum and the speed of light v in some transparent medium can be represented as follows:

$$c = \lambda_0 v, \quad v = \lambda v.$$

Here $\lambda_0$ and $\lambda$ are the corresponding wavelengths, $v$ is the radiation frequency. Dividing the equalities term by term, we get

$$v = c \frac{\lambda}{\lambda_0}.$$

(1)

The diffraction grating creates a picture of alternating maximums and minimums of illumination on the screen. Maxima are observed in directions determined by the following condition:

$$d \sin \varphi = k \lambda,$$

where $d$ is the grating period, $\varphi$ is the observation angle, $k = 0, \pm 1, \pm 2, \dots$ is the number of the corresponding maximum.

We will observe the first order maximum. Then, setting $k = 1$ and taking into account that for small angles sin $\varphi = tg \varphi$, we write the maximum conditions for the case of vacuum and this medium in the form (see figure)

$$\frac{db_0}{l} = \lambda_0, \quad \frac{db}{l} = \lambda.$$

If these equalities are divided by each other and the ratio $\lambda/\lambda_0$ is substituted into expression (1), we obtain the working formula:

$$v = c \frac{b}{b_0}.$$

(2)

According to this formula, using the known speed of light in vacuum $c = 3 \cdot 10^8$ m/s, changing $b_0$ and $b$, one can find the speed of light $v$ in the interdrying environment. The whole problem will simply come down to changing the distances $b_0$ and $b$.

As experience shows, these distances turn out to be quite large, and therefore the measurement accuracy is quite high (if the length of the bath $l$ is about 0.2-1 m).

Formula (2) can be rewritten as

$$n = \frac{c}{v} = \frac{b_0}{b},$$

where $n$ is the absolute refractive index of the transparent medium under study. This means that using the proposed device, along with the speed of light in a substance, it is also possible to determine its refractive index.

As is known, the first classical experiments to determine the speed of light in a transparent medium were carried out a very long time ago - in the middle of the 19th century - and, of course, with impossible light sources. The experiments were quite cumbersome, and the accuracy of the measurements was low. The laser makes it possible to test the necessary experiment quickly, clearly and clearly.
