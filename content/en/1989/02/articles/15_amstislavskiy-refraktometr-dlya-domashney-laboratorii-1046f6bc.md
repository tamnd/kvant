Refractometer for home laboratory

Candidate of Pedagogical Sciences
Y. E. AMSTISLAVSKY

It turns out that with the help of simple and accessible at home methods, it is possible to measure important optical characteristics of some physical devices and properties of matter.

Consider, for example, a simple method for measuring the focal lengths of a line and mirrors and the radii of curvature of their surface - this can be useful to an astronomer - and a method for determining the refractive index of a liquid - and this is useful for everyone.

Experiment 1. Let's take a small translucent screen in the form of a sheet of wool, in the middle part of which there is a light bulb from a pocket camera.

Narika. (The illuminator screen will be discussed in more detail below.) We fix the light bulb so that its filament is in the plane of the screen. Let's place the illuminator-screen $\partial$ above the concave spherical mirror $\partial$ lying on the table in such a way that the thread $S$, playing the role of an accurate light source, is close to the main optical axis of the screen (the line connecting the top of the screen with the center of its spherical surface), and the plane of the screen is perpendicular to this axis (Fig. 1).

By moving the screen in height and observing it from above, we can easily find a position in which a sharp and bright image $S'$ appears on the screen next to the source $S$. Achieving this position means that the center of curvature of the screen is also located on the surface of the screen - only in this case, the rays from the source go almost normally to the spherical surface of the screen and, changing their direction to the opposite when reflected, are focused near $S$. To determine the radius of curvature $R$ of the screen, all that remains is to change the distance from the screen $\partial$ to the stripe (vertex) of the screen $\partial$.

Routine 2. Let’s set up the experiment by pouring a small amount of the test liquid, for example water, onto the screen $\partial$. Then a thin liquid line $J_{\pm}$ (Fig. 2, a) is formed on top of the screen, and the first line along the ray of the surface of this line is flat, i.e., has a radius of curvature $r_1 \rightarrow \infty$, and the second is convex - it is in contact with the surface of the screen, i.e. $r_2 = R$.

If the water does not cover the entire mirror, then the image $S'$ on the screen $\partial$, distant from the screen $\partial$, is preserved - it creates a free part of the screen surface. But, lowering the screen by about 0.3 $R$ (this value is different for different liquids), we get a second color and bright image $S''$, forming that part of the screen that is covered with liquid. For this image to appear in the plane of the screen, it is necessary that, after refraction on a flat liquid surface, the line falls on the screen again almost normally to its surface. Only under such reflection conditions do the rays change their direction by 180° and form an image $S''$ adjacent to $S$.

Let's assume that we have achieved the appearance of a clear image $S''$. Let us denote the corresponding distance from the screen plane to the screen stripe by $d$. Let us now separate the mental liquid line $J_{\pm}$ from the screen (Fig. 2, b). In the absence of a screen, the rays, which, as we have established, fall normally on the lower surface of the line, would pass this surface without deflection and form a mini-image $S'$ at the point of intersection of the continuation of the rays, i.e. at the center of curvature of the spherical surface at a distance $f = R$. Therefore, the thin line formula for our case looks like:

$$\frac{1}{d} - \frac{1}{R} = \frac{1}{F},$$

where $F$ is the focal length of the line.

What might it depend on? Obviously, from the refractive index of the liquid $n$ (we assume that the effect surrounding the lens in its optical parameters does not differ from vacuum) and from the radii of curvature of its surfaces $r_1$ and $r_2$. As calculations show (you can easily reproduce them, for which you only need the law of light refraction and a little geometry), the focal length of a thin lens is determined by the expression:

$$\frac{1}{F} = (n - 1) \left( \frac{1}{r_1} - \frac{1}{r_2} \right),$$

moreover, for a surface convex along the bow, $r > 0$, and for a thin wave, $r < 0$. In our case $r_1 \to \infty$, and $r_2 = -R$, therefore from equalities (1) and (2) we have

$$n = R/d.$$

Thus, to determine the refractive index of a liquid, it is necessary to change the lens two distances from the screen $\theta$ to the mirror $3$, at which clear images $S'$ and $S''$ are formed on the screen, and find the ratio of the larger distance to the smaller one.

Routine 3. Let us now replace the liquid lens $\lambda_j$ with a glass lens $\lambda_1$, and the wave mirror $\theta$ with a small flat mirror $\delta_1$ (Fig. 3; for clarity, the lens is slightly raised above the mirror). By moving the screen illuminator $\theta$ in height, a bright image $S'$ is added to it next to the source $S$. It's easy to see that this will happen when the screen aligns with the focal plane of the lens. Therefore for

$$\frac{1}{F} = (n - 1) \left( \frac{1}{r_1} - \frac{1}{r_2} \right),$$

To limit the focal length $F_1$ of the lens, it is enough to change the distance from the screen in its specified position to the central section of the lens. (Note that a similar method, known as the autolimation method, is used to establish the initial orientation of the telescope required in a number of optical schemes.)

Routine 4. Let us now bring the illuminator-screen $\theta$ closer to the lens $\lambda_1$. At a certain distance $d_1$, which for a biconvex symmetrical lens is about 0.5 $F_1$, a clear image appears on the screen again - $S''$, although not as bright as $S'$. It is created due to reflection, albeit relatively weak, from the lower polished surface of the lens, which plays the role of a wave mirror with a low reflection coefficient ($\varphi \approx 0,05$). It is not difficult to verify this by taking the lens from the mirror and crossing it directly onto the table (or onto a book, or onto a dark surface): the image $S''$ is preserved and even becomes more contrasting.

The nature of the image $S''$ is the same as in the case of a liquid lens in the second optet, with the difference that the liquid lens is replaced by a glass one and the first surface of the lens along the bow can be convex, and the reflection coefficient from the lower surface of the lens is small. Therefore, we apply formula (1) to image $S''$. By measuring $F_1$ (by image position $S'$), $d_1$ (by image position $S''$) and using formula (1), we can calculate the radius of curvature $r$ of the lower lens surface $\lambda_1$ along the bow:

$$r_1 = \frac{F_1 d_1}{F_1 - d_1}.$$

If the surfaces of the lens have different curvatures, then it is necessary to change two values ​​$d_1$ and $d_2$ for the case when the lens lies on one and the other of its surfaces, and calculate $r_1$ and $r_2$.

Experiment 5. Using a glass lens and a flat mirror, it is not difficult to determine the refractive index $n_{ж}$ of an arbitrary unknown liquid, and, unlike the method discussed in the second experiment, for this purpose only a few drops of liquid are sufficient, which, moreover, may not be transparent.

Let's apply 3-4 drops of the liquid under study to the mirror $3_1$ and cover it with lens $J_1$ (Fig. 4). A thin concave-planar liquid lens $J_2$ is formed between the surface of the lens and the surface of the mirror. The first surface along the bow is concave - it is in contact with the second surface of the glass lens, i.e. $r_{ж} = -r,$ and the second surface of the liquid lens is flat, i.e. $r_{ж} \to \infty,$ and formula (2) for $J_2$ takes the form

$$\frac{1}{F_2} = -(n_{ж} - 1)\frac{1}{r}.$$

Lens $J_2$ together with glass lens $J_1$ form an optical system $J_3,$ whose focal length $F_3$ can be changed in the manner described in the third experiment. For an optical system $J_3,$ consisting of two adjacent thin lenses, we have (this expression is not difficult to obtain on your own)

$$\frac{1}{F_3} = \frac{1}{F_1} + \frac{1}{F_2}.$$

Let us express from here the optical power of a liquid lens through easily variable focal lengths $F_1$ and $F_3$:

$$\frac{1}{F_2} = -\frac{F_3 - F_1}{F_1 F_3}.$$

Then for $n_{ж}$ we get

$$n_{ж} = 1 + \frac{d_1(F_3 - F_1)}{F_3(F_1 - d_1)}.$$

If the surfaces of the lens have different radii of curvature, then, touching the liquid with different surfaces of the lens, we will change different pairs of values ​​$d_1$ and $F_3.$ But, regardless of the orientation of such a lens, both calculated values ​​$n_{ж}$ for a given liquid should be the same (within the possible error of the result). Therefore, in the case of an asymmetric lens, you can conduct two experiments with the same liquid and obtain two values ​​of the desired quantity $n_{ж}.$

Experiment 6. In conclusion, consider a case that is a combination of previous experiments. Let's take glass from glasses for further use - a positive meniscus lens $J_m$ of small optical power of 1.5-2 diopters. Let us carry out three experiments with this glass in succession: No. 1, No. 3 and No. 3 + No. 2 (the experimental diagrams are shown in figure 5) and change the corresponding values ​​$R_1, F_1$ and $F_3.$ Then, based on formulas (2) and (5), we obtain the following expression for calculating the refractive index on the surface of the lens $J_m$ liquid:

$$n_{ж} = 1 + \frac{R_1(F_1 - F_3)}{F_1 F_3}.$$

What you need to make with your own hands in a home laboratory is a screen illuminator. To this end, proceed as follows. A circle with a diameter of 20-25 mm is cut out of cardboard, a hole with a diameter of 8-9 mm is made in its middle part, this circle is “threaded” onto the ring of a light bulb from a flashlight and pushed all the way to the lamp canister. The stretched cardboard circle serves as the basis for a spacer paper ring with a screen glued to it. The ring is made from a batman strip 15 mm wide and 45 mm long. Along the opposite long edges of the strip, neat transverse slits 5 mm deep are made with scissors, following each other every 2 mm. Then, near one of the ends of the strip, place three “petals” on each edge, lubricate the resulting “tail” of the strip with glue, roll up the strip and glue the resulting ring, pressing the tail to the middle part of the opposite end of the strip. After the glue has dried, all the petals are bent to the outer area by 90° and the resulting “wheel” is glued as a spacer to a cardboard circle, and a Batman screen the size of half a sheet of paper from a school sketchbook (for example, 15x20 cm) with a 12-13 mm diameter in the central part is glued to the opposite side of the wheel. The width of the spacer wheel is expanded so that the light bulbs are in the plane of the screen. Two thin flexible conductors 1.5-2 m long are connected to the base of the light bulb, which during the experiment are connected to the terminals of 4.5 V flashlight batteries. The base can be wrapped in one layer of electrical tape and the illuminator-screen can be held by it during experiments. To reduce unnecessary staining of the side screen from the light bulb, the spacer wheel can be covered from the inside with strips of black paper.

The methods for producing quantities $R, F, n$ and $n_{ж}$ considered in experiments 1–6 are almost equivalent in concept and availability of execution. However, there are still some differences. For example, if the measurement base is of magnitude $n_{ж}$ and the home laboratory has some choice of mirror and line, then preference should be given to optics with a large radius of curvature of the surface. With $R \sim 0,5$ m and careful measurements, the relative error of the result does not exceed 1%. Of the three methods for determining $n_{ж}$, described in experiments 2, 5, 6, method 5 differs in that, as has already been noted, it allows one to limit oneself to only a few drops of the liquid under study. However, we will focus in detail only on the method described in experiment 6.
