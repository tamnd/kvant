ARCHIMEDES' LAW FROM A MATHEMATICS POINT OF VIEW

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
S. L. TABACHNIKOV

Probably everyone is familiar with the story of how Archimedes shouted “Eureka!” looked out of the bath... The reason for the work was the law he discovered:
A body immersed in a liquid loses as much in everything as it weighs when enclosing the liquid in its volume.
It is, of course, pleasant to discover such a simple and elegant law. However, I want to convince you that this law is, in essence, very clear. Judge for yourself.
figure 1 shows a body immersed in a liquid in red. And next to it the exact volume of liquid is shown. This (blue) volume is, of course, motionless. This means that the force of gravity acting on it is compensated by a force directed upward from the liquid. Exactly the same force acts on the red body. This means that it loses as much as it weighs when it contains liquid in its volume.

That's all the proof of Archimedes' law. I did not think that Archimedes himself knew this reasoning, but I am sure that he would have appreciated its simplicity. However, it would hardly satisfy a physicist - after all, not a word is said about the nature of the buoyant force. This is, rather, the reasoning of a mathematician...

I suggest you try yourself in the role of Archimedes. Imagine that the entire space is filled with water and two air bubbles are floating in this water (and there is nothing else in the world) - figure 2. How will these bubbles behave: will they move towards each other, repel each other, or simply stay in place (of course, we neglect the force of friction on the water)? Come on, try to guess the answer!

Now let's solve this problem together. Let's start by replacing the air in one of the bubbles with water, and we won't touch the second bubble. Then only one air bubble will float in the water, and in the place of the second there will be a allocated volume of water A (Fig. 3). It is clear that all water, including volume A, will be motionless. Let us mentally identify a volume of water B, symmetrical to an air bubble relative to A, and ask the question: what forces act on volume A?

Firstly, this is the force f of attraction to the air bubble directed to the left, and secondly, the force F of attraction to volume B is directed to the right. All other forces balance each other: for example, symmetrical volumes B and G at figure 3 attract volume A with equal forces.

Now let's do a little math. Let us denote the masses of air bubbles by p and q, and the masses of water volume bubbles by P and Q. Air is lighter than water, so the numbers denoted by small letters are smaller than the numbers denoted by large ones: p < P and q < Q. We need

know the force with which different bodies are attracted to each other. The answer is given by the law of universal gravitation:
two bodies of masses m and M, located at a distance R from each other, are mutually attracted with a force kmM/R²
(k is a constant positive coefficient, the value of which we will not need). In our calculations, the distance R will not change, so k/R² can be considered a constant factor and, for simplicity, not written down.

So, we will assume that bodies m and M attract with force mM. Therefore f=pQ and F=PQ. We see that f<F. However, the volume of water A does not think of moving to the right at all - it is motionless. This means that from the side of the liquid it is acted upon by a force F−f=PQ−pQ directed towards the air bubble. This force can be called “Archimedes”, and the formulated statement fully deserves the name “Archimedes’ law”.

Now comes the decisive moment of our deliberations. Let us again replace the volume of water A with an air bubble. What forces are acting on this bubble? The attractive force g towards the first bubble is directed to the left (Fig. 4), the attractive force G towards the volume of water B is directed to the right, and the Archimedean force F−f is directed to the left. The remaining forces balance each other.

Finding the forces g and G is no more difficult than f and F. Named: g=pq and G=Pq. Now we know all the forces acting on bubble A, and we can find their resultant - this is the force acting to the left
