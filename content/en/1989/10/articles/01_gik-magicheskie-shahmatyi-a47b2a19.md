MAGICAL
CHESS

Let's continue our acquaintance with unusual genres of chess composition, which are generally classified as fairy-tale, fantastic chess. Today's turn is magic chess, in which an amazing transformation of the pieces will occur.

Two types of magic chess have been invented. In one version, a magic field is indicated, when any figure appears on it, it changes its color. In the other, the proper specific figure has a special property.

K. Vidler, 1986
Cooperative checkmate in 4 moves.
The h8 square is magical.
As usual, in the cooperative problem, Black starts and helps White checkmate. After 1.Kh8 their knight turns into white. Now it's White's move and this knight comes into play - 1...Kf7! 2. Kph4 Kh8! Again the horse changes color and becomes black. 3. Kg6 Kph6 4. Kh8! The knight is white again and finishes the job - 4...Kg6×!

J. van Alten, 1986
Checkmate in 2 moves.
Field c4 is magical.

First, let's study the false trail: 1. Qg5 with the threat of 2. Qb5×. Refutations related to taking the c4 pawn do not work: after 1...R:c4 the rook becomes white, and follows 2. Nc3×; in case of 1...K:c4 follows 2. Kb6×. However, with a rough 1...f5 there is no checkmate on the second move.

Solves 1. Qf8! White's Teperhausa is more heartfelt: 2. Fa8×. And the c4 pawn, which takes away the b5 square from the king, is inviolable: 1...R:c4 - on c4 there is a white rook and 2. Q:b4×; 1...K:c4 - on c4 the white knight and 2. Qe8×!

Let's move on to the second type of magical chess: with the participation of magical pieces. If such a piece makes a move, then the pieces that fall under its attack (both aliens and swans) simultaneously change color. The figure also changes color and, after its move, comes under a magical attack.

G. Aust, 1987
Cooperative checkmate in 3 moves.
King d7 is magical.
1. Cre6! The f5 rook becomes white and moves. 1...Rf8 2. Kpd5! A white knight appears on c6, which restores its color after 2...Kd4 (it is again under attack from the king). 3. Cre4. Changes the color of three figures at once! 3...Re8× and the goal is achieved! The e3 pawn is black, but it indirectly protects the knights. It is true that when any of them is captured, the king's movement causes the color of the e3 pawn to change, and when White moves, the king is in check.

O. Farna, 1986
Cooperative checkmate in 2 moves.
Both kings are magical.
1. Kg1 (bishop h2 and knight f2 change color) 1...Kpg 3+ (both minor pieces change color again) 2. Kph1 (bishop black again) 2...Kpf3× (on f2 there is a white knight, square g1 is inaccessible to the black king - the white bishop h2 is revived).

In the last problem, all events took place within the 3x3 square in the lower right corner of the board. Truly a magic square! This replacement brings to mind another magic square found in mathematics. A magic (or magic) square of order n is an n×n square table filled with all integers from 1 to n², in which the sum of the numbers of each row, each column and the two main diagonals is the same. Magic squares have nothing to do with the game itself, but they appear in chess mathematics. For example, one of the most surprising solutions to the knight's move problem generates a semi-magic square.

Moving along the board according to this numbering, the knight goes around all the squares of the board; the route is even closed. The sums of the move numbers in rows and columns are equal to 260 (and along the diagonals - differences from 260).

Let's build a “model” of the design plane. First, all points of the upper hemisphere without the equator are glued to the corresponding points of the lower hemisphere; Now we also need to glue the diametrically opposite points of the equator - the boundaries of the hemispheres (Fig. 1). We transform the equator into a quadrilateral ABCD (Fig. 2, 3), glue its antifiber vertices A and C, B and D, and then its sides - AB with CD, AD with CB (Fig. 4); check that the rule for gluing equatorial points will be observed). When gluing the second pair of sides, self-intersection of the surface cannot be avoided. A line of self-intersection arises, each point of which must be represented as a double: one instance of the point belongs to one entity, the other to another. If, moving along the design plane, we pass through the line of self-intersection, then we will put the outer side of the surface on the inner one. Thus, in essence, this surface does not have any two sides - like the Möbius strip, it is one-sided.

If we glue each point of the sphere with a diametrically opposite point, we obtain a surface equivalent, up to continuous deformations, of the design plane (see pp. 10-11). We show two methods of such gluing.
