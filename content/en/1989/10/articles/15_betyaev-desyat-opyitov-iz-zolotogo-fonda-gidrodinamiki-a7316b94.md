7. Kelvin-Helmholtz stability*)

Hydrodynamic stability, which was already discussed in the first part of the article, is complex and multifaceted. Another amazing example on this topic is the stability of a surface separating two different liquids. From different sides of this surface, the tangential components of the fluid velocities are different,

they seem to slide relative to each other. The stability of a sliding surface is represented by the famous German natural physicist Kelvin (W. Thomson, 1824-1907).

There are three stages of stability: an increase in sinusodal disturbances, the formation and evolution of a spiral structure, and a random decay process. Well-stability manifests itself when the influence of stabilizing factors is insignificant. These include stable tension and positive development in the density of the lower and upper layers of the liquid, when the force of gravity acts in a direction perpendicular to the rupture surface.

In 1898, the English hydrodynamicist Hill-Shaw created an original installation, which was later named after him. Between two parallel glass plates he passed a clear liquid (water, glycerin) and introduced a red substance (Fig. 1) at several points in the inlet part of the tray. 9. Waves behind a moving spar

When a submarine or aircraft moves at subsonic speeds, the disturbances caused by immi propagate in all directions. We can hear the approach of a subsonic aircraft, for example, as soon as the power of the sound waves ahead of it exceeds the sensitivity threshold of the ear. And a supersonic plane overtakes the sound waves propagating from it, so we don’t hear its approach in advance.

If we talk about distances that are large compared to the size of the aircraft, then the aircraft can be considered a point. The front of sound waves from an exact source, in accordance with Huyens' principle, is the surface of a sphere. The disturbances generated by the plane when it is at point M, after time t, fills a sphere with radius vt, where v is the speed of sound (Fig. 6). During the same time, an airplane moving at speed u>v will cover the distance OM=ut. The disturbances initiated by the aircraft at each point of the segment OM will fit into a cone, which is called the Maxa cone. The generatrix OA of the Maxa cone touches the front sphere, “expanding” from an arbitrary point of the segment OM from the moment of time when the plane is at this point. The half-volume of the Maxa cone solution is equal to

$$\beta = \arcsin \frac{AM}{OM} = \arcsin \frac{vt}{ut} = \arcsin \frac{1}{M},$$

where the ratio of the speed of movement to the speed of sound M=u/v>0 is called the Maxa number.

Being at point B, we have a sound signal from an aircraft moving at supersonic speed at an altitude h=BC, when it covers the distance CO=BC stg α=h√M²−1. Moreover, the signal will be double, like “bang-bang”: “bang” - from the incident sound wave 1, “bang” - from the reflected sound wave 2 (see Fig. 6).

Ship waves have a completely different nature - they are formed as a result of vibrations of the sea surface under the influence of gravity forces. A photograph taken with an impact above the ship (Fig. 7) shows that the disturbances induce from the ship and the turbulence formed behind it on the left fill a wedge-shaped region with an angle of $\alpha \approx 19,5^\circ$. This angle corresponds to the Mach number

$$M = \frac{1}{\sin \alpha} \approx \frac{1}{\sin 19,5^\circ} \approx 3.$$

It turns out that any ship (as opposed to an airplane), regardless of its shape and size, moves as if its “effective” Mach number was 3.

10. Sphere in supersonic flow

The sound wave we just talked about is generated far from the aircraft. The situation is different near the plane. Here the wave is compressed, turning into a shock wave - a surface on which the velocity component normal to it suffers a break. Supersonic before the shock wave, the speed becomes subsonic after the shock wave.

Shock waves are easy to observe in wind tunnels. figure 8 shows a photograph of a flow around a sphere moving at a speed that corresponds to the Mach number $M=1,5$. Beyond the section of the bow shock wave that is located directly in front of the ball, the flow will be subsonic up to the angle $45^\circ$ (the angle of absence from the direction of flight of the ball). Then the flow accelerates and becomes supersonic. At an angle of approximately $90^\circ$, the laminar boundary layer breaks away from the ball, creating an oblique shock wave emanating from it, as if hanging above the turbulent wake.
