Media whose properties are not the same in different directions are called anisotropic. The reason for the anisotropy of crystals, as you now understand, is the non-equivalence of direction, resulting from the regular arrangement of atoms.

Another important manifestation of anisotropy is that the current in the crystal can flow in a direction other than that in which the electron field is directed. Let us explain what we are talking about. From formula (1) it is clear that the current density $j=I/S$ is proportional to the direction of the electric field $E=U/L$ applied to the sample:

$$j=\sigma E.$$

However, the current and field in the medium are characterized not only by their magnitude, but also by their direction. The field vector $\vec{E}$ specifies the magnitude and direction of the force $\vec{F}=e\vec{E}$ acting on each charge $e$. Under the influence of this force, the electrons move on average with a certain speed $\vec{v}$. The current density vector $\vec{j}$, which for brevity we will simply call the current vector, is parallel to the average velocity of the electrons $\vec{v}^*$). Ohm's law, written in the form (2), relates only the magnitudes of the current $j$ and the externally applied field, but says nothing about the relative orientation of the vectors $\vec{j}$ and $\vec{E}$.

“She’s very rude,” you say. — Field $\vec{E}$ and force $\vec{F}$ are parallel. The average speed of the electrons $\vec{v}$ is parallel to the acting force $\vec{F}$. And the current $\vec{j}$ is parallel to $\vec{v}$. This means that the vectors $\vec{j}$ and $\vec{E}$ are also parallel, and together (2) we can write:

$$\vec{j}=\sigma \vec{E}.$$

It's very simple!>

Is this reasoning correct? Yes... if we are talking about a manufactured environment - an environment in which all directions are equivalent. When all directions are equivalent, the electrons don't care which way they go, and once we apply an external field, they don't have to choose—the vector $\vec{E}$ gives a single vision of direction. The current $\vec{j}$ will flow along it.

By the way, $C_n$ is the generally accepted designation of the axis of symmetry; the number $n$ is called its "order".

From the point of view of symmetry, we can clarify the concept of directional equivalence: any directions that “turn into each other” when turning around the symmetry are equivalent.

The more high-order axes a crystal has, the more equivalent directions it has, the more strongly symmetry competes with anisotropy. How to and how not to handle rows

Ancient Greek mathematics expressed in disputes about the infinite. Back in the 5th century BC. e. The philosopher Zeno of Elea showed the results of careless handling of this tanistic concept. He “proves,” for example, that the swift Achilles never reaches the slowly crawling turtle, that the lightest arrow never moves from its place, and other equally paradoxical things. The basis of these paradoxes was the possibility of breaking a segment into an infinite number of unequal parts. To do this, it is enough to first split the segment in half, then split one of the resulting parts in half again and continue this operation endlessly many times (in mathematical folklore, this method is recommended to kill a lion in the desert, but divide not the segment, but the desert in half).

After Zeno's paradoxes, the concept of infiniteness was expelled from mathematics for a long time, and only in the 17th century was it restored. Without him, the creation of a new science - mathematical analysis - was unthinkable. And one of the most powerful means of this science was infinite series, that is, sums consisting of an infinite number of terms. After all, Zeno’s reasoning was already familiar that the whole consists of the sum of a half, a quarter, an eighth, etc., i.e., it was based on the equality

$$\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2^2} + \dots + \frac{1}{2^n} + \dots = 1.$$ (1)

It becomes more clear if we imagine that first half of a segment of length 1 is cut off, then half of the remainder, and this “cutting” is continued until the end. It is clear that nothing remains of the segment, i.e. the sum of the lengths of the cut parts is equal to 1. And this is expressed by equality (1).

More general than (1) is the equality

$$1 + q + q^2 + \dots + q^n + \dots = \frac{1}{1-q}.$$ (2)

Of course, it is true only if $|q| < 1$ - in this case, the models of terms are killed, and the value of the sum $1 + q + \dots + q^{n-1}$ of the first $n$ terms approaches $\frac{1}{1-q}$ as $n$ grows. But in the 18th century, scientists tried to use this equality for any values ​​of $q$. For example, putting $q = -1$, they got the equality

$$1 - 1 + 1 - 1 + 1 + \dots = \frac{1}{1+1} = \frac{1}{2},$$

and for $q = 3$ - equality

$$1 + 3 + 3^2 + \dots + 3^n + \dots = \frac{1}{1-3} = -\frac{1}{2}.$$

In general, in the 18th century, finite series were treated as finite sums. Without worrying about anything, the terms in them were rearranged, two finite series were multiplied by each other like ordinary polynomials, etc. But then it was discovered that with this approach the sum of half numbers can turn out to be a negative number (as we have already seen).

Modern mathematicians are trained to handle infinite series with caution. Scientists of the 18th century did not know all these subtleties, but the boldness with which they handled such series often brought them unexpected victories. And the bravest and most successful was the St. Petersburg academician Leonhard Euler (1707-1783). Series and infinity of prime numbers
Since the time of Euclid, a theorem has been known: there are infinitely many prime numbers. Euler came up with a new, extremely ingenious proof of this theorem. Let us put $q = \frac{1}{p}$ in formula (2), where $p$ is a prime number, i.e. $p = 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, \dots$. We get equalities
$$\frac{1}{1 - \frac{1}{2}} = 1 + \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2^2} + \dots + \frac{1}{2^n} + \dots,$$
$$\frac{1}{1 - \frac{1}{3}} = 1 + \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{3^2} + \dots + \frac{1}{3^n} + \dots,$$
$$\frac{1}{1 - \frac{1}{5}} = 1 + \frac{1}{5} + \frac{1}{5^2} + \dots + \frac{1}{5^n} + \dots,$$
