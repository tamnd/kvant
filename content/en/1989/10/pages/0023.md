IN THE MYSTERIOUS WORLD OF ENDLESS ROWS

Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
N. Y. VILENKIN

How to and how not to handle rows

Ancient Greek mathematics expressed in disputes about the infinite. Back in the 5th century BC. e. The philosopher Zeno of Elea showed the results of careless handling of this tanistic concept. He “proves,” for example, that the swift Achilles never reaches the slowly crawling turtle, that the lightest arrow never moves from its place, and other equally paradoxical things. The basis of these paradoxes was the possibility of breaking a segment into an infinite number of unequal parts. To do this, it is enough to first split the segment in half, then split one of the resulting parts in half again and continue this operation endlessly many times (in mathematical folklore, this method is recommended to kill a lion in the desert, but divide not the segment, but the desert in half).

After Zeno's paradoxes, the concept of infiniteness was expelled from mathematics for a long time, and only in the 17th century was it restored. Without him, the creation of a new science - mathematical analysis - was unthinkable. And one of the most powerful means of this science was infinite series, that is, sums consisting of an infinite number of terms. After all, Zeno’s reasoning was already familiar that the whole consists of the sum of a half, a quarter, an eighth, etc., i.e., it was based on the equality

$$\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2^2} + \dots + \frac{1}{2^n} + \dots = 1.$$ (1)

It becomes more clear if we imagine that first half of a segment of length 1 is cut off, then half of the remainder, and this “cutting” is continued until the end. It is clear that nothing remains of the segment, i.e. the sum of the lengths of the cut parts is equal to 1. And this is expressed by equality (1).

More general than (1) is the equality

$$1 + q + q^2 + \dots + q^n + \dots = \frac{1}{1-q}.$$ (2)

Of course, it is true only if $|q| < 1$ - in this case, the models of terms are killed, and the value of the sum $1 + q + \dots + q^{n-1}$ of the first $n$ terms approaches $\frac{1}{1-q}$ as $n$ grows. But in the 18th century, scientists tried to use this equality for any values ​​of $q$. For example, putting $q = -1$, they got the equality

$$1 - 1 + 1 - 1 + 1 + \dots = \frac{1}{1+1} = \frac{1}{2},$$

and for $q = 3$ - equality

$$1 + 3 + 3^2 + \dots + 3^n + \dots = \frac{1}{1-3} = -\frac{1}{2}.$$

In general, in the 18th century, finite series were treated as finite sums. Without worrying about anything, the terms in them were rearranged, two finite series were multiplied by each other like ordinary polynomials, etc. But then it was discovered that with this approach the sum of half numbers can turn out to be a negative number (as we have already seen).
