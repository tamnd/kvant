Regular polyhedra and rotations

V. N. VAGUTEN

Although stereometry is studied only in high school, every student is familiar with the cube, regular pairs and other simple polygons.
This article will cover two topics: the construction of all regular polytopes and the description of all rotations at which the polytopic is combined with its original position.

A correct $n$-pleaser on the plane - $n$-pleaser, for which all sides and all angles are equal - exists for any $n$: if you indicate on the plane the point $O$ (center) and $A_1$ (one of the vertices), then turn by angles multiples of $180^\circ/n$ around the point $O$ from $A_1$ turns out all the other vertices $A_2$, ..., $A_n$ correctly $n$-pleasure with center $O$.

But in space there are only a finite number of different regular polytopes.

Five toll phones. A polyhedron is called regular if all its faces are identical regular polygons and the dihedral angles at all edges are equal$^*$). From the definition it follows that a regular polyhedron is “perfectly symmetrical”: if we mark some face $\Gamma$ and one of its vertices $A$, then for any other face $\Gamma'$ and its vertex $A'$ one can combine the polyhedron with itself by moving in space so that the face $\Gamma$ is aligned with $\Gamma'$ and at the same time vertex $A$ will hit point $A'^{**}$.

*) Throughout we are talking only about convex polyhedra (lying on one side of the plane of each of their faces).
**) In this phrase, the word “face” can be replaced with the word “edge”.
