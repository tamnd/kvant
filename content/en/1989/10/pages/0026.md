And

$$\cos x = 1 - \frac{x^2}{2!} + \frac{x^4}{4!} + \dots + (-1)^{n-1} \frac{x^{2n-2}}{(2n-2)!} + \dots$$

For a modern mathematician, the above derivation of formulas (10)-(12) would serve as a target for various jokes. “For goodness sake,” he will say, “how is it known that the functions $e^x$, sin $x$, cos $x$ are generally expanded into power series? And who said that infinite series can be differentiated according to the same rules as polynomial ones? After all, an arbitrary sum of an infinite set of terms is not always equal to the sum of its derivatives, and it is generally impossible to integrate series term by term.” And so on, and so on. And the saddest thing is that he will be absolutely right. But in the 18th century they did not know these subtleties and, guided by the rule “Work, and faith will come to you,” mathematicians derived wonderful formulas, leaving their grandchildren to give strict proof of the results obtained by deeds.

Moreover, they boldly used series expansions not only for active, but also for complex values ​​of an argument. This allowed them to find values ​​for powers with complex exponents, sines and cosines of complex numbers. Consideration of functions in a complex domain allows us to establish their hiding properties.

Many of these properties are related to the Euler formula (8) mentioned above. To prove it, it is enough to substitute $ix$ into series (10) together with $x$, and then separately group the terms containing $i$ and the terms in which $i$ is not included. Do these calculations yourself. From Euler's formula it further follows that $\cos 2\pi + i \sin 2\pi = 1,$ and therefore $e^{z+2\pi i} = e^z e^{2\pi i} = e^z$. This means that the exponent function is periodic, like trionometric functions, only its period is the minimum number $2\pi i$. From Euler's formula it further follows that $\cos z = \frac{e^{iz} + e^{-iz}}{2}$ and therefore, for example, $\cos i = \frac{e^{i^2} + e^{-i^2}}{2} = \frac{e^{-1} + e}{2} > 1$.
