Archimedes' Law from a Mathematician's Point of View

Let us denote by $p$ the mass of an air bubble, and by $P$ the mass of an equal volume of water; through $Q$ is the mass of the metal ball, through $q$ is the mass of an equal volume of water. Then $p < P$, $q < Q$.

1. A volume of water with mass $P$ is acted on by the liquid by the Archimedean force $P(Q - q)$, in the direction from the metal ball.
2. The ball and bubble will repel. The following forces act on the air bubble: the tension force on the metal ball $pQ$; the tension force to the volume of water, symmetrical to the metal ball relative to the bubble, $pq$ and the Archimedean force of repulsion from the ball, found in the previous problem, $P(Q - q)$. Their equivalent is directed away from the metal ball and is equal to $(P - p)(Q - q)$.
3. A similar miracle shows that the balls will bend.

We will say that volumes $a, b, c, \dots, k$ form a series. By convention, the librarian swaps only volumes belonging to the same series. With each permutation, one volume falls into its place, and the series in which it was included is swung by 1. Thus, to put in place the $n$ volumes that form the series, $n - 1$ permutations are required. In total, the librarian will need to carry out (20—s) permutations, where $s$ is the number of all series.
