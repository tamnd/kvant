9. Waves behind a moving spar

When a submarine or aircraft moves at subsonic speeds, the disturbances caused by immi propagate in all directions. We can hear the approach of a subsonic aircraft, for example, as soon as the power of the sound waves ahead of it exceeds the sensitivity threshold of the ear. And a supersonic plane overtakes the sound waves propagating from it, so we don’t hear its approach in advance.

If we talk about distances that are large compared to the size of the aircraft, then the aircraft can be considered a point. The front of sound waves from an exact source, in accordance with Huyens' principle, is the surface of a sphere. The disturbances generated by the plane when it is at point M, after time t, fills a sphere with radius vt, where v is the speed of sound (Fig. 6). During the same time, an airplane moving at speed u>v will cover the distance OM=ut. The disturbances initiated by the aircraft at each point of the segment OM will fit into a cone, which is called the Maxa cone. The generatrix OA of the Maxa cone touches the front sphere, “expanding” from an arbitrary point of the segment OM from the moment of time when the plane is at this point. The half-volume of the Maxa cone solution is equal to

$$\beta = \arcsin \frac{AM}{OM} = \arcsin \frac{vt}{ut} = \arcsin \frac{1}{M},$$

where the ratio of the speed of movement to the speed of sound M=u/v>0 is called the Maxa number.

Being at point B, we have a sound signal from an aircraft moving at supersonic speed at an altitude h=BC, when it covers the distance CO=BC stg α=h√M²−1. Moreover, the signal will be double, like “bang-bang”: “bang” - from the incident sound wave 1, “bang” - from the reflected sound wave 2 (see Fig. 6).
