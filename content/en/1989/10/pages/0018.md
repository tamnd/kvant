Without being a skilled artist, it is difficult to convey a picture that would be revealed to a “traveler” walking around inside the crystal and admiring the perspective of the orderly atomic rows. Therefore, only small fragments of three different types of crystal structures are shown in Figures 5-7.

On figure 5 atoms are located at the vertices of an unremarkable oblique parallelepiped. This crystal (like its two-dimensional counterpart at figure 3) has no equivalent directions. But the following examples are more interesting. On figure, 6 atoms are located at the vertices of a rectangular parallelepiped, and lines, designated by the symbol $C_2$, are drawn through its center perpendicular to the faces. On figure 7, where a cube is depicted instead of a parallelepiped, similar lines are offset by the symbol $C_4$ and, in addition, the spatial diagonal of the cube is drawn - $C_3$. What's so special about all these lines?

Lines $C_2$, $C_3$, $C_4$ are the symmetry axes of the crystal structures shown in Figures 6 and 7. This is the name given to directions distinguished by the fact that, by turning around as a whole, one can obtain an arrangement of atoms that is completely indistinguishable from the original one. All you have to do is select a certain angle of rotation: $180^\circ$ for axis $C_2$, $120^\circ$ for axis $C_3$, $90^\circ$ for axis $C_4$ - i.e. $2\pi/n$ for axis $C_n$; multiples of these values ​​of rotation angles are also suitable, since the answers are repeatedly indicated on top. By the way, $C_n$ is the generally accepted designation of the axis of symmetry; the number $n$ is called its "order".

From the point of view of symmetry, we can clarify the concept of directional equivalence: any directions that “turn into each other” when turning around the symmetry are equivalent.

The more high-order axes a crystal has, the more equivalent directions it has, the more strongly symmetry competes with anisotropy. How this competition relates to conductivity, you will now see, but it is premature to find examples of equivalent and nonequivalent directions in the crystal structures shown in Figures 6 and 7. In particular, it will be important for us that in a cubic crystal the axes $C_4$ themselves are equivalent directions (transition into each other when rotated around $C_3$), and the analogous axes $C_2$ on figure 6 are not equivalent.
