Table. Five "platome bodies"; here $n$ is the number of sides of the face, $k$ is the number of edges adjacent to the vertex; $B, \Gamma, P$ is the total number of vertices, faces and edges, respectively.

| $n$ | $k$ | $B$ | $\Gamma$ | $P$ |
| :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| Tetrahedron | 3 | 3 | 4 | 4 | 6 |
| Cube | 4 | 3 | 8 | 6 | 12 |
| Octahedron | 3 | 4 | 6 | 8 | 12 |
| Dodecahedron | 5 | 3 | 20 | 12 | 30 |
| Icosahedron | 3 | 5 | 12 | 20 | 30 |

Since the times of Plato and Euclid, it has been well known that there are several types of regular polyhedra (see table). Let's prove this fact. Let all the faces of a certain polyhedron be regular $n$-gons and $k$ be the number of faces adjacent to a vertex (it is the same for all vertices). Let's look at the vertex $A$ of our polyhedron. Let $M_1, M_2, \dots, M_k$ be the ends $k$ of the edges coming out of it; since the two angles at these edges are equal, $AM_1M_2 \dots M_k$ is a regular paramid: when rotated by an angle $360^\circ/k$ around the height $AH$, the vertex $M_1$ goes into $M_2$, the vertex $M_2$ - into $M_3, \dots, M_k$ - into $M_1$ (Fig. 1). Let's compare equilateral triangles $AM_1M_2$ and $HM_1M_2$. They have a common base, and the side side $AM_1$ is larger than $HM_1$, therefore $\angle M_1AM_2 < \angle M_1HM_2 = 360^\circ/k$. But $\angle M_1AM_2$ is the angle of a regular $n$-gon on the plane, i.e. $180^\circ(n-2)/n$. So, $180^\circ(k(n-2)/n < 360^\circ, k(n-2) < 2n, k < \frac{2n}{n-2}$. From this inequality (and the fact that $k \ge 3$) it is easy to deduce that for the numbers $n$ and $k$ the only possible cases are those given in the table.

All corresponding polyhedra can be constructed using a cube as a basis.

To obtain a regular tetrahedron, it is enough to take four non-adjacent vertices of the cube and cut straight lines from it with four planes, each of which passes through three of the taken vertices (Fig. 2, a). Such a tetrahedron can be written into a cube in two ways. The intersection of two such regular tetrahedra is just a regular octahedron: a polyhedron of eight triangles with vertices located at the centers of the faces of the cube (Fig. 2, b). It remains to construct two of the most complex regular polyhedra: the $\delta$decahedron - a dodecahedron made from regular pentagons - and the $\iota$icocahedron - a dodecahedron made from regular triangles. But before we turn to the construction, we will make an absence.

This absence is preparation for the construction of the dodecahedron. We will fill in how to construct a regular pentagon on a plane. It is enough to construct a triangle with angles $36^\circ, 72^\circ$ and $72^\circ$ - having described a circle (Fig. 3) around such a triangle $ACD$, we can easily find two other vertices $B$ and $E$ of a regular pentagon. Let $AC=AD=1$. The side $CD$ is not difficult to find by noting that the bisector $CL$ divides the triangle into two equilateral ones, one of which - $CLD$ - is similar to the triangle $ACE$: $1-\tau=\tau^2$, whence $\tau=(\sqrt{5}-1)/2$ (this number is called the "golden ratio", and the Greek letter "tay" is often used to designate it). figure 4 shows a convenient construction method $\tau$ for filling in: if you sew a square from side 1 into a semicircle, then the diameter segments lying outside the square will be exactly equal to $\tau(PH \cdot HQ=RH^2)$, i.e. $\tau(1+\tau)=1$).
