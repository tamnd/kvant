Nya. The inner cylinder, coaxial with the first and also perforated, serves to supply oxygen, and the heat transfer elements are placed in the gap between the cylinders. The need to cover the ends of the cylinders with end plates creates a difficult, although solvable, mathematical problem.

Heat conducting elements. Their manufacture will apparently be cheaper than in the case of nuclear reactors, since there is no need to enclose the fuel in a shell, which in this case is even undesirable, since it impedes access to oxygen. Various types of lattices were calculated, and even the simplest of them - close-packed spheres - was quite satisfactory. Calculations of the optimal size of these spheres and the corresponding location tolerances are now being completed. Coal is easily formed, and the production of such spheres will not present any serious difficulties.

Oxidant. Pure oxygen is ideal for this purpose, but it is expensive and the cheapest substitute is air. However, 78% of air consists of nitrogen. If even part of the nitrogen reacts with carbon, toxic gas is formed, then it will also be a source of serious danger to the health of operating personnel (see below).

Management and control. The reaction begins to occur only at a fairly high temperature (988° Fahrenheit). This temperature can be easily achieved by passing an electric current of several thousand amperes of at least 30 volts between the outer and inner cylinders of the reactor. In this case, the end plates must be made of insulating ceramics, and this, together with a bulky battery, will significantly increase the durability of the installation. Some kind of spontaneous combustion reaction, such as between phosphorus and hydrogen peroxide, can also be used to trigger it, and this possibility should be overlooked. The course of the reaction after startup can be controlled by regularizing the supply of oxygen, which is almost as simple as controlling a conventional reactor using control rods.

Corrosion. The reactor walls must withstand temperatures above 1000 K in an atmosphere containing oxygen, nitrogen, carbon monoxide and dioxide, sulfur dioxide and various impurities, many of which are still unknown. Not many metals and specialty ceramics can withstand such conditions. Air quality is the bottom sniffer, but you may have to use pure nickel.

Safety precautions. The release of toxic gases from the reactor poses a serious threat to the nickel operator, but pure nickel may have to be used.
