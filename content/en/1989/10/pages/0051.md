The centers of the faces of the icosahedron, in turn, serve as the vertices of the dodecahedron*). If we surround each vertex of the icosahedron with a plane that cuts off a third of each edge emerging from it, we get a polyhedron with 32 faces, 20 hexagonal and 12 pentagonal - this is how modern soccer balls are made.

Relatively recently, it was discovered that a symmetrical body in the shape of an icosahedron was also invented by living nature: the protein shells of many viruses (in particular, the well-studied “bacteriophage λ” and the “tobacco mossica” virus) have this shape.

Let's see what kind of rotations there are in space with a fixed point A. Let us show that such a rotation is necessarily a rotation through a certain angle around a certain straight line passing through point A. It is enough for our motion F (with F(A) = A) to indicate a fixed straight line. You can find it this way: consider three points M₁, M₂=F(M₁) and M₃=F(M₂), different from the fixed point A, draw a plane through them and drop a perpendicular AH (Fig. 9) onto it - this will be the desired straight line. (If M₃=M₁, then our straight line passes through the middle of the segment M₁M₂, and F is axial symmetry: a rotation through an angle of 180°.)

So, self-alignment of a polyhedron is necessarily a rotation around point A, i.e., it has a common fixed point O into itself, i.e., it has a common fixed point O. This point is the center of gravity of a system of equal masses located at the vertices of a given polyhedron.
