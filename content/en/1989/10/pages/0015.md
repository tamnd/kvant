SYMMETRY, ANISOTROPY
AND OMA'S LAW

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
S. N. LYKOV,
Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
D. A. PARSHIN

Probably, every person has an intuitive idea of ​​symmetry - the petals in a flower, ornamental patterns, atoms in a crystal lattice, etc. are symmetrically arranged. There are beautiful connections with symmetry. And not only in art or architecture, but even in mathematics and physics. Moreover, in the latter case, it plays an extremely useful role: it turns out that the reported symmetry alone is sufficient to solve a number of difficult physical questions. We will try to convince the reader of this with the help of illustrative examples from solid state physics.

Imagine that we are given a task: to determine the electrical conductivity*) of some crystalline substance (with currently unknown properties). Let's cut a rectangular sample from it and connect it to the circuit, as shown in figure 1. By measuring the current through the sample and the voltage across it, using Ohm's law we will find the specific conductivity $\sigma$:

$$\sigma = \frac{IL}{US}.$$

It does not depend on the size of the sample and therefore, it would seem, unambiguously characterizes the ability of a given substance to conduct current. But why “kazaLOBY”? Can a crystalline have several conductivity values?
