Conclusion: the movement of a cube, tetrahedron or octahedron, combining it with itself, is a rotation around an axis of one of three types: the center of the polyhedron is the vertex, the center of the polyhedron is the middle of the edge, the center of the polyhedron is the center of the face.

Let's check this output for the cube. In a cube, the axis of the first type is the large diagonal. Let $AM_1$, $AM_2$, $AM_3$ be the edges emerging from the vertex $A$ of the cube, $AB$ its diagonal (Fig. 10, a). Distance rotation $R$, transforming points $A$, $M_1$, $M_2$, respectively, into $A$, $M_2$, $M_3$. During this rotation, the cube is aligned with itself, with the vertex $B$ remaining in place, so that the axis of rotation is the diagonal $AB$. Let's repeat this turn $R$ three times; in this case, $M_1$ will go to $M_2$, then to $M_3$ and again to $M_1$ - the cube will rotate to $360^\circ$ and align with its original position. Therefore, $F$ is a rotation around the $AB$ axis by an angle $120^\circ$.

In general, if a polyhedron is combined with itself when the cube of a straight line is rotated by an angle $360^\circ/m$, then this straight line is called an axis of symmetry of $m-2o$ order. For a cube, the axis of rotation of the type is the axis of symmetry of the 3rd order, the axis of the second type is the axis of symmetry of the 2nd order, and the axis of the third type is the axis of symmetry of the 4th order (Fig. 10, b). How many different self-alignments does a cube have? Around each 3rd order axis there are two different non-directional rotations: by the angle $120^\circ$ and by the angle $240^\circ$; and there are 4 such axes - only 8 movements. There are 6 axes of the 2nd order, around each there is one rotation - another 6 movements. There are 3 axes of the 4th order, around each there are three rotations (at angles $90^\circ$, $180^\circ$, $270^\circ$) - another 9 movements. Total $8+6+9=23$ illegal movements. The cube has 24 pairs “face - its vertex” (6 faces × 4 vertices) and any pair can be transferred by movement to any of the 23 others - this requires 23 different movements.

In the case of a tetrahedron, the axes of the first and third types coincide: the axis drawn through the center of the tetrahedron and its vertex also passes through the center of the opposite face. These axes are of 3rd order; There are 4 such axes in total. Axes of the second type are straight lines connecting the midpoints of opposite edges of the tetrahedron; There are three such axes, and they are of 2nd order. Thus, the tetrahedron is combined with itself $4\cdot 2+3\cdot 1=11$ by various rotations, and this is consistent with the fact that the tetrahedron $4\cdot 3=12$ has “face-vertex” pairs.

Movement and symmetry. When considering self-alignment of polyhedra, we can include not only rotations, but also any movements that transform the polyhedron into itself. Here, movements are any transformation of space that preserves pairwise distances between points.

In addition to rotations, the number of movements must also include mirror movements. Their averages are symmetry relative to a plane (reflection), as well as the composition of reflection relative to a plane and rotation around a straight line peridicular to it (this is a general form of mirror motion that has a fixed point). Of course, such movements cannot be realized by continuous movement of the polyhedron in space (just as it is impossible to combine the left shoe with its mirror image - the right shoe).

All regular polyhedra have planes of symmetry (the tetrahedron has 6 of them, the cube and octahedron have 9 each, and the dodecadron has 15 each). And the total number of mirror self-combinations turns out to be the same point as the number of real (own) self-combinations—turns (including also actions).
