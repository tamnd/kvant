Let us assume that the current $\vec{j}$ flows at an angle to symmetry axis 1. Let us rotate the crystal together with the field $\vec{E}$ applied to it by $180^\circ$ around this axis. Obviously, then everything that is inside the crystal - the “stand” of atoms and its arbitrary flow of electrons - will also turn. And, since the vector $\vec{j}$ is not supposed to be parallel to the rotation axis, its new orientation, shown as a dotted line in figure 8, will be different from the original one. But the microfurnace picture that determines the direction of the current $\vec{j}$, after such a rotation, looks the same as before: the new position of the atoms is indistinguishable from the first initial one, and the field $\vec{E}$ was and remains parallel to direction 1. This means that the orientation of the current $\vec{j}$ should be the same.

Thus, we have arrived at a contradiction, and the only way to avoid it is to admit that in this case the vector $\vec{j}$ is oriented like the vector $\vec{E}$ - along axis 1 (Fig. 9).

Moreover, this conclusion is not sensitive to the details of the microstructure of the crystal - it was not necessary to find out exactly how the atoms are located and what are the directions that are easiest for electrons to move. The origins of other axes of symmetry were also not used. Even the order of the symmetry axis along which the field $\vec{E}$ is directed is not important - the reasoning will remain valid for any value of the rotation angle (not a multiple of $360^\circ$), we assume by symmetry.

Thus, if the crystal has a symmetry axis and the field $\vec{E}$ is directed along it, the current $\vec{j}$ flows along the field. Under these conditions, the connection between $\vec{j}$ and $\vec{E}$ is given by equality (3), where the proportionality coefficient $\sigma$ in this case is the conductivity along this symmetry axis. We carry out the measurement (Fig. 1) on a sample cut along the symmetry axis of the crystal, we can find $\sigma$ using formula (1). Indeed, unlike the situation depicted in figure 4, the field will certainly not have components transverse to the current; therefore $E = U/L$, and (1) immediately follows from (3).

Correspondence, three measurements on samples oriented along symmetry axes 1, 2, 3, gives us three conductivity values: $\sigma_1$, $\sigma_2$, $\sigma_3$. It is clear that, generally speaking, they turn out to be different, since directions 1, 2, 3 are not equivalent:

$$\sigma_1 \neq \sigma_2 \neq \sigma_3.$$

What will the picture be like if the field $\vec{E}$ in our crystal is not directed along the symmetry axis? Where does the current $\vec{j}$ go? To understand this, let’s find the projections of the vector $\vec{E}$ on axes 1, 2, 3, i.e., imagine the field as a sum $\vec{E} = \vec{E}_1 + \vec{E}_2 + \vec{E}_3$ (Fig. 10). Now let's see what contribution each of the terms makes to the current.

The field $\vec{E}_1$ is directed along the symmetry axis 1 and, therefore, generates a current in the same direction:

$$\vec{j}_1 = \sigma_1 \vec{E}_1.$$

Similarly, the other two field components cause currents along symmetry axes 2 and 3:

$$\vec{j}_2 = \sigma_2 \vec{E}_2, \quad \vec{j}_3 = \sigma_3 \vec{E}_3.$$
