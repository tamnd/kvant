This means that the vectors $\vec{j}$ and $\vec{E}$ are not parallel (Fig. 11). This fact, which is by no means new to us, is, as we see, directly related to the difference in conductivity in different directions.

And here's what's interesting. Formula (5) tells us: for any orientation of the field $\vec{E}$, the magnitude and direction of the current $\vec{j}$ are completely determined only by the conductivity values ​​along the symmetry axes of our crystal. There is no question of an infinite number of dimensions; it is enough to find $\sigma_1$, $\sigma_2$, $\sigma_3$. True, first we need to find the directions of the symmetry axes itself, but that’s a different story.

The next example is a cubic crystal (Fig. 7). But this is a special case of the previous one? Yes. And at the same time the most interesting! Firstly, cubic symmetry is the most possible for crystals. Secondly, it is possessed by crystalline semiconductors that are important for practice - Ge, Si, etc. Thirdly, this will be the most striking example of the influence of symmetry on the conductivity of crystals.

So, there are three mutually perpendicular axes of symmetry $C_4$. The conductivity values ​​along them will still be denoted by $\sigma_1$, $\sigma_2$, $\sigma_3$. Then the reasoning of the word at, already known to us, leads to formula (5), which is valid for any orientation of the vector $\vec{E}$. Now remember that all three directions $C_4$ in a cubic crystal are equivalent to each other. And from this, as you agree, it follows that

$$\sigma_1 = \sigma_2 = \sigma_3 = \sigma.$$ (6)

Yes, symmetry ensures that the conductivity along these three directions is the same. But only along them? Indeed, taking into account (6), formula (5) turns... into formula (3), known to us as Ohm’s law for a manufactured medium -

$$\vec{j} = \sigma \vec{E}_1 + \sigma \vec{E}_2 + \sigma \vec{E}_3 = \sigma (\vec{E}_1 + \vec{E}_2 + \vec{E}_3) = \sigma \vec{E},$$

i.e., firstly, regardless of the direction of the applied field $\vec{E}$, the current $\vec{j}$ is always parallel to the field $\vec{E}$ and, secondly, its magnitude is determined by only one value of conductivity $\sigma$. The conductivity of a cubic crystal is the same in all directions!

Isn't it true that the result is unexpected and certainly beautiful? After all, not all directions in a cubic crystal are equivalent - compare, for example, the lines $C_4$ and $C_3$ on figure 7. Both of them are axes of symmetry, and the current, of course, will be parallel to the field $\vec{E}$ if it is applied along any of them. But why should the current be the same in both orientations $\vec{E}$? After all, electrops “stun” the equivalence of direction, and this, it would seem, should manifest itself in different values ​​of the corresponding conductivity. But no! It does not matter in which orientation the sample is cut, the measurements should be a single value $\sigma$. And we just proved it based on just the symmetry messages.

So, in this case, symmetry has completely defeated anisotropy: with respect to electrical conductivity, a cubic crystal is similar to a prepared medium in which, as already mentioned, all directions are equivalent.
