Chitt, in a complete region the cosine may be greater than 1.

But we digress from the story of the zeta function and its significance. We nominate that the problem is to find the values ​​of ζ(2), ζ(4), ..., ζ(2k), ..., i.e., the sums of the series.

$$1 + \frac{1}{2^2} + \frac{1}{3^2} + \dots + \frac{1}{n^2} + \dots,$$ (13)

$$1 + \frac{1}{2^4} + \frac{1}{3^4} + \dots + \frac{1}{n^4} + \dots$$ (14)

Euler also solved this problem. To do this, he took yes and factored the infinite series. He knew that if the polynomial $a_0x^n + a_1x^{n-1} + \dots + a_n$ has roots $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n$, then the equality

$$a_0x^n + a_1x^{n-1} + \dots + a_n = a_n\left(1 - \frac{x}{x_1}\right) \times \left(1 - \frac{x}{x_2}\right) \dots \left(1 - \frac{x}{x_n}\right)$$ (15)

(after all, both sides of this equality vanish at $x = x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n$, and at $x = 0$ both sides are equal to $a_n$). Euler boldly applied this formula to infinite series. Since the function $\frac{\sin x}{x}$ vanishes at points of the form $\pm n\pi$, where $n$ is a natural number, he, by analogy with formula (15), wrote

$$\frac{\sin x}{x} = \left(1 - \frac{x}{\pi}\right)\left(1 + \frac{x}{\pi}\right) \times \left(1 - \frac{x}{2\pi}\right)\left(1 + \frac{x}{2\pi}\right) \dots$$

(Euler also used here the equality $\frac{\sin x}{x} = 1$ for $x = 0$, which the reader might prefer to replace with the formula for the limit $\frac{\sin x}{x}$ for $x \to 0$. But Euler was not afraid to grab the triple in “algebra and analysis”!) After this, grouping the connecting terms, Euler received:

$$\frac{\sin x}{x} = \left(1 - \frac{x^2}{\pi^2}\right)\left(1 - \frac{x^2}{2^2\pi^2}\right) \times \dots \times \left(1 - \frac{x^2}{n^2\pi^2}\right).$$ (16)

And here the ignorance of the dangers for the emergence of Euler quickly marvels ahead. Less than a year later, the German mathematician K. Weierstrass (1815-1897) strictly substantiated Euler's disentanglement. Moreover, he proved that if the function $f(z)$ for any (complex) value $z$ is the sum of the series $a_0 + a_1z + \dots + a_nz^n + \dots$, then it can be factorized in a manner similar to that used by Euler.

And from formula (16) we easily obtain the sum of series (13). To do this, you just need to open the brackets on the right side of equality (16), and then replace sin $x$ according to formula (11), and then compare the coefficients for $x^2$ on the next and on the right. Let's do this. If you open the brackets in the final product

$$\left(1 - \frac{x^2}{\pi^2}\right)\left(1 - \frac{x^2}{4\pi^2}\right) \dots \left(1 - \frac{x^2}{n^2\pi^2}\right),$$

then the resulting polynomial has a coefficient of $x^2$ equal to

$$-\frac{1}{\pi^2} - \frac{1}{4\pi^2} - \dots - \frac{1}{n^2\pi^2};$$

if we direct $n$ to infinity, this coefficient will tend to the sum of the infinite series

$$-\frac{1}{\pi^2} - \frac{1}{4\pi^2} - \dots - \frac{1}{n^2\pi^2} - \dots,$$

i.e. $k - \frac{1}{\pi^2} \zeta(2)$. The series for $\frac{\sin x}{x}$ has a coefficient for $x^2$ equal to $-\frac{1}{3!} = -\frac{1}{6}$. Comparing the coefficients for $x^2$, we get a completely unexpected equality

$$\zeta(2) = 1 + \frac{1}{2^2} + \frac{1}{3^2} + \dots + \frac{1}{n^2} + \dots = \frac{\pi^2}{6}.$$ (17)

Similarly, comparing the coefficients of $x^4$ on the left and right and taking into account equalities (14) and (17), we obtain

$$\zeta(4) = 1 + \frac{1}{2^4} + \frac{1}{3^4} + \dots + \frac{1}{n^4} + \dots = \frac{\pi^4}{90}.$$ (18)
