Now it makes sense to look again at how the image of an object moves along the main optical axis from infinity to the focus $F$ and the far one (Fig. 7). When approaching the focus, the image goes to infinity and immediately after the focus returns, but on the other side (no surprise - the straight line is closed). At the same time, from the direct one it becomes inverse, appearing on the other side of the axis (and this, so to speak, is effectively a Möbius strip).

Our cover

Figures 1 through 4 on the back cover show how to construct a surface in 3D space that is topologically equivalent to the design plane. This surface has a line of self-intersection, at the ends of which the smoothness of the surface is disrupted - at these points it is impossible to draw a tangent plane to it. The self-intersection of the image is possible only by going out into four-dimensional space, but here is an example of a smooth surface, topologically equivalent to the existing design plane, and cut the hemisphere into 3 slices, as shown in figure 5 on the 4th page of the cover. Let's take one slice, turn the two sides of the hexagon that fall on it into loops (Fig. 6) and bend the surface so that one of the loops (loop $b$) lies on the surface (Fig. 7), loop $a$ would turn into loop $b$ when turning $R$ to 120° around the NS axis, and at point $S$ formed an angle of 120°. The same is done with two other slices, which are attached to the first one so that the resulting figure turns into itself when turning $R$. The Boy surface is formed by gluing the segments along the cut lines and loops (the loops form a three-lobed closed line of self-intersection). The 1st page of the cover shows the top view of this surface, from the side of point $N$.
