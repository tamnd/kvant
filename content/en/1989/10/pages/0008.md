A quarter of a century ago, in 1964, the American W. Little and the Soviet academician V.L. Ginzburg considered a special mechanism for combining superconductor electrons into pairs, which ensured a significant increase in the critical temperature.

The new mechanism of superconductivity initially aroused considerable interest in the BC problem among many scientists. Many theoretical works appeared in which this idea was deepened and developed. Experimental research also began (however, its main result was the emergence of new directions at the intersection of physics and chemistry, which developed without any connection with BC). However, the main thing did not happen: the idea of ​​BC did not take hold of the masses. Repeated requests from enthusiasts (primarily Ginzburg himself) to expand research on BC on a broad front, concentrating the services of physicists, chemists and materials scientists on the search for high-temperature superconductors, remained unanswered.

Moreover, over time, experts have developed a stable stereotype of a cool and often openly negative attitude towards BC. The majority did not believe in the feasibility of the corresponding program, and there were those who for some reason really did not want it to be implemented. Two large and authoritative specialists even proved a special theorem about the fundamental necessity of BC (and the author of these lines had to work hard to refute it). In general, by the mid-70s, a kind of more condensation of almost universal skepticism and disbelief in the BC program had formed.*)

Should we then be surprised that the discovery of BC happened unexpectedly and turned out to be accidental in many respects? The materials discovered by BC were not on the radar of most specialists (although ten years before the discovery of BC there was a signal that complex structures such as oxocuprates could have a relatively high critical temperature). In a certain sense, the authors of the discovery were random figures who came to the physics of superconductivity from another field (although, perhaps, this is just natural: Müller and Bednorz did not experience the influence of the common present of specialists). The data of the discovery turned out to be accidental: back in the late 70s, chemists from different countries were interested in oxocuprates of the desired state and even changed the dependence of their resistance on temperature (but, without thinking about superconductivity, they worked at too high a temperature).** Such research
