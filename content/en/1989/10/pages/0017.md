In an anisotropic medium, they may turn out to be incorrect, and then the vectors $\vec{j}$ and $\vec{E}$ will not be parallel! You can visually imagine this situation like this.

The external force $\vec{F} = e\vec{E}$ drives electrons “through the system” of atoms, and in it there are “easy” and “difficult” directions for movement. When colliding with atoms, an electron often changes its flight direction, and only on average can we talk about the electron moving in a straight line. Having covered many mechanical distances, he manages to shift more in the “easy” direction than in the “hard” direction, and, moreover, not necessarily along $\vec{F}$. It all depends on the details of the interaction of electrons with atoms and the orientation of the $\vec{E}$ field in relation to the most visible crystalline directions.

The thoughtful reader is probably ready to ask a tricky question: in our experiments (Fig. 1) the field is always applied along the sample and the current, without a doubt, can also only be applied along the sample. Where is the parallelism of the vectors $\vec{j}$ and $\vec{E}$ here?

We admit that this is an important fact that removes this misunderstanding from you. We did not say that (all because of the same anisotropy) the field $\vec{E}$, which causes a current $\vec{j}$ in the thickness of the crystal, is not necessarily with an electric field of directionality $U/L$ applied along the sample. Neither in size nor in direction. A possible picture in the case of an anisotropic medium is shown in figure 4, where you see the result of a chain of events,

occurring from the moment of application to the sample. At this moment, the electrons begin to be affected by the field applied along the sample—vector $\vec{E}_{\parallel}$ on figure 4. The resulting current, as already mentioned, due to the anisotropy of the medium, may be directed differently, for example, to the right and slightly upward (the electrons move slightly downward and slightly downward). A transverse change of electrons will lead to the fact that the faces of the sample begin to charge (upper positive, lower negative), and a transverse field $\vec{E}_{\perp}$, additional to the application, will appear in the sample. But it itself prevents the downward transverse movement of electrons, so it increases only until this movement stops. All this happens in the blink of an eye (in some $10^{-13}$ s) and subsequently the picture does not change: the current $\vec{j}$ continues to flow along the sample, but the resulting field $\vec{E} = \vec{E}_{\parallel} + \vec{E}_{\perp}$ is directed at an angle to it. By the way, this is easy to check - between the upper and lower boundaries there should be a potential difference due to the additional component $\vec{E}_{\perp}$ of the field vector $\vec{E}$. This reality is revealed through experience.

So, already the first acquaintance with Ohm’s law for anisotropic conductors shows that the conductivity of a crystal, generally speaking, is different in different directions, and the current $\vec{j}$ can be exactly at an angle to the electric field $\vec{E}$.
