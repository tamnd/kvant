If the role of scientific public opinion in the history of BC is truly as described above, then the reasons for the emergence of this phenomenon undoubtedly deserve study. Since both objective and purely psychological factors played a role here, the corresponding analysis should be carried out, in addition to physicists and chemists, also by historians of science, psychologists, and perhaps also “engineers with souls” who write about science.

In any case, one legal lesson can already be drawn from the history of BC. There is no doubt that major and authoritative scientists play a special role in shaping the scientific atmosphere. No one, of course, is trying to take away their right to have their own opinion about the reality of a scientific program, to believe or not to believe in its feasibility. However, the higher the potential importance of the program and the larger the name of the scientist, the greater the moral responsibility he should feel (and the more purposeful he will be) in expressing his opinion. Thank God, our teachers left us enough examples of a truly intelligent attitude towards new ideas (the author is referring primarily to the Mandelstam-Tamm school of theoretical physics, to which he himself belongs).

We must not forget that a situation similar to the history of BC may repeat itself - new scientific programs may arise, the feasibility of which (given their potential importance) is not very obvious in advance, although it cannot be refuted. What should the authors of such a program do if they do not have the resources to implement it and were unable to overcome the prejudice of their colleagues? Wait, as before, for a similar program to be implemented abroad, and then negotiate with all your might, as happened with BC? Try, as before, to prove that the program is of particularly important strategic importance, in the hope of winning over the corresponding rich departments?

I would like to hope that our reconstructed society will develop a mechanism for moral, organizational and/material support for such programs, the action (or inaction) of which will be determined not by the emotions and sentiments of a certain circle of people, but by an objective assessment of the seriousness of the relevant proposals.
