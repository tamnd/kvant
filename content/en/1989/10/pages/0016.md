But even in the absence of conductivity layers, the medium may depend on the direction of the current in it. Many crystals have this property. And here's why.

figure 3 shows the arrangement of atoms in a crystal (real crystals, of course, are three-dimensional, but the essence of the matter becomes clear in flat pictures). Compare the two directions indicated by the red and blue lines: it is clear that they are not equivalent. Why? Well, at least because along the “blue” one. Try to choose any other direction, and you will see that it is not equivalent to the previous ones. It is natural to expect that electrons also somehow “shock” this nonequivalence - in some directions it is easier for them to pass between atoms, in others it is more difficult. This means that it should not be surprising that the conductivity of a crystal can depend on the direction of the current.

Media whose properties are not the same in different directions are called anisotropic. The reason for the anisotropy of crystals, as you now understand, is the non-equivalence of direction, resulting from the regular arrangement of atoms.

Another important manifestation of anisotropy is that the current in the crystal can flow in a direction other than that in which the electron field is directed. Let us explain what we are talking about. From formula (1) it is clear that the current density $j=I/S$ is proportional to the direction of the electric field $E=U/L$ applied to the sample:

$$j=\sigma E.$$

However, the current and field in the medium are characterized not only by their magnitude, but also by their direction. The field vector $\vec{E}$ specifies the magnitude and direction of the force $\vec{F}=e\vec{E}$ acting on each charge $e$. Under the influence of this force, the electrons move on average with a certain speed $\vec{v}$. The current density vector $\vec{j}$, which for brevity we will simply call the current vector, is parallel to the average velocity of the electrons $\vec{v}^*$). Ohm's law, written in the form (2), relates only the magnitudes of the current $j$ and the externally applied field, but says nothing about the relative orientation of the vectors $\vec{j}$ and $\vec{E}$.

“She’s very rude,” you say. — Field $\vec{E}$ and force $\vec{F}$ are parallel. The average speed of the electrons $\vec{v}$ is parallel to the acting force $\vec{F}$. And the current $\vec{j}$ is parallel to $\vec{v}$. This means that the vectors $\vec{j}$ and $\vec{E}$ are also parallel, and together (2) we can write:

$$\vec{j}=\sigma \vec{E}.$$

It's very simple!>

Is this reasoning correct? Yes... if we are talking about a manufactured environment - an environment in which all directions are equivalent. When all directions are equivalent, the electrons don't care which way they go, and once we apply an external field, they don't have to choose—the vector $\vec{E}$ gives a single vision of direction. The current $\vec{j}$ will flow along it.
