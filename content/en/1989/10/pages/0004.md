In the first few years, when the era of scientific and technological revolution began with the emergence of a new powerful force - atomic energy - the specialty of physics was considered a prestigious feature. Later, society not only cooled towards representatives of this profession, but also began to see in them the source of global problems facing humanity. This evolution was sensitively captured by the poses: Slutsky’s poems “Something physicists are held in high esteem...” were replaced by Galich’s song “It was the bastard physicists who made a bet spin the ball on the contrary...”.

It looks like physics stocks have been on the rise again lately. This was accompanied by a major scientific sensation - the discovery of high-temperature superconductivity (abbreviated as BC).

The reader will find a detailed story about the amazing world of superconductivity and the discovery of BC in the article by Academician A. A. Abrikosov “Superconductivity: history, modern ideas, recent successes”, published in No. 6 of Quaenta for 1988. And in this article, the author tried, without sacrificing everything the reader needed to get a general idea of ​​the problem, to focus on its universal aspects.

The frame is formed by a regular crystal lattice, composed of metal atoms that vibrate around the lattice nodes. In the space between the atoms there are easily mobile electrons, as well as atoms of foreign impurities.

When a power source (for example, a battery) is connected, an electric current will arise in the conductor, i.e., the electrons of the metal begin to move. They inevitably become involved with the collapsing atoms of the lattice and with the atoms of impurities. This leads to the deceleration of electrons with the transformation of their initial ordered movement into a chaotic one (in other words, with the release of heat). Therefore, when the battery is opened, the current quickly fades, and its energy turns into heat.

A measure of such braking is the resistivity of the conductor (hereinafter, for brevity, simply called resistance). For a given material, the lower the concentration of impurities and the temperature of the conductor, the lower it is. Therefore, it has long been believed that the resistance of a very pure conductor, when cooled to absolute zero (-273 °C), should gradually tend to zero.

To test this belief, the Dutch physicist H. Kamerlingh Onnes checked in 1911 measurements of the resistance of others (liquid and therefore easily cleaned metal) when it was deeply cooled. At first, everything went according to expectations: as the temperature decreased, the resistance also dropped. However, at a temperature of about 4 K, the resistance number suddenly dropped to zero and continued to be absent upon further cooling.
