Prelude

There is a city in the Ryazan region that bears the name of the great Russian scientist. Chaplygin is the name of this city.

Once upon a time, Tsar Peter I gave the village of Slobodskoye to his favorite Prince Menshikov. Menshikov built a small fortress here and renamed the village Oranenburg. The road from Moscow to Voronezh, which was under construction at that time, passed through this small town with a toy fortress. Subsequently, the road to Orenburg was built through the fortress and the Menshikov town began to write “Ranenburg” in papers.

Sergei Chaplygin was born in this city on April 5, 1869 (March 24, old style).

Father Sergei - Alexey Timofeevich Chaplygin, who came from a merchant family, worked as a clerk in a store. He had only an elementary education, but he read a lot and had an excellent memory. Mother, Anna Petrovna Chaplygina, also belonged to the merchant class. Anna Petrovna's marriage with Alexei Timofeevich was happy, but this happiness was short-lived. When Sergei was only two years old, the Chaplygin family suffered greater grief: at the age of 24, father Sergei died of cholera. Later, Anna Petrovna married again, and the family moved from Ranenburg to Voronezh.

Sergeraros was taken by a boy. Anna Petrovna dreamed of giving her son an education and, when Sergei was already old enough, she hired him a seminarian tutor. In the fall, Sergei successfully passed the exam for the preparatory class and became a student at the gymnasium.

The gymnasium teachers very quickly noticed the unusual abilities of Sergei Chaplygin. The pedagogical council exempts him from tuition fees. When the parents of Chaplygin’s gymnasium learned about the mathematical abilities

After identifying his fellow students, they began to invite him to be a tutor for their sons. Sergei graduated from high school with flying colors - with a gold medal. A famous mathematician who lived in Voronezh and author of numerous school textbooks, A.P. Kiselev, admired Chaplygin’s abilities and predicted a great future for him. At the age of 17, taking with him 200 rubles, accumulated by tutoring, and a small boat with belongings, Sergei settled in Moscow.

coming of age

Chaplygin was enrolled in the Faculty of Physics and Mathematics of Moscow University.

His student life was spent in a harsh environment; there was no need for help from his family. On the contrary, as in his high school years, Sergei continued to help his family financially. To do this, he had to take a large number of lessons. And yet he never missed a single lecture at the university.

In Moscow, Sergei lived with his Voronezh friends. Seven of us lived. He was the only mathematician in the room. All two competitions have always been inherent in this company. Once, one of his friends complained about the complexity of the inorganic chemistry course, and Sergei offered to bet that within three days he would read the entire textbook on this subject. He won the bet. His other bet ended in the same victory: he completed his English course in two weeks.

In general, foreign languages ​​were very easy for Sergei. However, during the holidays, Chaplygin worked as a tutor in a Polish family. In the shortest possible time, he studied the Polish language and, to the surprise of all members of this family, began to read Polish writers in the original. That same summer, Chaplygin learned another language - Italian. Chaplygin's ability to learn foreign languages ​​was amazing. There was a moment in his life when he chose international and eastern languages.

Having become a student at Moscow University, Chaplygin proposed to specialize in pure mathematics. But starting from the third year, his interests began to lean towards mechanics. The lectures of Professor N. E. Zhukovsky, in which a wide range of scientific problems were revealed, truly captivated Chaplygin, and he became one of Zhukovsky’s first students. In his last, fourth year at the university, Chaplygin, under his leadership, carried out his first scientific research on hydromechanics. Chaplygin’s diploma work “On the motion of heavy bodies in an incompressible fluid” was awarded a gold medal by the University Council. On January 1, 1891, at the suggestion of Zhukovsky, graduate S. Chaplygin was left at the university as a candidate to prepare for the professorship.

At the end of the 19th century, he began to take his first steps in practical aviation. In Russia in 1881, naval officer A.F. Mozhaisky received a patent for an aircraft with a steam engine, which he called an aerial projectile. Mozhaisky's plane was built in 1885, but crashed during takeoff. In England and France during the same years, attempts were also made to create an aircraft, but they were not crowned with success. In the period from 1891 to 1896, the German scientist O. Lilienthal managed to construct several successful glider models. These gliders were built and successfully tested.

World aviation took its first steps at random, against the universe. Airplanes were built only on the basis of intuitive assumptions. I never made any preliminary calculations or experiments. The lack of a scientific basis for aircraft construction and, as a consequence, the imperfection of designs, was the cause of a large number of aviation accidents. During this period, scientists in many countries began to conduct the first theoretical studies in the field of aerodynamics. A major contribution to them is the works of N. E. Zhukovsky and his student, the future academician S. A. Chaplygin.

In August 1893, S. A. Chaplygina began teaching as a physics teacher at the Moscow Catherine Institute *). From September 1895, he taught mathematics at Moscow University, and then at the Moscow Technical University.

At the same time, he begins to study the movement of gas flows. As you know, liquid is practically incompressible. Gases, such as, for example, saturated air, on the contrary, are easily subject to rolling. In aerodynamics, it is nice to call any incompressible liquid medium simply a liquid, and the compressed one - a gas. It turns out that at low speeds the air flow behaves like an incompressible medium and therefore can, to a certain approximation, be called a liquid. As the flow velocity increases, this approximation becomes too rough. In addition, we come to consider a more complex mathematical model of the theory. Chaplygina began researching such a model during these years.

In 1903, at thirty-four, Sergei Alekseevich became a professor at Moscow University. During these years he was already considered a brilliant young scientist. He was invited to lecture at the Moscow Higher Women's Courses. A few years later, the council of professors of higher women's courses elects S. A. Chaplygin as director. While occupying this position, Sergei Alekseevich organized the construction of educational buildings that were magnificent for those times.
