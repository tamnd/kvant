In the meantime, let’s take a look at things that are far from forensics and let’s talk about how you can determine the chemical composition of a substance, that is, find out which elements and in what proportion are present in a sample.

From your chemistry course, you know that this can be done using chemical reactions characteristic of substances and elements, using the methods of so-called “wet chemistry.” However, in some cases the application of these methods encounters serious difficulties. For example, when it is necessary to perform a chemical analysis of a very small sample; when extremely high accuracy of analysis of many elements at the same time is required. For example, it may require identifying one “foreign” atom among a million or 100 million others and determining what type it is.

What do you do in such cases?

It has been known for approximately 100 years that the most effective methods of chemical analysis are often physical methods. And among them, the real champion of sensitivity is neutron activation analysis (NAA). To explain its principles, we first recall how the atomic nucleus is structured.

The atomic nucleus consists of positively charged protons and electric neutrons. The number of protons determines the charge of the nucleus and serves as its main characteristic. Some elements differ from others primarily in the number of protons in the nucleus. The first element of the periodic table - hydrogen (H) - has one proton in the nucleus, the second - helium (He) - two, etc. Well, how many neutrons are there in the nucleus? It turns out that the nuclei of the same element can contain different numbers of neutrons. Nuclei with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons are called isotopes.

Let us explain this with an example. There are always two protons in the helium nucleus. And the neutron can be 1, 2, 4 or 6, i.e. there are four isotopes of He - $^3$He, $^4$He, $^6$He, $^8$He (the index indicates the total number of protons and neutrons). Tin has a record number of isotopes - 10, xenon has 9 isotopes, cadmium and tellurium have 8, etc. Natural isotopes are unevenly distributed. As a rule, the lion's share (90% or more) of all natural reserves of an element is the same isotope.

The chemical properties of isotopes are the same. But the physical ones may differ and very noticeably. For example, due to “extra” neutrons, the atomic weights of different isotopes of an element are different. But this is the main thing for us. A large group of isotopes has an amazing property - radioactivity. They are sources of radiation that cannot be seen or felt, but which can be registered using special devices - radiation detectors. The simplest of them are photoplastic and Geiger counter. We will not delve into the question of the nature of radiation. It is complex and beyond the scope of this article. But for clarity, you can imagine radiation as a stream of invisible particles.

The emission of each radioactive isotope is highly specific. This is his individual portrait, unique, like human fingerprints. The most characteristic features in this portrait are the radiation energy (for us - the energy of particles) and the half-life.

What a half-life is, there are relatively few explanations. Using a detector, you can not only register radiation, but also measure its intensity, i.e., determine how many particles fall per unit area per unit time. The greater the intensity, the higher the degree of physical energy gain. The more often the Geiger counter will click, etc. Period

The half-life of an isotope (denoted $T_{1/2}$) is the time during which the intensity of its radiation decreases by half. During this time, half of the original radioactive nuclei turns into non-radioactive (as they say, stable). The half-lives of different isotopes are different - from fractions of a second to millions of years. Special detectors not only measure the intensity of radiation, but also its spectrum - a graph of the dependence of radiation intensity on its energy. An example of such a spectrum is shown in figure 1. Sharp “spikes” of intensity are called spectrum lines - they correspond to radiation from different isotopes. Using pre-compiled tables, each line can easily be associated with its “own” isotope (this is how the isotopes indicated in figure were “recognized”).

Constructing the emission spectrum thus solves the problem of qualitative chemical analysis of the sample, i.e., it makes it possible to determine what radioactive elements it consists of.

What we have told so far should have convinced you, dear readers, of one thing: if the state samples were from radioactive isotopes, then it would be relatively easy to determine their qualitative composition from the spectra. But here’s the problem: “normal” samples are made from natural non-radioactive isotopes. Therefore, to carry out analysis they must be activated, that is, made radioactive. There are different ways to activate. The easiest way is to “bombard” the sample in the reactor with neutrons. Using emission spectra, you can carry out not only a qualitative analysis, but also a quantitative one—to find out how many atoms of a particular isotope are present in a sample. The fact is that the intensity of an isotope’s radiation is directly proportional to the number of its nuclei. Having standards (i.e. samples with a previously known content of the analyzed element), it is possible, by activating them, to construct a graph of this dependence. And by measuring the emission intensity of a given isotope in the test sample, which was activated together with the standard, it is easy to determine the content of this isotope from a graph. (After what we said about the half-life, you should understand why the activation of the sample and the standard and the change in their spectra are carried out simultaneously. If this condition is not met, then to determine the amount of the isotope from the standard graph, it is necessary to make corrections for the half-life.)

The method of qualitative and quantitative analysis we described is called neutron activation analysis - HAA.
