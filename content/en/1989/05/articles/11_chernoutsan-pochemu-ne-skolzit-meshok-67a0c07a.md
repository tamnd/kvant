This example shows that when analyzing forces acting on a body or on a system of bodies, it is also necessary to take into account the time of their action. For example, when there is a rupture from the outside, an external force acts on it - the force of gravity. Despite this, we can assume that the momentum of the system is conserved (the momentum outside is equal to the total momentum of the fragments), since the change in the momentum of the system over a very short explosion time is nothing small.

“No,” an observant student may object, “there’s clearly something wrong here!” Look at the hard ball that bounces off the floor (Fig. 2). The impact time is short, and the change in momentum is quite noticeable:

$$\Delta P_y = mv - (-mv) = 2mv.$$

What's the matter?

The student is certainly right. It is not always the case that the effect of a force becomes negligible for a short time of its action.*) This is true only in the case when the magnitude of the force is fixed, as, for example, in a gravity force. The situation is completely different with the ground reaction force,

*) The action of force can be changed by the impulse that it would impart to the body, acting simultaneously. In the case of a constant force, this quantity (it is called the force impulse) is equal to $\vec{F} \Delta t$, in the case of a variable force - $\sum \vec{F}_i \Delta t_i$. The average force is seen differently, defining it by the equality $\vec{F}_{\text{cp}} \Delta t = \sum \vec{F}_i \Delta t_i$.

Which acts on the ball when it hits the plane. If we add a decrease in the impact time, for example, by a factor of 10 (due to the stiffness of the materials of the ball and the plane), then the average value of the reaction force will increase ten times, and the change in the momentum of the ball will still be equal to $2mv$. We will call such forces shock conditions. Their actions can be significant in any interaction, no matter how short.
