Where are auroras visible? Polar lights, in particular northern lights, can be observed not only near the pole. They can be seen on the Black Sea coast, and even in Rome. But as we approach the poles, the frequency of auroras increases sharply.

Long-term observations have made it possible to map lines connecting places with the same frequency of auroras (such lines are called isochases). They show, for example, that on the Black Sea coast aurora can be observed once (one night) in 10 years, in the north of England or on the Kola Peninsula - 100 nights a year, and on most of the coast of the Arctic Ocean - almost every night. Here we express the frequency of auroras as a number of nights per year. This does not mean that auroras are observed only at night. They also appear in the daytime, but against the background of a bright sky, the relatively weak glow of auroras is much more difficult to observe.

How and why do auroras occur? Lomonosov was not right to attribute an electric nature to the aurora. But, as we have now found out, in the complex processes leading to auroras, magnetic phenomena also play an equally important role. And this is no coincidence.

The globe is a giant natural magnet, and therefore our Earth is surrounded by a magnetic field. It, this field, is similar to a magnetic field created by a magnetized rod or solenode through which a direct current flows. figure 1 shows a picture of the Earth's magnetic field indication lines. The solenoid and its current source shown in figure, of course, are not inside the Earth, but electric currents, the “culprits” of the Earth’s magnetic field, really exist in it (in the liquid part of the Earth’s core). From figure it is clear that the north and south magnetic poles do not coincide with the geographic poles, but are deviated from them by approximately 11°. The Earth's magnetic field will extend to a distance of approximately three Earth radii (from its center). The value of the magnetic field indication is small - only about 5·10⁻⁵ T.

The second, no less important “actor” in the process we are considering is the Sun. In addition to visible and invisible light, the Sun (its corona) also emits plasma, a state of excess protons and electrons. Such a stream of particles—the solar wind—“blows” onto the Earth.

These two facts are enough to understand the phenomenon of auroras.
