silicon Silicon forms the basis of miniature interral circuits, without which electronics are impossible today. And their properties depend sharply even on incredibly low impurity contents. Without the ability to determine the composition of small silicon samples with very high accuracy, progress in the development of new generations of computers is impossible.

What do you do in such cases?

It has been known for approximately 100 years that the most effective methods of chemical analysis are often physical methods. And among them, the real champion of sensitivity is neutron activation analysis (NAA). To explain its principles, we first recall how the atomic nucleus is structured.

The atomic nucleus consists of positively charged protons and electric neutrons. The number of protons determines the charge of the nucleus and serves as its main characteristic. Some elements differ from others primarily in the number of protons in the nucleus. The first element of the periodic table - hydrogen (H) - has one proton in the nucleus, the second - helium (He) - two, etc. Well, how many neutrons are there in the nucleus? It turns out that the nuclei of the same element can contain different numbers of neutrons. Nuclei with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons are called isotopes.

Let us explain this with an example. There are always two protons in the helium nucleus. And the neutron can be 1, 2, 4 or 6, i.e. there are four isotopes of He - $^3$He, $^4$He, $^6$He, $^8$He (the index indicates the total number of protons and neutrons). Tin has a record number of isotopes - 10, xenon has 9 isotopes, cadmium and tellurium have 8, etc. Natural isotopes are unevenly distributed. As a rule, the lion's share (90% or more) of all natural reserves of an element is the same isotope.

The chemical properties of isotopes are the same. But the physical ones may differ and very noticeably. For example, due to “extra” neutrons, the atomic weights of different isotopes of an element are different. But this is the main thing for us. A large group of isotopes has an amazing property - radioactivity. They are sources of radiation that cannot be seen or felt, but which can be registered using special devices - radiation detectors. The simplest of them are photoplastic and Geiger counter. We will not delve into the question of the nature of radiation. It is complex and beyond the scope of this article. But for clarity, you can imagine radiation as a stream of invisible particles.

The emission of each radioactive isotope is highly specific. This is his individual portrait, unique, like human fingerprints. The most characteristic features in this portrait are the radiation energy (for us - the energy of particles) and the half-life.

What a half-life is, there are relatively few explanations. Using a detector, you can not only register radiation, but also measure its intensity, i.e., determine how many particles fall per unit area per unit time. The greater the intensity, the higher the degree of physical energy gain. The more often the Geiger counter will click, etc. Period
