Square

The square is perhaps the most perfect geometric figure. It is found in a wide variety of works of art: from the bases of the Egyptian pyramids to Malevich’s “Black Square”. In mathematics, the square first appeared in the Pythagorean theorem. The proof of the Pythagorean theorem is contained in Figures 1 and 2.

The problem of “circle squaring” is not much younger: construct a square with a compass and a ruler, equal in size to the given circle. The efforts of many remarkable mathematical scientists over the last two thousand years have been aimed at proving the unsolvability of this problem. On figure 3 an approximate solution to the “circle quadrature” problem is given.

To measure the area of ​​a figure in Ancient Greece, it was familiar to construct a square equal in area to this figure. Since then, the calculation of areas has been called quadrature. With the advent of interval calculus, areas began to be calculated using intervals (Fig. 4). Therefore, an expression that is a combination of intervals began to be called notation in quadratures.

Dividing the square by figure 5 can serve as proof that the sum of the odd numbers from 1 to 2n - 1 is equal to $n^2$.
