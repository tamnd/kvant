Of course, the experiment was scientifically based. For example, he was on the discovery of multi-quantum processes and, specifically, the multi-quantum concept of atoma. The fundamental possibility of realizing multi-quantum processes did not directly follow from Einstein’s law for the photoelectric effect and Bohr’s actions; it was justified in the process of further development of quantum theory.

The correspondence stated by Borum $E_n + hv = E_m$, which expresses the law of conservation of energy during the interaction of radiation with an atom, remains, of course, valid from the point of view of modern quantum theory. However, it represents only a special case of a more general law, which has the form

$$E_n + E = E_m.$$

In this general law of magnitude, $E$ is the energy that an atomic electron has received. This may be the energy of one radiation quantum, i.e. $E = hv$ (according to Bohr’s second construction). This may be the energy received by an atomic electron during the collision of the atoma with any particle that has a sufficiently large kinetic energy, for example, with a free electron. Finally, it may be the energy of several radiation quanta, i.e. $E = Khv$. In this case, the law of conservation of energy has the form

$$E_n + Khv = E_m,$$

and the transition of an atomic electron from the ground state (from energies $E_n$) to excitation (from energies $E_m$) is called a multiquantum transition. As a result of such a multi-quantum transition, the process of multi-quantum excitation of atoma (Fig. 2) is realized.

If the energy $Khv$ of several radiation quanta is greater than the initiation energy atoma $E_n$, i.e.

$$Khv > E_n,$$

then the electron can be aligned from the atom. This is the process of multi-quantum initiation of atoma (Fig. 3).

Multi-quantum excitation and multi-quantum initiation of atoms are the simplest examples of multi-quantum processes that arise depending on the state of the atom, molecules and various media.
