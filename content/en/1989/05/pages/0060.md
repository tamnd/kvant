So, in short-term processes (collisions, explosions), only shock external forces change the momentum of a body or system of bodies, while the action of forces of a fixed magnitude can be neglected.

- It's clear? - asks the teacher. - Well, then let's look at this example. Let the bag slide down the inclined chute and fall to the floor (Fig. 4). What will happen to him? Will it stop right away or will it slip a little first due to inertia?

“Well, everything is clear here,” says the student, “in the horizontal direction, the friction force acts on it, the impact time is short, and, just as in the example with the glass, the horizontal impulse of the bag will change upon impact.” The vertical implus will turn into a field due to the impact force of the reaction. Conclusion: after an impact, the bag will first certainly move, and only then will it stop.

No, this reasoning is incorrect, and everyone who saw the bag fall to the floor feels it. The fact is that the sliding friction force $\vec{F}_{\text{tr}}$, equal to $\mu N$, is impact to the same extent as the reaction force $\vec{N}$ itself. In the example with the glass, the reaction force had a fixed value equal to $m_{\text{ct}} g$, and therefore the effect of the friction force was negligible. In the case of a bag falling from an inclined plane, the forces $\vec{N}$ and $\vec{F}_{\text{tr}}$ are impact, and the change in the horizontal impulse during the impact cannot be neglected. Whether the bag will stop depends on the value of the friction coefficient $\mu$ - if its value is large enough, the speed of the bag may completely disappear during the impact (try to calculate for yourself what friction coefficient is needed for this).

Similar reasoning allows us to understand, for example, why a hypnast, when jumping from an apparatus, can land on his feet and freeze in place (the highest sports class!). Think about how to explain that when a jump hits a rough field, the slope may rebound at a different angle than the one at which the pad hit, what is the mechanism of the “twisting” impact. But the main thing is to decide to notice and come up with new examples and tasks for shock and non-strike forces.

Polar forces

This is the name given to multi-colored, seemingly recognizable “lights” that can be observed in the far north - in areas adjacent to the Arctic, and in the far south - in Antarctica.

Northern forces (this name is more common in literature) have been known for a very long time. Ancient authors mentioned them, and medieval chroniclers made several descriptions of the northern lights. The Northern Lights, observed in England in 1716, were described by Halley. Lomonosov, who spent his childhood and youth in the north and often observed auroras (Russian Pomors call them flashes, disgraces), was one of the first to propose the electrical nature of this phenomenon.

What does the observer see? Polar forces (both northern and southern) are very diverse. These may be uniform green-yellow stripes or arcs with a sharp lower border and a blurry upper border. The altitude of the lower boundary is usually about 100 km, the upper – about 1000 km. These can be arcs or stripes made up of peculiar rays perpendicular to the earth's surface. You can also see pulsating stripes or arcs. Strong external forces are produced by the so-called polar forces, which have the form of luminous waves moving upward. It resembles a picture of a dying fire fanned by a gusty wind.
