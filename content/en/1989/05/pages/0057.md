It is impossible not to note the contribution to the debunking of the geocentric system of the world by the cardinal of the Catholic Church, Nicholas of Cusa. His argumentation was a theological wave: since God is a vededesus, any point in the Universe is equally close to him (or equally distant from him), and therefore can equally claim the right to be considered the center of the World. But one should not think that this scientific property is related only to this phrase.

Roger Bacon in the 13th century, Nicholas of Cusa in the 15th century, Francis Bacon in the early 17th century laid the foundations of modern scientific methodology, the main thesis of which can be expressed in the words “experience is the criterion of truth.”
Some may ask: isn’t it enough to have one thesis in four centuries? Let us remember that for more than a thousand years agreement with the Bible and Aristotle was considered the criterion of truth. After all, back in the 17th century it was not difficult to fall into the clutches of the Inquisition simply by re-reading the legs of a spider. Aristotle wrote that a spider has six legs, but try to count - you will certainly get eight (unless, of course, the spider is disabled), and this will be a great sin. But the fatal blow to the geocentric system was dealt only by Nicolaus Copernicus.

At the very beginning of the 17th century, the first telephone, predicted by Roger Bacon and designed by Leonardo da Vinci, was created. By 1670, Cassini had a realistic estimate of the radius of the Earth's orbit. 5 years later, Roemer, using the satellites of Jupiter and Cassini data, fulfilled Galileo’s dream - he measured the speed of light. Problem 5. The time interval between two successive satellites of Jupiter No during the year varies from a minimum value of 42 and 28 minutes 21 s to a maximum value of 42 and 28 minutes 51 s. Determine the speed of light from these data.

Since the orbit of Jupiter is more than the Earth’s orbit, and the speed of Jupiter is much less than the speed of the Earth, we can assume that during the year the relative positions of the Earth and Jupiter practically do not change.
