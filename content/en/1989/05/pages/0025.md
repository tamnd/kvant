What is it?

In the mid-sixties, a group of experimental physicists tried to carry out the following experiments: ionize an isolated atom (that is, “tear off” an electron from it) by irradiating it with visible light. At first glance, the very idea of ​​such an experience may seem absurd.

Indeed, to ionize an atom, it is necessary to absorb a quantum (photon) with energy $h\nu$, greater than the ionization energy $E_n$:

$$h\nu > E_n.$$

For different atoms, the values ​​of $E_n$ lie in the range of 4–24 eV (recall: $1\,\text{эВ} = 1,6 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{Дж}$); so the frequency of radiation, the quantum of which could lead to ionization, must be at least $\sim 1 \cdot 10^{15}\,\text{c}^{-1}$. And the frequency range of visible light is from $\sim 0,4 \cdot 10^{15}\,\text{c}^{-1}$ (red light) to $\sim 0,8 \cdot 10^{15}\,\text{c}^{-1}$ (violet light). So for the ionization of atoms, quanta of not visible radiation ($\sim 2\,\text{эВ}$), but higher-frequency (shorter-wavelength), for example ultraviolet, are needed.

Please note: the inequality $h\nu > E_n$ is similar to writing the significant Einstein’s law for the photoelectric effect - $h\nu > A$, where $A$ is the work function of an electron leaving the metal surface. This law laid the foundation for the quantum theory of light in 1905. The analogy stated above is based on the unified physical nature of the processes of electron separation from the atom and the surface of the metal.

So, ionization of an atom by a visible light quantum is impossible. If the energy of such a quantum is equal to the difference between the energies of the ground ($E_n$) and excited ($E_m$) states of the electron in ATO-me, i.e. $h\nu = E_m - E_n$, then the atom can absorb the quantum and go into the excited state. And the reverse transition (return to the ground state) will be accompanied by the emission of a photon with a frequency of $v$. Condition

$$E_n + h\nu = E_m$$

This is nothing more than Bohr's second postulate. Thus, the absorption of a visible light quantum can only lead to the excitation of the atom, and not to its ionization (Fig. 1).
