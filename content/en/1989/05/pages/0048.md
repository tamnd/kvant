The half-life of an isotope (denoted $T_{1/2}$) is the time during which the intensity of its radiation decreases by half. During this time, half of the original radioactive nuclei turns into non-radioactive (as they say, stable). The half-lives of different isotopes are different - from fractions of a second to millions of years. Special detectors not only measure the intensity of radiation, but also its spectrum - a graph of the dependence of radiation intensity on its energy. An example of such a spectrum is shown in figure 1. Sharp “spikes” of intensity are called spectrum lines - they correspond to radiation from different isotopes. Using pre-compiled tables, each line can easily be associated with its “own” isotope (this is how the isotopes indicated in figure were “recognized”).

Constructing the emission spectrum thus solves the problem of qualitative chemical analysis of the sample, i.e., it makes it possible to determine what radioactive elements it consists of.

What we have told so far should have convinced you, dear readers, of one thing: if the state samples were from radioactive isotopes, then it would be relatively easy to determine their qualitative composition from the spectra. But here’s the problem: “normal” samples are made from natural non-radioactive isotopes. Therefore, to carry out analysis they must be activated, that is, made radioactive. There are different ways to activate. The easiest way is to “bombard” the sample in the reactor with neutrons.
