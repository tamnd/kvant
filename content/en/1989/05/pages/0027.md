Thus, modern quantum theory expands the content of Bohr's second postulate, allowing the possibility of multiquantum transitions of an electron between stationary states in the atom, and expands Einstein's law, allowing the possibility of multiquantum innization. It was precisely towards the discovery of multiquantum innization that the experiment discussed above was aimed.

A natural question arises: why only in the sixties did physicists begin to attempt to detect multiquantum processes? Why, until this time, multiquantum processes were not taken into account either in optics or spectroscopy and were not mentioned not only in school textbooks, but also in the university physics course? The fact is that to implement multiquantum processes, an exceptionally high radiation intensity is required - only under this condition can an atom simultaneously absorb many quanta. No light sources that existed before the sixties of our century could provide the radiation intensity necessary for the implementation of multiquantum processes. The situation changed radically after the creation of lasers.

The exceptional properties of laser radiation are well known. A small divergence angle (the beams in the laser beam are practically parallel) makes it possible to focus the radiation onto a very small area; the ability to continuously change the radiation frequency, high radiation power - all this provides an intensity that is many orders of magnitude higher than the radiation intensity of old light sources. Therefore, as soon as powerful lasers were in the hands of physicists in the early sixties, experimenters began attempting to detect multiquantum processes.
