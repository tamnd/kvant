A remarkable fact is that Einstein was the first to indicate the possibility of implementing multi-scale transitions back in 1905, long before the creation of quantum mechanics. Discussing Stokes' law for logistics in one of his papers (with photoluminescence, the wavelength of emitted light is larger than the wavelength of absorbed light), Einstein noted that if “the number of quanta simultaneously interacting with a unit volume is large, then the energy quantum of emitted light can receive its energy from several incident quanta.” Discussing at the same time in another work the fluorescence that arose under the influence of cathode rays (electrons), Einstein noted that the kinetic energy of an electron can be transformed into several light quanta. Finally, in the same works, Einstein also formulated the basic correspondence for the probability of multi-quantum transitions, arguing that the intensity of radiation resulting from the absorption of two quanta should be proportional to the square of the intensity of the incident radiation.

Thus, back in 1905, Einstein recognized the existence of processes of multi-wavelength absorption and radiation and formulated the basic correspondence for the probability of multi-wavelength processes. These statements by Einstein qualitatively and absolutely correctly reflect the essence of multi-scale processes. (Another confirmation of that exceptional intuition that is characteristic of Einstein’s black scientific work.)

For almost two decades after Einstein’s remark, he did not address multi-scale processes in any way. Finally, in 1927, P. Dirac, one of the creators of quantum mechanics, in a large work related to the quantum theory of light dispersion, took into account the possibility of two-quantum radiation when calculating the probability of the scattering process. Dirac, naturally, already used the concept and mathematical language of quantum mechanics. The next step was taken by Marney Geppert-Mayer, who devoted two works in 1929 and 1931 to quantitatively calculating the probability of a two-quantum transition of excited hydrogen atom to the ground state. The works of Dirac and Goeppert-Mayer laid the foundations for the modern description of multi-scale processes. (What a great constellation of scientists, however, created the foundations of the physics of multi-scale processes - three Nobel Prize winners: A. Einstein, P. Dirac and M. Goeppert-Mayer!)

The first systematic presentation of the basic physical laws of multi-wave processes is contained in the book “Wave Mechanics” by the famous Soviet theoretical physicist Ya. I. Frenkel, published in 1933. In the second volume, Ya. I. Frenkel, from a detailed mathematical description of multi-wavelength processes, makes the following conclusion: “In the case of light, such (multi-wavelength - N.D.) transitions are fundamentally possible, the probabilities of which are proportional not to the first, but to the second and even higher degrees of intensity (exciting light - N.D.). In order for such efficiencies to be observed, the intensity of light must, however, be extremely high - much greater than that with which we are promised to deal in our experiments... We are forced, therefore, to come to the conclusion that double transitions caused by the influence of light alone have no practical place..."

This conclusion remained undeniable until the creation of powerful lasers.
