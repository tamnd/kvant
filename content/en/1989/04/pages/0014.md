To move from a physical discussion of nodes to a server-side mathematical conversation, precise definitions are needed. Let's start with a definition: a node is a connected (i.e., consisting of one piece), closed, oriented (i.e., equipped with a traversal direction) broken line in space. Nodes are depicted in flat drawings in a special way - in the form of diagrams. A diagram is also called a projection of a node onto a plane, in which the curve intersects itself only at isolated double points, and there is a space

Elementary properties and equivalence of knots: a) replacing the link AB with the two-strand broken line ACB (and vice versa), provided that the triangle ABC has no common points with the knot (of course, except for the points of the segment AB); b) destruction of the loop using one elementary lead; c) re-alignment of the entire section of the ACDEB node to the position AC'D'E'B using a series of six elementary leads.

In projection, it indicates which of the two branches passes below. On figure 1 there are only five real diagrams: the projection of the node $Y_6$ is not a diagram - it has a triple point (A), a point without a space in the lower branch (B) and a whole section of (non-isolated) double points (C). We consider here only oriented nodes and indicate the direction of traversal with an arrow on the diagram. (In the diagram, along with the broken line, there is a broad outline of a smooth curve, for the sake of beauty.)

Two knots are considered equivalent (i.e., identical) if by skimming, stretching, moving one knot in space (without breaking or gluing), it can be transformed into another *). The node $Y_1$ thus turns into a trivial one, that is, into an ordinary circle (Fig. 3). Among the knots figure 1 there is another trivial knot (try to guess which one), but it is not as easy to untangle as the knot $Y_1$.

Guess what kind of node this is? That's right: node $Y_4$ is also trivial. But a person with normal spatial imagination usually does not immediately unravel it, only with the help of a pencil, paper and eraser (or blackboard and chalk).

Exercise 1. Draw the process of unraveling the knot $Y_4$.

Now it is time to raise general questions.

1. Unraveling problem. Find an algorithm that can tell from any node diagram whether a node is trivial or not.

This question is a special case of an even more general one.

2. The problem of comparison. Find an algorithm that can tell from any pair of diagrams whether they are equivalent or not. (The disentanglement problem is a comparison gap with a trivial knot.)

*) A large, precise definition: two nodes are equivalent if one can go from one to the other along a finite chain of elementary links (replacing one link of a broken line with a two-strand broken line and back - see figure 2).
