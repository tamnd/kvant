⟦folio 11⟧

Knots appeared in historical times - along with the first threads and ropes. Or maybe even earlier: vines get knotted easily. Practical skills in handling knots go deep into history - they were used by the first sailors, weavers, and builders. But scientists began to systematically study line knots relatively recently: at the end of the century. Apparently, the rigid framework of traditional geometry was too testing for these thin and flexible objects. The theory of knots began to be successfully developed only together with theology - the science of the deepest properties of figures and their arrangement - taking a modest but worthy place in it. Modest - for the time being.

In recent years, knot theory has ceased to be a leak of only a small number of specialties, and has unexpectedly turned into one of the most fashionable hobbies of mathematicians, physicists (both “classical” and “quantum”) and even geneticists.

But more on that a little later. For now, let's look at the nodes from the point of view of a mathematician and ask the most natural question:

Can any knot be untangled?
