So, if the invariant is different, then the nodes are different. If the converse is also true (the coincidence of invariants implies the equivalence of nodes), then the invariant is called complete. For such a complex task as comparing nodes, no simple complete invariant is known. However, I would like the invariant to be quite subtle, that is, to allow one to distinguish, if not all, then many nodes.

How to find such an invariant? To do this, we will have to somewhat expand the class of objects under consideration: study not only nodes, but also more general concepts -

Pinching

A split is a finite set of closed disjoint oriented broken lines in space (Fig. 5). A knot is a special case of a spell (a set of one broken line). The equivalence of appeals is determined in the same way as nodes.

The presence of several broken lines leads to fundamentally new knotting effects. For example, two circles (trival nodes) can form an unshared pair (3₁). Even more interesting is the Borromean chain mail (3₂) - three circles, unwrapped in pairs, but which together cannot be untangled. Sequentially sing small circles, you can build long chains and even knot them (3₅). The chain of more complex loops (3₆) is also interesting: if you unhook the “left lock” (i.e., change the leftmost double point to the opposite one), then the chain is sequentially distributed from left to right. This effect is somewhat reminiscent of the running of the “arrow” on nylon stockings or the “unraveling” of a woolen sweater.

Convel polynomial

named after the English mathematician J. Conwell. However, in scientific literature this polynomial is more often called the Alexander-Convel polynomial. J. Alexander, an outstanding American topologist, came up with his polynomial before Convel, in 1933 (the Convel polynomial differs from the Alexander polynomial by a simple change of variables), but his design is exquisitely beautiful! - is far from elementary. Convel's merit, of course, is not in the replacement of variables, but in the perfect elementary axiomatic construction of the polynomial. I describe this construction below, retaining for the polynomial only the name of J. Convel, one of the brightest and most versatile mathematicians of the 20th century, the creator of the game “Life” (see «Kvant», 1974, No. 9), sorreal numbers (see Quantum, 1979, No. 11), several sporadic monsters (see «Kvant», 1987, No. 2) and many other tricky things.

Convely arrived at the idea that each diagram of a knot or link L is associated with a polynomial (polynomial) in the variable x with integer coefficients. This polynomial is denoted P_L(x). In this case, three axioms must be fulfilled:

I. Equivalent diagrams L and L' correspond to the same polynomial:

$$P_L(x) = P_L(x).$$

II. The trival node corresponds to a polynomial (of degree zero), different 1:

$$P_0(x) = 1.$$
