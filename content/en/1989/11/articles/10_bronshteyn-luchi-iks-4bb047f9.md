X RAYS
(Chapters from the book)

M. P. BRONSHTEIN

First news

In January 1896, strange news covered the entire globe.

Some German scientist managed to discover hitherto unknown rays with mysterious properties.

The first mysterious property of rays is that they are invisible. No matter how much you direct your vision, it is impossible to smooth them out. They are not painted in any way - they have no color.

The second amazing property is that they pass through thick cardboard, through aluminum, through thick docks, through tin paper. What is opaque is transparent to them. You can’t hide from them behind a wooden wall, behind a door. A wooden door lets them through like a glass door.

And the third property of rays is that there are substances on which they produce an extraordinary effect. Crystals of barium platinocyanide, vilLEMENT, and zinc sulfide suddenly flare up with bright light as soon as invisible rays fall on them. Under the influence of invisible rays, a photographic plate turns black. And the air itself changes miraculously when it is produced by invisible rays: it acquires a new property - the ability to transmit electric current.

The newspapers that published what was known about the rays only briefly mentioned the name of the man who made the extraordinary discovery: Wilhelm Conrad Roentgen.

Our readers could already get acquainted with “The Inventors of Radiotelegraph” - chapters from this book were published in the federal issue «Kvant» for 1987. And now we offer you an excerpt from the book “X-Rays”. The publication was prepared by G. E. Gorelik.

Cautious Scientist

Wilhelm Conrad Roentgen was a professor of physics in the market town of Würzburg.

The shy professor, in such a voice as he reads his lectures from the department of the ancient university, was little known even in his own city. But scientists all over the world know him well.

In all twenty-five German universities there was no scientist who worked more conscientiously, carefully, more carefully than the physicist Roentgen. He studied many phenomena in his laboratory and made many precise measurements. But Roentgen did not report all of his works, not all of his experiments and discoveries in scientific journals. He had a strict rule: he published an article about his experiments only when he was finally convinced of their correctness and accuracy. If there was even the slightest doubt about the correctness of the experiment, the cautious scientist did not write anything about it.

Roentgen was wary of hasty hypotheses, hasty agreements, fantasies.

In 1895, Wilhelm Conrad Roentgen began to study how to account for electric current through rarefied gases.

Scientists studied this phenomenon even before X-ray. The German physicists Goldstein and Hittorf, long before Roentgen, passed an electric current through air rarefied by a strong air pump. They built special instruments to study this current and carried out the first experiments. But much still remained unclear. The famous physicist Heinrich Hertz - the same Hertz who discovered radio waves - argued that an electric current flowing through a rarefied gas is also a wave - vibrations similar to vibrations of sound. Another agreement was expressed by the Englishman Crookes. He said that the electric current in a rarefied gas is not waves at all, but a flow of tiny particles invisible to the eye - electrons. At an incredible speed - ten thousand kilometers per second! — they fly through the rarefied gas.

Scientists were divided. Some people read that Heinrich Hertz was right, others that William Crookes was right. And only the incredulous Roentgen did not participate in this dispute. He was neither on Hertz's side nor on Cruz's side.

He stubbornly resisted any assumptions and agreements: he argued that the time had not yet come for them and that it was necessary to do as many experiments as possible, to accumulate as many reliable facts as possible.

In 1895, in the last days of October, Roentgen collected all the necessary materials and instruments in his laboratory and began experimenting.

Beginning of experiments

X-ray took a glass ball with two soldered inner metal plates. There was a wire attached to both plates. The ends of the wires stuck out through the glass wall of the ball.

Then Roentgen took a glass pump and began pumping air out of the balloon. The air was leaving, and there was less and less of it. When it was possible to pump out so much air that only one millionth of it remained in the ball, X-ray burned the ball.

The device for passing an electric current through a rarefied gas was ready.

Now you just have to connect the ends of the wires coming out of the ball with the strips of the machine that supplies electrical voltage, and the current will flow inside the ball through rarefied air from one metal plate to another.

X-ray had a machine that produced high electrical voltage. It was an induction coil, a device invented in the mid-19th century by the Parisian mechanic Ruhmkorff. In appearance, this device looks like a spool of thread, but only it is much larger than an ordinary spool, and together with the threads, wiring is wound around it: ten thousand turns of the thinnest electrical wire, covered with reliable insulation.

The Ruhmkorff coil is not empty inside. Another coil is placed in it - several hundred turns of wire and not thin, but only. Two windings - broken and internal - are designed to increase the voltage of the electric current. If an alternating electric current is passed through the internal winding, then the

The Ruhmkorff coil is an electric current converter: it converts low-direction currents into high-direction currents. Thus began Roentgen's experiments.

An unexpected find

A few days later, on November 8, 1895, Roentgen discovered an unusual phenomenon.

It happened like this.

It was evening. The assistants, who had been working on their measurements all day, went home tired. The old resident also left. X-ray was left alone in the LAB. He was going to work until late at night. The hammer of the induction coil crackled, green-yellow light streamed from the glass bottle. This was not the first balloon, not the glass ball with which Roentgen began his experiments. Over the past week he had made several glass cylinders, all of them different. One was spherical, others were pear-shaped, and others were narrow and long glass tubes. Some cylinders contained rarefied air, others contained rarefied nitrogen, hydrogen, and oxygen. But in each cylinder - and in the ball, and in the tube, and in the pear, and in the oxygen cylinder, and in the nitrogen cylinder - metal plates were equally rotated, and thin runs protruded out of all the cylinders.

That evening, Roentgen was busy attaching his cylinders to the induction coil one by one and passing an electric current through them. He wanted to find out how the degree of rarefaction of the gas, the shape of the cylinder, and the shape and arrangement of metal plates affected the electric current.

Roentgen carefully entered the results of his observations into his laboratory diary.

The clocks struck the same. X-ray was ready to go to sleep. He covered his last cylinder with a thick cardboard case. All that remained was to warm up the current in the induction coil, turn off the light and leave. But due to his absent-mindedness, Roentgen forgot to turn off the coil. He turned off the light and was about to head towards the door when the crack of a knocker brought him out of his thoughts. X-ray returned, and then a surprising sight appeared before his eyes.

On the table—not on the table where the glass bottle stood, but on the one next to it—a strange glow flickered. A small object was burning with a dim green-yellow fire. X-ray went to the table in the dark to see what was going on there. It turned out that it was a piece of paper that was glowing. The paper was not simple: it was covered on one side only with a layer of barium platinocyanide. This substance tends to glow if the sun's rays fall on it. But it’s night outside, the room is completely dark. Why does barium platinocyanide glow?
In complete darkness, X-ray found the switch and opened the current.
The paper he was holding in his hand immediately stopped glowing.
He turned the current back on. The paper sparkled again.
They turned me down again. And the paper went out again.
X-ray no longer thought about leaving the laboratory.

A night without sleep
X-ray decided to investigate an incomprehensible phenomenon.

What makes paper glow? Is it an induction coil, through the windings of which an electric current flows, or a glass cylinder in which the current passes through a rarefied gas?

To check, Roentgen decided to remove the balloon and connect the coils with something else, well, at least with two metal balls that crawl in the laboratory to study electric sparks.

So he did. The hammer crackled again, and current ran through the coil again, but now it no longer went into a cylinder of rarefied gas, but pumped an electric spark between the metal balls.

X-ray looked at the paper with barium platinocyanide. Paper is like paper. No shine.

Then he reconnected the coils to the balloon, and the paper flared up again.

There was no more doubt left. The induction coil has nothing to do with it. She alone cannot make the paper glow. It's all about the balloon: when an electric current passes through a balloon with rarefied air, then barium platinum cyanide glows.

This means that under the influence of current, a glass container with rarefied gas acquires some kind of special, mysterious force.

What is this invisible thing that passes not only through the glass walls of the cylinder, but also through the cardboard case covering this cylinder?

Roentgen spent the entire night from November 8 to 9, 1895, without sleep in his laboratory.

X-rays
Roentgen decided to call the unknown, newly discovered phenomenon “X-rays.” X is a Latin letter. In algebra, this letter is used to denote unknown quantities.

Indeed, the “force” discovered by Roentgen was of a completely unknown magnitude.

How much did Roentgen himself know about her?
Just only three things.

He knew that in order to cause it, he needed to pass an electric current through a cylinder of rarefied gas.

He also knew that it solidifies platinocyanide with barium to glow.

And he also knew that she could freely penetrate the cardboard: after all, the barium platinocyanide was separated from the cylinder by a cardboard case, and all the X-rays emitted by the cylinder reached the paper.

That's all that Roentgen knew about X-rays.

And he decided to continue his experiments until the unknown turns into the known.
