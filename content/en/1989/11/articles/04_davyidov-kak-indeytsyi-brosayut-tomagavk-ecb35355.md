But what aroused our greatest admiration was the amazing perfection with which the Indian warriors wielded their main weapon - the tomahawk.

It is difficult to find an author of "Indian" novels who has not devoted at least a few episodes to the wonderful art of ax throwing. Interest in this problem was constantly fueled by cinema. At that time, “Indian” films periodically appeared on our screens, for the heroes of which there was always the opportunity to throw a tomahawk at the target once or twice. The last ax cuts the rope, and when the victim, unconscious from fear, slides to the ground, spectators see with admiration the outline of a human body on the wall, formed by tomahawks descending into it. After visiting the cinema, we especially wanted, if not to learn how to throw an ax - we understood that this was beyond the abilities of the glided-faced boy - then at least to understand the technical skills of the Indians.

Now, it seems, the children’s interest in the “Indian problem” has dropped, and my children, for example, will no longer be able to distinguish a Huron from a Comanche and will it hurt to tell who Osceola is? However, they are not left indifferent to the amazing abilities of the North American aborigines to handle their traditional weapons.

The idea that formed the basis of the theory of ax throwing proposed by the reader was born when we began to regularly go hiking. In our free time, we found a dead tree to use from the camp (of which there are a great many in our forests) and tried to hit it with an ax. This is where a remarkable fact came to light. It turned out that if you stand at a certain distance from a tree, the likelihood that a thrown ax will hit it (and not bounce off, hitting the butt or handle) increases sharply. At the same time, it only takes a minimal amount of grinding to learn (of course, standing at this distance) to hit a dry tree, for example, this is one time out of a hundred. Attempts to understand this phenomenon allowed the author to formulate the model proposed below. We will see that in order for a person to be able to handle a tomahawk, it is not at all necessary to be born a redskin; All you need to do, in fact, is develop the ability to estimate the distance by eye, and everything else will work out by itself.

Let us now move on to consider the model. Obviously, the problem is naturally divided into two.

Firstly, you just need to be able to hit the ax into a pole or dry tree; secondly, it is advisable to hit with a blade, and not with a butt or an axe. We will assume that the reader can cope with the first problem.

The movement of the hand when throwing occurs as follows. The hand holding the ax rotates at the elbow with a certain angular velocity $\omega$, and the throw occurs at the moment when the velocity of the center of mass of the ax is directed horizontally. Strictly speaking, if we want the ax to appear at a certain point on the pillar, the direction of its speed at the moment of throwing may differ from horizontal. However, we set ourselves a more modest task: simply stick the tomahawk into a vertically standing post (no matter at what point). With this formulation, one can claim and accept the decision of the Earth. The influence of gravity will cause the ax to appear in the pillar somewhat lower.

In our model it is believed - and this is very important - that when throwing the ax will not twist the ax sourly. With all the desire, it is practically necessary (and the reader can easily verify this from his own experience) to give the tomahawk a noticeable additional rotation with the movement of acid within a short time of throwing. Thus, at the moment of the throw, we simply release the ax, allowing it to continue to move freely.

Let's enter the following parameters (see figure on p. 19): $l$ - the distance from the center of rotation of the arm at the elbow to the point $B$, a certain place on the hatchet where we hold the ax; $a$ — distance from point $B$ to point $M$ — center of mass of the axe; $\alpha$ is the angle formed by the hand and the axe. The angle $\alpha$ can be changed within certain limits, but it is most convenient to throw when $\alpha \approx \pi/2$. In this case, the speed of movement of the center of mass of the thrown ax will describe the following form:

$$v = \omega \sqrt{a^2 + l^2}.$$

Let us now determine the angular velocity of rotation of the flying tomahawk. The easiest way to do this is to go to the reporting system, moving along with the center of mass of the ax at a speed of $v$. In this reporting system, point $M$ (center of mass) is buried, and point $B$ (like all other points of the tomahawk) rotates around it; the speed $u$ of point $B$ at any moment is directed perpendicular to the ax and is equal to $\omega_B a$, where $\omega_B$ is the angular velocity of rotation of the flying tomahawk. In a stationary reporting system, point $B$ at the moment of throwing had a speed $\omega l$, directed perpendicular to $l$, i.e. along the axe. Thus,

$$u = \omega_B a = \sqrt{v^2 - (\omega l)^2}.$$

Substituting $v = \omega \sqrt{a^2 + l^2}$, we get

$$\omega_B a = \omega a, \text{ т. е. } \omega_B = \omega$$

a thrown tomahawk will spin with an angular speed $\omega$, equal to the angular speed of rotation of the arm when throwing. This conclusion will remain valid even if the angle $\alpha$ is different from $\pi/2$. The result obtained, despite its simplicity, is very important. Indeed, it follows from it that the ratio of the translational speed of the center of mass of the tomahawk to its angular velocity of rotation does not depend on the “force of the throw” (on the impulse imparted to the ax at the moment of the throw) and is equal to $v/\omega = \sqrt{a^2 + l^2}$. This means that the distance $L_n$ that the ax will fly after making $n$ revolutions also does not depend on the force of the throw. Considering that the time required for the ax to make $n$ revolutions is $2\pi n/\omega$, we find $L_n$:

$$L_n = 2\pi n \sqrt{a^2 + l^2}.$$

We came to a remarkable conclusion: there is a “spectrum” of distances $L_n$, and in order to be successful when throwing, you need to be at a distance from the target

$$L_n + \arctg (l/a)\sqrt{a^2 + l^2}$$

(the appearance of the second term is due to the fact that at the moment of throwing the ax handle forms an angle with the vertical equal to $\arctg (l/a)$).

Let's estimate the values ​​of the elements of the “quantum” $L_1$, i.e. the distance that the ax has flown one revolution. Let's take $l = 33$ cm, $a = 20$ cm (the author took measurements on his own hand and his personal carpenter’s hatchet). Calculations give $L_1 = 2,42$ m. Thus, an ax thrown from a distance of 2.82 m (remember that to $L_1$ you also need to add the term with the arctigens), making one revolution, will hit the target.

Experience shows that practically without any training you can successfully hit the target from distances $L_1$ and $L_2$. The rise to the next “quantum levels” is already more difficult, but many of the author’s acquaintances without visible enhancements are on the order of a dry tree from a distance of $L_4$, which exceeds 10 m.

“This is all wonderful,” the reader will say. “However, the Indian is capable of hitting the target not only from distances $L_n$. How does he manage to do this?

It seems that everything is very simple. Our model has a parameter $a$, which is easy to change: to do this, just when throwing the tomahawk, grab another place on its handle. In this case, the “spectrum” will also be different. Thus, a change in the position of the capture point $B$ leads to blurring of the “levels” $L_n$ and the formation of its own figurative “zone structure”. Being inside one of these permitted zones, you can select the position of the point $B$ (or, which is the same, the parameter $a$), and make a successful throw. Moreover, if you use a tomahawk with a sufficiently long handle, you can add overlap of zones corresponding to connecting levels $L_n$ and $L_{n+1}$. Let us estimate the length of the handle required for this

ki $b$. Obviously, in order for adjacent zones to overlap, the following task must be performed:

$$2\pi n \sqrt{l^2 + b^2} = 2\pi (n + 1)\sqrt{l^2 + a_{\min}^2},$$

where $a_{\min}$ is the minimum distance from point $B$ to the center of mass $M$. Experience shows that if $a$ is less than 10 cm, throwing an ax is inconvenient. Therefore, to estimate $b$, the value $a_{\min}$ can be taken equal to 10 cm. Solving the last equation, we find $b$:

$$b = \frac{1}{n} \sqrt{(2n + 1)l^2 + (n + 1)^2a_{\min}^2}.$$

Consideration of this expression shows that the longest ax is required to overlap the first and second zones, i.e. for $n = 1$. Substituting the values $l = 33$ cm, $a_{\min} = 10$ cm, $n = 1$ into the formula for $b$, we get $b \approx 60$ cm. To cover the second and third zones ($n = 2$), it is enough to have a tomahawk with a handle length of $b = 40$ cm, etc. Thus, in order to be able to hit a target from any distance, the values $b$ should be quite large. No wonder Indian tomahawks have such a long handle!

In reality, you can get by with a shorter ax length: the ax can be cut into a dry tree by both the upper and lower parts of the forest, which leads to an additional narrowing of the boundaries of the permitted zones. The author’s experience suggests that it is quite enough to have an ax 50 cm long.

So, the considered model shows that learning to accurately throw a tomahawk is not so difficult. You just need to estimate the distance to the target and hold the ax at the appropriate place on its handle. In this case, the ax can be pre-programmed by marking divisions on the ax that correspond to the given distances to the target.

But how do Indians throw a tomahawk? It is quite possible that they do this in the manner described above. But maybe they can also twist the ax with a brush? The author does not know this because he does not know any Indians. All that remains is to find out that such an acquaintance does happen someday, and even then the author will not miss the opportunity to find out additional secrets of this wonderful art.

Tomahawk throwing is a fun sport. maybe he will find his adherents among us, who will form an association, carry out northern explorations and - who knows? — will it ever be included in the Olympic program?
