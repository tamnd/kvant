Symmetry and physical properties of crystals

Once upon a time, people associated the harmony of the world with symmetry. The ancient Greeks, who valued beauty most of all in life, attached exceptional importance to it. Considering beauty to be a criterion of truth, they felt a certain hidden meaning in symmetry and perceived it as the beginning that determines the properties of the world. A celebration of the properties of the world. Celebration, however, beauty has lost its position and has become the property of only artists and poses. Interest in symmetry was also lost.

But new times have come. A new physics was formed, and it became clear that the Greeks were absolutely right. The properties of the real world are to a large extent determined by its symmetry. (We have already written about the connection between the symmetry of space and time and the laws of conservation of momentum and energy. See, for example, «Kvant», 1988, No. 5, p. 45.)

But what is this - symmetry? Let's explain with a simple example. Let's say you hold some body in your hands and return it both this way and here. And suddenly you notice that at some turn the body aligns with itself. Then they say that the body is symmetrical with respect to this rotation. The rotation itself is called an operation or transformation of symmetry, and

the axis around which such a rotation occurs is the axis of symmetry.

Let's take, for example, a cube. It is clear that if you rotate it 90° around any of the three axes, passing through the centers of opposite faces, the cube will align with itself. These are the axes of symmetry. They are called four-order symmetry axes. The twisting arose due to the fact that to turn 360° you need to make four consecutive 90° turns. Why is it compared to a 360° rotation? Yes, simply because turning any body 360° is equivalent to the fact that we did not touch it at all. This is the so-called identity operation.

Are there any other symmetry operations in nature other than spatial rotations? Generally speaking, yes, and quite a lot. But if he means a single body, then there is only one more symmetry operation - mirror reflection in a plane. (And, of course, all kinds of combinations of rotations and reflections.) Try to find the planes of symmetry for the cube yourself and find out if it has any other axes of rotation besides the three that we have already mentioned.*)

The axes of rotation and planes of reflection are called elements of symmetry of the body. The more elements of symmetry a body has, the more symmetrical it is. And, accordingly, the easier it is. Compare, for example, a soccer ball (any straight line drawn through its center is an axis of symmetry of infinite order) with a rebbe ball. In a certain sense, a more symmetrical body is much easier to deal with, and all sorts of problems are less likely. For example, I can well imagine that, if necessary, I could hit the goal with a soccer ball. But I can’t guarantee that I can do the same if there is a reb ball in place of the soccer ball.

*) See the article by V. N. Vaguten “Regular polygraphs and rotations”, published in the last issue of the magazine. (Note ped.)

But what does all this have to do with physics? It turns out to be the most uncertain. To understand this, let's look at crystals. By the way, we note that upon closer examination, the overwhelming majority of solids in nature are crystalline, so our interest in them is of relatively located atoms, rather than a lot of atoms randomly piled up in a heap. (Again, he wants to remind you that the more elements of symmetry a system has, the simpler it is.) And indeed, it is the existence of a lattice (i.e., symmetry) that is the main, functional and compelling factor that allows, at the end of the 30s of our century, to build a theory of crystalline bodies (so to speak, “to hit the gate”), and already at the present time - to create all the various electronics that, in essence, determine the style of modern life.

The main point in the study of crystals is the fact that in any lattice one can identify a certain elementary cell from which this lattice is built. Moreover, the physical properties of crystals are to a very large extent determined by the symmetry of this unit cell, that is, it depends on whether it is a cube, or a parallelepid, or has some other shape. And the rule is still true: the more symmetry elements a unit cell has, the easier it will be to detect some unexpected physical effects in it.
