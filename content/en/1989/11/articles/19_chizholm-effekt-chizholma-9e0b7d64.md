⟦folio 64⟧

Chisholm effect

Like most scientific discoveries, generally accepted in this work, rest on experimental data, in the long process of accumulation of which several generations of observables participated. It is my pleasant duty to thank them for the voluminous notes in which everything related to various kinds of delays and failures is recorded; this is a whole mountain of data, and until now there has been no rigorous theory that would connect them into a coherent science.

I don’t want to say that there has been a lack of attempts to explain what exactly happens when people suffer to complete something. Already in the Middle Ages, fortune was considered a capital god, and Shakespeare was close to the heart of the matter when he called it “fickle.” A strictly scientific explanation of the phenomenon under consideration has become possible only in our time. The difference between the expectation and the results obtained, as it turns out, can be written down in the form of an exact ratio... An organization called the International Association of Philosophical Engineers has already published some of its observations: “Whatever calculations are made, any error that can creep into it will creep in” and “Any device that requires adjustment and adjustment is subject to both with maximum difficulty.”

It remains only to generalize these and many other observations made in various special fields, and to write down the perfect general, universal principle behind them, which is subject in all cases to purposeful human activity. I call this generalization *Chisholm's first law*:

Everything that can go bad will go bad.

Further research shows that the logic that governs the phenomena we are considering is not Aristotelian, since the consequence of Chisholm’s first law is as follows:

Everything that cannot deteriorate will also deteriorate.

Anyone who has to deal with plans, projects and programs will immediately notice the order that these approval processes impose on their own failures. Indeed, these generalizations are distinguished by that classical simplicity by which we immediately recognize fundamental discoveries like $E=mc^2$. Administrators, football coaches, married generals, trying to recount their husbands, will immediately be forced to admit (each for his field of activity) the validity of the first law.

It has long been known that in physical systems energy (a measure of disorder) tends to increase and that systems with high energy lose it in the struggle with a less highly processed environment. An analogue of this second law of thermodynamics also exists in life... Therefore, I formulate in the most general form *Chisholm’s second law*:

When things are going well, something is bound to go wrong and in the very near future they will go even worse.

One can easily obtain the second corollary:

If it seems to you that the situation is improving, then you have not noticed something.

Traditionally, fundamental scientific laws are grouped in groups of three, so I will hasten to formulate *Chisholm's third law*. Previous work in this area has been carried out by many lecturers, writers, committee chairmen and lovers, who often find that people hear things from you that you did not tell them. So, to summarize:

People understand any goal differently than the person indicating it.

Corollary of the first:

If the clarity of your explanation excludes false interpretation, someone will still sing you incorrectly.

Corollary two:

If you are sure that your action will meet with universal approval, it will be wrong for someone.

Taking into account Chisholm's laws as decisive factors when planning any process should reduce general nervous tension and solve the national problem of overproduction of adrenaline.

F. Chisholm
(From the book “Physics continue to joke”)
