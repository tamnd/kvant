In cases, the crystal is said to be isotropic (in contrast to the anisotropic case, when the properties of the body are different in different directions).*) When the shape of the unit cell changes (this can happen, in particular, with a change in temperature), the number of symmetrine elements in it decreases, and the properties of the crystal change sharply. For example, cubic crystals in their elastic properties are characterized by only three moduli of elasticity: slope, shear and torsion. If the unit cell has the shape of a parallelepid, then the number of elasticity models is already equal to eighteen. These are, of course, the same models of roll-off, shear and torsion, but they are now different in different directions.

A completely similar situation occurs during thermal expansion of crystals. While a crystal with a cubic lattice is characterized by a single coefficient of thermal expansion, the expansion of crystals with a more complex formula cell is determined by three factors.

Interestingly, the number of shapes a unit cell can have is limited. And this is connected with this. The crystal lattice, by its very meaning, is a periodic system. This means that when it is displaced, or, as they say in physics, translated, over certain distances (period), the lattice is aligned with itself. In other words, with a lattice, in contrast to a single body, it is possible to carry out a specific symmetry operation—translation. So, it turns out that not every shape of a unit cell is consistent with translational symmetry. For example, it is possible to build a periodic structure from regular hexagonal structures, but not from pentahedral ones.

Today, all sorts of types of crystal lattices have been predicted, and it is more or less known about each of them, in which directions it splits more easily, and in which it turns out differently, how crystals of different symmetries conduct heat and how they behave in an electron field, how sound propagates through them and whether a piezoelectric effect is possible for them (the appearance of charges on the grains of a crystal during its deformation) and much more.

In conclusion, I would also like to say that the role of symmetry in physics cannot be exhausted by the replacement properties of crystals. Along with themes, physical laws also have symmetry. The only difference is that the bodies are symmetrical relative to spatial rotations and reflections, and the laws are relative to some other operations. And if the symmetry of bodies has been studied practically to the end, then the role of the symmetry of the laws of nature is still far from clear. But there is reason to believe that it is along this path that the most outstanding discoveries await us.

E. E. Gorodetsky

Diffraction of light by a circle

I would like to start with a somewhat strange, at first glance, question: does light always pass through the answer continuously? It is clear that from the very tone of the question it should be answered that this is not always the case. But still, how can responsibility be placed on something? Using the “deductive method” (remember Sherlock Holmes?), we can safely say that, in principle, an answer can influence something in two ways: either something does not pass through it, or something else passes through it.

Let's try to understand what this “something” is. To make the task easier, let's remember some information about light as a wave.

An electromagnetic wave, of which visible light is a special case, is the propagation of an oscillation of electric and magnetic fields (propagation speed $c=3 \cdot 10^8$ m/s, wavelength, for green light, $\lambda \sim 5 \cdot 10^{-7}$ m and oscillation frequency $\omega \sim 4 \cdot 10^{15}$ s$^{-1}$). Despite the fact that the magnitude of the electric (and magnetic) field strength at each point changes continuously with time, the strength values ​​at any given moment at two different points are uniquely related to each other. So, if the distance between two points is equal to an integer number of wavelengths, or, what is the same, an even number of field waves, then the tensions at these points are always equal to each other. At points, the distance between which is equal to an odd number of field waves, the tensions are equal in magnitude, but opposite in voltage.

From the above, a simple rule follows that allows one to find the conditions for maximum and minimum illumination during the interference of two waves. Let the coherent sources be synchronized in such a way that the electric field strength at the points where the sources are located is equal to each other. In this case, the sources are said to oscillate in phase. If the distances $l_1$ and $l_2$ from the sources to an arbitrary point $L$ are such that

$$|l_1 - l_2| = 2k \frac{\lambda}{2}, \quad k = 0, 1, 2, \dots,$$

i.e. the path difference is even

$$|l_1 - l_2| = 2k \frac{\lambda}{2}, \quad k = 0, 1, 2, \dots,$$

i.e., the path difference is equal to an even number of field waves, the oscillations will weaken each other and at point $L$ there will be a minimum of illumination.

The concept of a wave front plays an extremely important role in describing wave phenomena in general and the propagation of light in particular. This is the name for a surface of equal strength (it is also called a surface of equal phase or a wave surface). For each specific source, it is not difficult to find the wavefront. For an accurate source, for example, surfaces of equal intensity will be spheres, for a luminous thread - coaxial cylinders, for a flat source - parallel planes. And if the wave front is known, then the further propagation of the wave can be described as follows. According to Huygen's principle, each point is a source of secondary light waves. Moreover, all these sources are coherent and fluctuate in phase. Then the illumination at an arbitrary point in space behind the wave front is entirely determined by the result of the interference of these secondary waves.

In the general case, calculating the interference pattern is a rather complicated task. Therefore, even in our simplest case of light passing through a round hole, we will limit ourselves to calculating the illumination only at a point lying on the axis of the hole, perpendicular to its plane (see Fig.ok).

Before moving on to a specific calculation, let us pay attention to the role of symmetry in our calculations. We agreed on the shape of the wave fronts for various sources, of course, from the symmetry messages. Similarly, the choice of point for determining illumination is associated with the

the metric location of this point relative to the hole and the light source.

So look at figure. It shows an exact source O of monochromatic light, a screen with a circular hole of radius R and surfaces of equal phase (blue circle). We are interested in the illumination at point L, or more precisely, in its dependence on the radius of the hole.

Let's use Heugens' principle and find the illumination at point L as a result of the interference of secondary waves emitted by all points of the wave surface S touching the screen. Calculations will be significantly simplified if you use the method proposed by Fresnel.

Let us divide the wave surface S into concentric zones - called Tepler Fresnel zones - with the center on the axis of the hole (again because, due to symmetry, the distances from various points of such a “ring” zone to point L are the same). Moreover, we will choose the sizes of the zones such that the difference in path from two adjacent sections to point L is equal to $\lambda/2$. The voltage of the electric field created by the incident wave is the same at all points of the wave surface. We have thus obtained a set of "ring" secondary sources, oscillating in phase with each other. Since the areas of all Fresnel zones are the same (a little later you will be able to check this yourself), it is obvious that secondary waves from neighboring zones, adding up, will cancel each other out. And depending on whether an even or odd number is placed on the hole, the illumination at point L will be close to zero or maximum. In this case, the maximum illumination is created by the only zone for which there was not enough pair.

It is clear that the qualitative calculations presented here are applicable only when not too many Fresnel zones are placed on the hole. Let's estimate the radius of the zone. Let the distances from the source to the hole (a) and from the hole to the observation point ($l_1$) be of the order of one meter (a little larger or a little less - it doesn’t matter, we are only interested in the order of magnitude), the wavelength of light is $\lambda \sim 5 \cdot 10^{-7}$ m, the radius of the hole can vary from a value on the order of the wavelength to a centimeter (such holes for light can already be considered infinitely large). If the distance from the top of the wave front $O'$ to point L is equal to $l_1$, then the first zone is limited by points of the wave surface, the distance from which to point L is equal to $l_1 + \lambda/2$. Expressing the radius of the zone $r_1$ from right triangles OBC and BCL, we obtain

$$r_1^2 = a^2 - (a - x)^2 = (l_1 + \lambda/2)^2 - (l_1 + x)^2.$$

From here we find the values ​​of x:

$$x = \frac{l_1}{a + l_1} \frac{\lambda}{2}$$

and radius $r_1$:

$$r_1 = \sqrt{\frac{a l_1}{a + l_1}} \lambda.$$

In our case

$$r_1 \sim \sqrt{\frac{1 \cdot 1}{2 \cdot 1}} 5 \cdot 10^{-7} \text{ м} = 5 \cdot 10^{-4} \text{ м} = 0,5 \text{ мм}.$$

Thus, if the radius of the hole increases, then as long as it is less than the radius of the zone, the illumination at point L will increase. Then, as the second zone “appears,” the waves begin to compensate each other, and the illumination will decrease. Etc.

In principle, it is possible to make a flat surface that would cover all even or all odd zones. Then all the waves arriving at point L will be in phase and will harmonize each other (we have blocked the waves that arrive in antiphase). The result was a device quite similar to a lens.

So, in response to the question posed at the beginning of the note about what “passes” or “does not pass” into the hole, we can say that these are Fresnel zones.
