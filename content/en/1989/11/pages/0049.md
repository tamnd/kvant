An electromagnetic wave, of which visible light is a special case, is the propagation of an oscillation of electric and magnetic fields (propagation speed $c=3 \cdot 10^8$ m/s, wavelength, for green light, $\lambda \sim 5 \cdot 10^{-7}$ m and oscillation frequency $\omega \sim 4 \cdot 10^{15}$ s$^{-1}$). Despite the fact that the magnitude of the electric (and magnetic) field strength at each point changes continuously with time, the strength values ​​at any given moment at two different points are uniquely related to each other. So, if the distance between two points is equal to an integer number of wavelengths, or, what is the same, an even number of field waves, then the tensions at these points are always equal to each other. At points, the distance between which is equal to an odd number of field waves, the tensions are equal in magnitude, but opposite in voltage.

From the above, a simple rule follows that allows one to find the conditions for maximum and minimum illumination during the interference of two waves. Let the coherent sources be synchronized in such a way that the electric field strength at the points where the sources are located is equal to each other. In this case, the sources are said to oscillate in phase. If the distances $l_1$ and $l_2$ from the sources to an arbitrary point $L$ are such that

$$|l_1 - l_2| = 2k \frac{\lambda}{2}, \quad k = 0, 1, 2, \dots,$$

i.e. the path difference is even

$$|l_1 - l_2| = 2k \frac{\lambda}{2}, \quad k = 0, 1, 2, \dots,$$

i.e., the path difference is equal to an even number of field waves, the oscillations will weaken each other and at point $L$ there will be a minimum of illumination.

The concept of a wave front plays an extremely important role in describing wave phenomena in general and the propagation of light in particular. This is the name for a surface of equal strength (it is also called a surface of equal phase or a wave surface). For each specific source, it is not difficult to find the wavefront. For an accurate source, for example, surfaces of equal intensity will be spheres, for a luminous thread - coaxial cylinders, for a flat source - parallel planes. And if the wave front is known, then the further propagation of the wave can be described as follows. According to Huygen's principle, each point is a source of secondary light waves. Moreover, all these sources are coherent and fluctuate in phase. Then the illumination at an arbitrary point in space behind the wave front is entirely determined by the result of the interference of these secondary waves.

In the general case, calculating the interference pattern is a rather complicated task. Therefore, even in our simplest case of light passing through a round hole, we will limit ourselves to calculating the illumination only at a point lying on the axis of the hole, perpendicular to its plane (see Fig.ok).
