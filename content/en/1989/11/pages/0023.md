Let us now determine the angular velocity of rotation of the flying tomahawk. The easiest way to do this is to go to the reporting system, moving along with the center of mass of the ax at a speed of $v$. In this reporting system, point $M$ (center of mass) is buried, and point $B$ (like all other points of the tomahawk) rotates around it; the speed $u$ of point $B$ at any moment is directed perpendicular to the ax and is equal to $\omega_B a$, where $\omega_B$ is the angular velocity of rotation of the flying tomahawk. In a stationary reporting system, point $B$ at the moment of throwing had a speed $\omega l$, directed perpendicular to $l$, i.e. along the axe. Thus,

$$u = \omega_B a = \sqrt{v^2 - (\omega l)^2}.$$

Substituting $v = \omega \sqrt{a^2 + l^2}$, we get

$$\omega_B a = \omega a, \text{ т. е. } \omega_B = \omega$$

a thrown tomahawk will spin with an angular speed $\omega$, equal to the angular speed of rotation of the arm when throwing. This conclusion will remain valid even if the angle $\alpha$ is different from $\pi/2$. The result obtained, despite its simplicity, is very important. Indeed, it follows from it that the ratio of the translational speed of the center of mass of the tomahawk to its angular velocity of rotation does not depend on the “force of the throw” (on the impulse imparted to the ax at the moment of the throw) and is equal to $v/\omega = \sqrt{a^2 + l^2}$. This means that the distance $L_n$ that the ax will fly after making $n$ revolutions also does not depend on the force of the throw. Considering that the time required for the ax to make $n$ revolutions is $2\pi n/\omega$, we find $L_n$:

$$L_n = 2\pi n \sqrt{a^2 + l^2}.$$

We came to a remarkable conclusion: there is a “spectrum” of distances $L_n$, and in order to be successful when throwing, you need to be at a distance from the target

$$L_n + \arctg (l/a)\sqrt{a^2 + l^2}$$

(the appearance of the second term is due to the fact that at the moment of throwing the ax handle forms an angle with the vertical equal to $\arctg (l/a)$).

Let's estimate the values ​​of the elements of the “quantum” $L_1$, i.e. the distance that the ax has flown one revolution. Let's take $l = 33$ cm, $a = 20$ cm (the author took measurements on his own hand and his personal carpenter’s hatchet). Calculations give $L_1 = 2,42$ m. Thus, an ax thrown from a distance of 2.82 m (remember that to $L_1$ you also need to add the term with the arctigens), making one revolution, will hit the target.

Experience shows that practically without any training you can successfully hit the target from distances $L_1$ and $L_2$. The rise to the next “quantum levels” is already more difficult, but many of the author’s acquaintances without visible enhancements are on the order of a dry tree from a distance of $L_4$, which exceeds 10 m.

“This is all wonderful,” the reader will say. “However, the Indian is capable of hitting the target not only from distances $L_n$. How does he manage to do this?

It seems that everything is very simple. Our model has a parameter $a$, which is easy to change: to do this, just when throwing the tomahawk, grab another place on its handle. In this case, the “spectrum” will also be different. Thus, a change in the position of the capture point $B$ leads to blurring of the “levels” $L_n$ and the formation of its own figurative “zone structure”. Being inside one of these permitted zones, you can select the position of the point $B$ (or, which is the same, the parameter $a$), and make a successful throw. Moreover, if you use a tomahawk with a sufficiently long handle, you can add overlap of zones corresponding to connecting levels $L_n$ and $L_{n+1}$.
