8. Note. Let us use the median as the front of the segments described in the conditions. The clockwise direction of the polygon boundary will be called right, and the counterclockwise direction will be called left.
Let us find all the medians of the polygon and then that any two medians intersect inside it. We will move along the boundary of the polygon to the left from the end $A$ of the median $AD$. If the end $B$ of the median $BC$ turns out to be close to the point $A$ (Fig. 11), then the end $C$ turns out to be close to the point $D$ (proof). Thus, the neighboring medians $BC$ and $AD$ form “butterflies” - a figure made up of triangles $AOB$ and $COD$. The areas of triangles $AOB$ and $COD$ are equal. The area of ​​the “butterfly” is equal to
$$\sigma=\frac{1}{2}xy\sin \alpha+\frac{1}{2}(a-x)(b-y)\sin \alpha=\frac{ab\sin \alpha}{4}+\left(\frac{a}{2}-x\right)\left(\frac{b}{2}-y\right)\sin \alpha,
$$
where $AD=a, BC=b, x=OA, y=OB, \angle BOA=\alpha$, and $\sigma<\frac{a}{4}$, since $a\leq 1, b\leq 1$, and $\left(\frac{a}{2}-x\right)\left(\frac{b}{2}-y\right)\leq 0$.
Moving further along the boundary of the polygon to the left from point $B$ to point $D$, we obtain in turn all the “butterflies” formed by neighboring medians. The sum of the areas of these “butterflies”, according to what has been proven, will be less than four the sum of their corresponding angles, i.e. less than $\pi/4$.
It remains to prove that the “butterflies” completely cover the polygon.
