⟦folio 18⟧

Let's start with $m=3$. In addition to 0, 1, $X$, $Y$, the equation $x^3=x$ also has a solution $X-Y$:

$$\begin{align*}
(X-Y)^3 &= X^3 - 3X^2Y + 3XY^2 - Y^3 \\
&= X - 3XY + 3XY - Y = X-Y.
\end{align*}$$

(By the way, $(X-Y)^2=1$, which also implies that $X-Y$ satisfies the equation $x^3-x$.) In addition, any solution to the equation $x^3=x$ can be changed sign, and this gives four more solutions: $-1, -X, -Y, Y-X$. So, we found nine solutions:

$$0, 1, -1, X, -X, Y, -Y, X-Y, Y-X.$$

Exercise 1. Prove that these nine solutions exhaust all solutions to the equation $x^3=x$.

The case $m=4$ (as well as the case of even $m$ in general, see the theorem below) is not interesting:

Exercise 2. Prove that the equation $x^4=x$ has exactly the same solutions as the equation $x^2=x$.

But at $m=5$ a new solution appears. To describe them, it is enough to indicate one of them, and this can be done like this. Let's make a sequence $z_k$, in which $z_1=2$ and the number $z_k$ consists of the last $k$ digits of the number $z_{k-1}$:

$$z_1=2, z_2=32, z_3=432, \dots$$

Exercise 3. Prove that $z_k$ is obtained from $z_{k-1}$ when writing a word of one digit and that $z_{k-2}=z_k$ for any $k$ is divisible by $10^k$.

The numbers $z_k$ make up a supernumber (we write it with 10 digits):

$$Z = \dots 9879186432.$$

Exercise 4. Equation $x^5=x$ has fifteen solutions: nine solutions to equation $x^3=x$ (see equation 1) and another $Z, -Z, X-Z, Z-X, X+Z, -X-Z$.

Large $m$ new solutions do not appear. The reader can try to prove the following theorem on his own:

If $m$ is even, then equation $x^m=x$ has (in supernumbers) the same solutions as equation $x^2=x$. If $m$ has the form $4n-1$, then equation $x^m=x$ has the same solutions as equation $x^3=x$. If $m$ has the form $4n+1$, then equation $x^m=x$ has the same solutions as equation $x^5=x$.

Let us give another formulation related to the equation $x^m=1$:

If $m$ is odd, then the equation $x^m=1$ has a unique solution $(x=1)$. If $m$ is divisible by 2 but not divisible by 4, then the equation $x^m=1$ has four solutions: $1, -1, X-Y, Y-X$. If $m$ is divisible by 4, then the equation $x^m=1$ has eight solutions: $1, -1, X-Y, Y-X, X-Z, Z-X, X+Z, -X-Z$.

Two more exercises. Exercise 5. For which (common) numbers $m$ is the equation $mx=1$ solvable in supernumbers (i.e., which common fractions are present among the supernumbers)?

Exercise 6. Is Belika’s Fermat’s theorem true for supernumbers: if $n$ is a (common) number greater than 2, then there are no non-zero supernumbers $X, Y, Z$ such that $X^n+Y^n=Z^n$?

Final note. If you see something familiar in super numbers, don't be surprised; These numbers are familiar to every mathematician as “integer 10-adic numbers.” All the unpleasantness, or better yet, all the surprises that we encountered here, are connected with the fact that 10 is a composite number; in particular, in $p$-adic numbers with prime $p$ the equation $x^2=x$ would have, as expected, 2 solutions. It can be said that if our number system had a simple basis, then we would have found material for such an article as this.
