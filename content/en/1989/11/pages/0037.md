X RAYS
(Chapters from the book)

M. P. BRONSHTEIN

First news

In January 1896, strange news covered the entire globe.

Some German scientist managed to discover hitherto unknown rays with mysterious properties.

The first mysterious property of rays is that they are invisible. No matter how much you direct your vision, it is impossible to smooth them out. They are not painted in any way - they have no color.

The second amazing property is that they pass through thick cardboard, through aluminum, through thick docks, through tin paper. What is opaque is transparent to them. You can’t hide from them behind a wooden wall, behind a door. A wooden door lets them through like a glass door.

And the third property of rays is that there are substances on which they produce an extraordinary effect. Crystals of barium platinocyanide, vilLEMENT, and zinc sulfide suddenly flare up with bright light as soon as invisible rays fall on them. Under the influence of invisible rays, a photographic plate turns black. And the air itself changes miraculously when it is produced by invisible rays: it acquires a new property - the ability to transmit electric current.

The newspapers that published what was known about the rays only briefly mentioned the name of the man who made the extraordinary discovery: Wilhelm Conrad Roentgen.

Our readers could already get acquainted with “The Inventors of Radiotelegraph” - chapters from this book were published in the federal issue «Kvant» for 1987. And now we offer you an excerpt from the book “X-Rays”. The publication was prepared by G. E. Gorelik.

Cautious Scientist

Wilhelm Conrad Roentgen was a professor of physics in the market town of Würzburg.

The shy professor, in such a voice as he reads his lectures from the department of the ancient university, was little known even in his own city. But scientists all over the world know him well.

In all twenty-five German universities there was no scientist who worked more conscientiously, carefully, more carefully than the physicist Roentgen. He studied many phenomena in his laboratory and made many precise measurements. But Roentgen did not report all of his works, not all of his experiments and discoveries in scientific journals. He had a strict rule: he published an article about his experiments only when he was finally convinced of their correctness and accuracy. If there was even the slightest doubt about the correctness of the experiment, the cautious scientist did not write anything about it.

Roentgen was wary of hasty hypotheses, hasty agreements, fantasies.
