The Ruhmkorff coil is an electric current converter: it converts low-direction currents into high-direction currents. Using a Ruhmkorff coil, you can create powerful electrical discharges and electrical sparks.

The indicator coil that Roentgen had produced electric sparks 10-15 centimeters long.

This is what he connected to the ends of the wires of his glass ball. A strong and frequent crackling sound was sent - it was a hammer shaking in the Ruhmkorff coil, opening and closing the intermittent current in the internal winding. And now another current ran through all the turns of the outer winding - a high-direction current. He rushed along the wires into the glass ball and continued his way through the rarefied air. He tek from one metal plate to another, and a faint greenish glow flashed on the glass stacks of the ball.

Thus began Roentgen's experiments.

An unexpected find

A few days later, on November 8, 1895, Roentgen discovered an unusual phenomenon.

It happened like this.

It was evening. The assistants, who had been working on their measurements all day, went home tired. The old resident also left. X-ray was left alone in the LAB. He was going to work until late at night. The hammer of the induction coil crackled, green-yellow light streamed from the glass bottle. This was not the first balloon, not the glass ball with which Roentgen began his experiments. Over the past week he had made several glass cylinders, all of them different. One was spherical, others were pear-shaped, and others were narrow and long glass tubes. Some cylinders contained rarefied air, others contained rarefied nitrogen, hydrogen, and oxygen. But in each cylinder - and in the ball, and in the tube, and in the pear, and in the oxygen cylinder, and in the nitrogen cylinder - metal plates were equally rotated, and thin runs protruded out of all the cylinders.

That evening, Roentgen was busy attaching his cylinders to the induction coil one by one and passing an electric current through them. He wanted to find out how the degree of rarefaction of the gas, the shape of the cylinder, and the shape and arrangement of metal plates affected the electric current.

Roentgen carefully entered the results of his observations into his laboratory diary.

The clocks struck the same. X-ray was ready to go to sleep. He covered his last cylinder with a thick cardboard case. All that remained was to warm up the current in the induction coil, turn off the light and leave. But due to his absent-mindedness, Roentgen forgot to turn off the coil. He turned off the light and was about to head towards the door when the crack of a knocker brought him out of his thoughts. X-ray returned, and then a surprising sight appeared before his eyes.
