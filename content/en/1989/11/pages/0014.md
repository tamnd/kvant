Let us take a closed convex surface with area $S$ and move it in the normal direction by a distance $\varepsilon$. What is the area $S_c$ of the new surface? The answer is:

$$S_c = S + 2\varepsilon I + 4\pi\varepsilon^2,$$

where $I$ is the integral of the mean curvature over the original surface. By the way, the coefficient 4l at $\varepsilon^2$ is also equal to the integral over the surface - only not the average, but the Gaussian curvature (this was proven in the article “On curvature”).

The formula for $S_c$ can be proven in the same way as in the plane case, that is, by approximating the surface with a polyhedron. You just need to understand what is the analogue of mean curvature for polyhedral surfaces. But this is a topic for a separate conversation...

The oscillation plane of this pendula rotates relative to the Earth over time. The reason becomes especially clear if the pendulum is suspended from a pole. The plane of its oscillations remains unchanged, but the Earth rotates, so the plane of the pendulum’s oscillations rotates relative to the Earth. When approaching the equator, this effect weakens, and at the equator itself it disappears altogether.

This behavior of the pendulum is caused by the fact that its suspension point is moving (rotating around the axis of the Earth). It is convenient to consider the Earth motionless, and only the pendulum suspension point moves. So, we take a thread with a load suspended on it in our hand and hit the road. First we'll go along the equator. The plane of oscillation of the pendulum will not rotate relative to the direction of movement simply by virtue of symmetry, because the equatorial plane is the plane of symmetry of the sphere. For a similar reason, the Foucault pendulum effect is absent when moving along a meridian, and in general along any larger circle of a sphere - on a stationary sphere, each of them can be considered an equator. If the trajectory of movement is an arbitrary curve, then it can be approximated by a broken line consisting of segments of large circles. When moving along these segments, the angle between them and the plane of oscillation does not change, but in the corners of the broken line the direction of movement rotates relative to the direction of oscillation. Consequently, the more the trajectory differs from the larger circle, the stronger the Foucault pendulum effect.

What curves on the surface play the role of great circles of a sphere? Of course, a geodesic line. Apparently, if you follow the geodesics, the plane of oscillations will not turn relative to the direction of movement...
