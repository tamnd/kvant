Let us now see what an optical disc is. The most common method for recording optical pulses has become burning, the melting of microholes in a thin metal film.

In order for this process to proceed in the best possible way, a four-layer film structure (Fig. 2) is produced on the highly polished surface of the glass blank. The laser beam is focused on the film of the working metal (5) and in a small area of ​​heating it until it melts. Surface tension forces stretch the melt, and a through hole opens in the film. The thickness of the film is negligible, it does not exceed 0.02-0.05 microns, so the shape of the response follows the cross-section of a laser bow, and the diameter of the response may even be slightly smaller than the previously indicated diffraction limit. It is possible to obtain responses with a diameter of 0.3-0.5 microns; in industrial 3U, the response sizes are usually close to 1 micron, and the distances between adjacent responses and between tracks are about 2 microns.

With other complex structures, it is also important which one “does the job.” The dielectric pad (4) is made of materials with a high reflection coefficient - this has a difference of “0” and “1” when read. The aluminum screen (3) returns rays that, without being absorbed, passed through the metal film, and thereby increases the efficiency.
