Why is the shedding of a sheet of paper straight? I also pestered many of my friends with this question. The most common answer is: the Shiba line is the line of intersection of two flat halves of a sheet of paper. And the intersection of planes is a straight line. This answer does not convince me: the entire sheet of paper can be folded, and then it will not be perfectly flat, and the Shiba line will still turn out straight. You can also fold paper on an uneven surface and the result will be the same.

What happens to a piece of paper when folded? One of its positions is superimposed on the other while preserving the distances - after all, a sheet of paper is inextensible (see the article “Geometry of a sheet of paper” in «Kvant» No. 9 for 1988). More mathematically speaking, a separate leaf emerges into itself, which preserves the distance. This processing is called isometry, and the Shiba line is the curve formed by the fixed points of this isometry.

Let us assume that the Siba line where has nonzero curvature. By indenting from the Siba lines in this place bleiben and to the right by a small distance ε, we get two lines with lengths $L_\varepsilon$ and $L_{-\varepsilon}$ - see figure 3.

Since the curvature is non-zero, $L_\varepsilon > L > L_{-\varepsilon}$. On the other hand, isometry is processed one blue line to another. Therefore their lengths are equal: $L_\varepsilon = L_{-\varepsilon}$. A contradiction arises, which shows that the curvature of the red line is zero, i.e. this line is straight*).

As often happens, we proved more than we intended. Isometry occurs not only on flat surfaces. For example, a symmetrical sphere is relative to a plane passing through the center, and a cylinder (not necessarily circular) is relative to a plane perpendicular to the axis. Lines composed of fixed points of isometries have the property that their curvature is zero. This means that if there is no line for a short distance, then the rate of relative change in length at each point will be equal to zero: $\lim_{\varepsilon \to 0} \frac{L_\varepsilon - L}{\varepsilon L} = 0$ at $L, \varepsilon \to 0$. Such lines are called geodesics (and they are also discussed in the article “On curvature”). Geodesic lines, of course, rotate in space, but only in a direction perpendicular to the surface. They can also be described as the trajectories of a free particle on the surface, that is, particles that move by inertia.
