In 1895, Wilhelm Conrad Roentgen began to study how to account for electric current through rarefied gases.

Scientists studied this phenomenon even before X-ray. The German physicists Goldstein and Hittorf, long before Roentgen, passed an electric current through air rarefied by a strong air pump. They built special instruments to study this current and carried out the first experiments. But much still remained unclear. The famous physicist Heinrich Hertz - the same Hertz who discovered radio waves - argued that an electric current flowing through a rarefied gas is also a wave - vibrations similar to vibrations of sound. Another agreement was expressed by the Englishman Crookes. He said that the electric current in a rarefied gas is not waves at all, but a flow of tiny particles invisible to the eye - electrons. At an incredible speed - ten thousand kilometers per second! — they fly through the rarefied gas.

Scientists were divided. Some people read that Heinrich Hertz was right, others that William Crookes was right. And only the incredulous Roentgen did not participate in this dispute. He was neither on Hertz's side nor on Cruz's side.

He stubbornly resisted any assumptions and agreements: he argued that the time had not yet come for them and that it was necessary to do as many experiments as possible, to accumulate as many reliable facts as possible.

In 1895, in the last days of October, Roentgen collected all the necessary materials and instruments in his laboratory and began experimenting.

Beginning of experiments

X-ray took a glass ball with two soldered inner metal plates. There was a wire attached to both plates. The ends of the wires stuck out through the glass wall of the ball.

Then Roentgen took a glass pump and began pumping air out of the balloon. The air was leaving, and there was less and less of it. When it was possible to pump out so much air that only one millionth of it remained in the ball, X-ray burned the ball.

The device for passing an electric current through a rarefied gas was ready.

Now you just have to connect the ends of the wires coming out of the ball with the strips of the machine that supplies electrical voltage, and the current will flow inside the ball through rarefied air from one metal plate to another.

X-ray had a machine that produced high electrical voltage. It was an induction coil, a device invented in the mid-19th century by the Parisian mechanic Ruhmkorff. In appearance, this device looks like a spool of thread, but only it is much larger than an ordinary spool, and together with the threads, wiring is wound around it: ten thousand turns of the thinnest electrical wire, covered with reliable insulation.

The Ruhmkorff coil is not empty inside. Another coil is placed in it - several hundred turns of wire and not thin, but only.
