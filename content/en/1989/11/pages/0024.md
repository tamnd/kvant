$$2\pi n \sqrt{l^2 + b^2} = 2\pi (n + 1)\sqrt{l^2 + a_{\min}^2},$$

where $a_{\min}$ is the minimum distance from point $B$ to the center of mass $M$. Experience shows that if $a$ is less than 10 cm, throwing an ax is inconvenient. Therefore, to estimate $b$, the value $a_{\min}$ can be taken equal to 10 cm. Solving the last equation, we find $b$:

$$b = \frac{1}{n} \sqrt{(2n + 1)l^2 + (n + 1)^2a_{\min}^2}.$$

Consideration of this expression shows that the longest ax is required to overlap the first and second zones, i.e. for $n = 1$. Substituting the values $l = 33$ cm, $a_{\min} = 10$ cm, $n = 1$ into the formula for $b$, we get $b \approx 60$ cm. To cover the second and third zones ($n = 2$), it is enough to have a tomahawk with a handle length of $b = 40$ cm, etc. Thus, in order to be able to hit a target from any distance, the values $b$ should be quite large. No wonder Indian tomahawks have such a long handle!

In reality, you can get by with a shorter ax length: the ax can be cut into a dry tree by both the upper and lower parts of the forest, which leads to an additional narrowing of the boundaries of the permitted zones. The author’s experience suggests that it is quite enough to have an ax 50 cm long.

So, the considered model shows that learning to accurately throw a tomahawk is not so difficult. You just need to estimate the distance to the target and hold the ax at the appropriate place on its handle. In this case, the ax can be pre-programmed by marking divisions on the ax that correspond to the given distances to the target.

But how do Indians throw a tomahawk? It is quite possible that they do this in the manner described above. But maybe they can also twist the ax with a brush? The author does not know this because he does not know any Indians.
