Let us consider the symmetry of this strip relative to the geodesic. This symmetry, of course, may not preserve distances. However, as we know, changes in the length of the geodesic during a shift in the normal direction are of the second order of smallness in ε. We assume that the distortion of the length when the strip is symmetrical is a quantity of the second order of smallness in ε (i.e., the limit of the ratio of the distortion to ε is equal to zero at ε→0).

This reasoning can be made more straightforward. To get a geodesic, just press a narrow strip of paper tightly to the surface (see the article “On curvature”). The middle line of the strip is its axis of symmetry. A geodesic is the “almost” axis of symmetry of a strip on the surface.

So, let's start moving along the geodesic. The angle α between the direction of oscillation and the direction of movement is a function of time t. Since the geodesic is, up to second-order small terms, the axis of symmetry of a strip on the surface, the function α(t) is a constant up to second-order terms in t. This means that the time product α′(t) is equal to zero for any t, i.e. α(t) is a constant. Consequently, when moving along a geodesic, there is no Foucault magnet effect.

Let us now consider an arbitrary trajectory of movement. It can be approximated by a geodesic broken line, and the stripes of oscillations relative to the direction of movement can be repeated only at its vertices. This means that the greater the difference between the trajectory and the geodesic, the stronger the magnetic effect. The quantity that changes this difference is called the geodesic curvature of the line on the surface. It is defined in the same way as the curvature of a line on a plane - this is the rate of change in the length of the curve during a normal shift. So, a magnet is a device for changing the geodetic curvature of a line on a surface.

All this can be said otherwise. In the article “On curvature,” paalel transport on a surface was defined. Its result depends on the path along which it occurs; if this path is a geodesic, then each direction during translation maintains a constant angle with it. This transfer condition is uniquely specified. Our result about the magnet can be formulated as follows: when the magnetic suspension point moves, the direction of its oscillations is transferred parallel to the trajectory of movement.

Let's return to Earth and find the speed of rotation of the plane of oscillation of the magnet at a width of φ. The angle of rotation per day is formed by the initial direction and the direction obtained from it by parallel transfer along the parallel with the width φ. To find this angle, we construct a cone tangent to the sphere along this parallel (see Fig. 7). parallel transfer on a sphere leads to the same result as on a cone. But the cone can be cut and turned flat. Let us assume that the required angle is equal to the angle of the resulting circular sector. And it is equal to 2π sin φ.
