CHARGED
LIQUID SURFACE

Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
V. V. SHIKIN

Who among us has not observed ripples on the surface of a lake or “waves” in a glass... These excitations, which arise accidentally (from a blow of wind, from a weak push), gradually fade away - the free surface of the liquid returns to an equilibrium state. In other words, the equilibrium state of the free surface turns out to be stable. However, this is not always the case. In a number of interesting cases, the amplitude of randomly occurring small oscillations spontaneously increases with time. Such a state of a dynamic system (the free surface of a liquid is a special case of the system) is called unstable. Studying the details of the development of instability is very interesting from the point of view of understanding the physics of this fundamental phenomenon. It turns out that the processes occurring in different systems are largely similar. In this article we will consider the most well-known types of instability of the free surface of a liquid.

I. The simplest opportunity to monitor the development of instability of the free surface of a liquid is provided by observing a stream of water flowing from a water tap or, better yet, from a thin pipette. The instability here manifests itself in the fact that the initially smooth cylindrical surface of the jet at some distance from the edge of the pipette becomes “corrugated”, and even lower it breaks up into separate drops. An example of such jet behavior is at figure 1.

The essence of this phenomenon was understood at the end of the century thanks to the work of the famous English physicist Lord Rayleigh. The disintegration of the scab occurs when the energy of the tension surface of the liquid cylinder $W_\pi$ turns out to be greater than the energy $W_\kappa$ of drops that can form from it:

$$W_\pi > W_\kappa.$$ (*)

In the process of “dividing” the jet into droplets, surface tension forces play a decisive role. In addition to these forces, gravity forces are involved in the problem of a jet flowing out of a hole. However, in this case they are directed along the surface of the jet and therefore do not actively participate in the development of instability (the situation changes if the boundary is normal to the direction of the gravity force; we will talk about this case later).

Let's see under what conditions instability (*) corresponds.

So, the void of the unrecompensed cylinder is $l$, and its radius is $r_\pi$. In this case, the surface energy of the cylinder is

$$W_\pi = 2\pi r_\pi l\sigma,$$

where $\sigma$ is the tension surface coefficient. If the cylinder breaks up into $n$ approximately identical drops with a radius $r_\kappa$, then the surface energy of the entire volume of these drops is equal to

$$W_\kappa = 4\pi r_\kappa^2 n\sigma.$$

The total volume of the drops is equal to the volume of the cylinder (the full volume of liquid will be preserved). From this condition we can find the connection between the drop and the radius of the cylinder:

$$\pi r_\pi^2 l = \frac{4}{3} \pi r_\pi^3 n,$$

thus, inequality (*) is satisfied when

$$r_\pi l > 2n \left(\frac{3}{4} r_\pi^2 \frac{l}{n}\right)^{2/3},$$

t. e. the cylinder becomes unstable if its length is several times greater than the radius $r_\pi$:

$$\frac{l}{n} > \frac{9}{2} r_\pi.$$

For $n=1$ this condition is satisfied for $l > 4,5$ $r_\pi$.

A more precise consideration, taking the logical Rayleigh, shows that the events in this problem develop as follows. On the surface of a smooth liquid cylinder falling down from the pipette hole, weak vibrations arise for various random reasons. The wavelengths $\lambda$ corresponding to these vibrations can be different. If $\lambda$ is less than a certain critical value $\lambda^*$ ($\lambda < \lambda^*$), the oscillations turn out to be damped - they arise spontaneously and quickly die out. However, “long-wave” oscillations - from $\lambda > \lambda^*$ - to arise radially, begin to increase their amplitude. The critical wavelength, dividing the entire set of possible oscillations into two qualitatively different classes - stable and unstable, turns out to be equal to $\lambda^* = 2\pi r_\pi$.

This is slightly larger than the value $\lambda^* = 4,5r_\pi$ obtained with the help of our elementary calculations, but it follows the same physical principles of the occurrence of instability. At the moment when the amplitude of increasing oscillations becomes comparable to the radius of the liquid cylinder, the cylinder begins to disintegrate into individual drops. The time for the development of instability can be easily estimated by knowing the rate of liquid flow out of the pipette and measuring the length of the unreplaced part of the liquid column.

IV. Let us dwell on another version of the problem of the stability of a loaded surface with liquid energy - when the surface is horizontal and gravity forces are present. In this case, the gravitational force takes an active part in the formation of beating energy during the deformation of the free surface of the liquid, and the problem of the behavior of the oscillation of the loaded liquid surface becomes noticeably richer than in the options without gravitational force, which we have previously considered.

It is convenient to find a description of specific effects using a relatively simple formula that determines the relationship between the frequency $\omega$ and wavelength $\lambda$ of vibrations of the loaded liquid surface.

$$\omega^2 = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda} \left( \varrho g + \frac{4\pi^2 \sigma}{\lambda^2} - \frac{8\pi^2 \alpha}{\lambda} \right).$$

The first term (in parentheses) reflects the influence of gravitational forces (g - the acceleration of gravity), the second - the forces of surface tension, the third - the forces of the electrical sector of origin (α - the surface density of pollution).

Formula (**) was first obtained by Ya. I. Frenkel (1935). (By the way, it becomes clear that Frenkel was interested in the problems of stability back in 1935. Therefore, when in 1939 the problem of the decay of a heavy nucleus arose, the necessary associations were quickly made.)

In the case of $\alpha = 0$ (the liquid is not contaminated) $\omega^2 > 0$ for any values ​​of $\lambda$. In these cases, there are insignificant small vibrations of the free surface of the liquid, known to Lord Rayleigh. The surface of the liquid is stable.

What is the further behavior of the charged liquid surface? If in the previously discussed cases, violation of critical conditions led to non-standard phenomena - the disintegration of the liquid structure, the rupture of a charged drop - then in the “flat” version of the problem, with a careful transition to the supercritical region, a new, “reconstructed” state of the charged surface of the liquid will arise - the so-called dimple crystal. A standing deformation wave is established on the surface of the liquid, which has a periodic structure with a characteristic period equal to the capillary wavelength $\lambda^*$.
