Let's try this acceleration rasshiit. Let us assume that the Earth is at a distance of $R$ from the Moon, the mass of the Moon is $m$. Then the acceleration of the fall of the Earth onto the Moon is $a_0 = Gm/R^2$. Some part of the Earth (ocean) is close to the Moon - approximately by the radius of the Earth, so the distance to the Moon for this part is $R - r$. Its acceleration is $a_1 = Gm/(R - r)^2$. Acceleration difference (tidal acceleration) —

$$a_n = a_1 - a_0 = Gm(r/2R) / R^2 (R - r)^2 \approx \frac{2Gmr}{R^3}.$$

Let $M$ be the mass of the Earth. Let us slightly transform the resulting formula and procedure into the expression

$$a_n = 2g \left( \frac{m}{M} \right) \left( \frac{r}{R} \right)^3.$$

In our model, this is the maximum value of the pregenerating acceleration.

Such acceleration will be experienced by water at point $A$ on figure 2; it is easy to show that at point $C$ the acceleration will be the same. There it is directed away from the Moon, i.e. it also creates a tide. Points $B$ and $D$ “fall” with average acceleration, with acceleration $a_0$.

The maximum tidal acceleration turns out to be less than $g$ not by 300 thousand, but by 9 million times! And if we divide the radius of the Earth by this number, then we get exactly what we need - a little less than three quarters of a meter. This is approximately the height of the tide in an open ocean.

Note that we are still considering a rather rough model. If everything is done carefully, the picture turns out to be somewhat different. Tidal acceleration turns out to be constant in magnitude, equal to $\frac{3}{2} g \left( \frac{m}{M} \right) \left( \frac{r}{R} \right)^3,$

and its direction changes as shown in figure 3.

But this is still half-baked. Something else is worse. I have to admit that figure 2 is not much truer than figure 1. The real picture is at figure 4: everything is rotated almost 90 degrees!

Overall, it is clear that the tide must lag behind the Moon. Indeed, when a certain point on the Earth is in position $A$ (Fig. 2), tidal forces are maximum, and upward acceleration of water is maximum. But the Earth rotates, and this point moves to position $B$, where the acceleration decreases, but is still directed upward. So the tidal phase also needs to be dealt with in more detail.

The correct picture is given by the range model of tides, the essence of which is this. Ocean water undergoes forced oscillations under the influence of tidal forces. The phase of such oscillations depends on the correspondence between the natural frequency of oscillations of the system and the frequency of change in the forced force.

Let's swing the swing and see how it behaves when we don't touch it, that is, let's follow the swing's own oscillations. The period of these oscillations is usually one or two seconds. Now we will very slowly deviate the swing from its equilibrium position.
