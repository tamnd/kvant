III. The problem of the instability of a charged liquid surface became “famous” in connection with the study of the sources of heavy atomic nuclei. This is a fundamental problem in determining the property assumptions for the problem of the sources of a charged drop. The analogy between the phenomenon of spontaneous fission of heavy nuclei and the decay of a charged drop of liquid in the present is beautiful and original, and it makes sense to at least get acquainted with the history of this problem.

In 1939, German physicists O. Hahn and F. Strassmann discovered the fission of uranium nuclei as a result of bombardment by neutrons - a uranium nucleus that absorbed a neutron disintegrates into two approximately equal parts. (Later it turned out that not only uranium has this property. A year later, Soviet physicists G.N. Flerov and K.A. Petryak discovered the spontaneous fission of uranium nuclei.) An explanation of this effect followed practically immediately in the works of the classics of modern physics - the Soviet theorist Ya. I. Frenkel, the Dane Niels Bohr and the American J. Wheeler. In these works, nuclear fission was described as the rupture of a drop of charged liquid.

The fact is that, with the accuracy we are interested in, the properties of a nucleus are determined mainly by two different interactions between the particles that make up its composition - the nuclear interaction, which is short-term in nature, and the Coulomb interaction, which belongs to the long-range category. The nuclear forces through which all the nucleons of the nucleus interact with each other are much greater than the electrical repulsive forces acting between protons; Thanks to this, the nucleons are held in the nucleus, which can be imagined as a drop of charged liquid (with enormous density). The surface tension of such a droplet-nucleus is determined by the forces of nuclear interaction. Like an ordinary drop, the surface of the drop-nucleus can fluctuate.
