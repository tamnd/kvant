TIDAL FORCES

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
V. E. VELONUCHKIN

From small causes come important consequences.

K. Prutkov

Who doesn't know that the Moon is to blame for the tides? And Kepler said: “Gravity is the mutual desire of all bodies.” And Newton called his fourth law “seventh gravity”. Typically we perceive gravity as a force of attraction to the Earth. Without going into a detailed analysis of the differences between these two forces, let us pay attention to only one circumstance: both you and I, dear reader, both the “freely falling body” and the waters of the world’s oceans are pleasant not only to the Earth, but also to the Moon. True, the Moon is 81 times lighter than the Earth (more precisely, its mass is 81 times less), and it is located further away from us. To evaluate this “farther”, you need to compare the distance to the Moon with the distance to the center of the Earth (with the radius of the Earth) - a difference of 60 times. So the force of attraction of the Moon is almost 300 thousand times less than the “force of gravity”.

Maybe this figure gives us a clue to estimating the height of the tide? But what can we compare this height with? Two natural scales suggest themselves.

The first scale is the depth of the ocean. On average it is “only” 4 kilometers. If we divide by 300 thousand, we get a little more than a centimeter - not much.

The second scale is the radius of the Earth. But here it turns out to be about 20 meters - again it’s not the same, now it’s too much.

So, will the dimension messages that we tried to use now not help us at all? No, it will help if we are a little more careful about what tidal forces are, or, as they are also called, tidal forces.

By the way, 20 meters is not such a meaningless figure. This is exactly what the “tide” would be like if we managed to hold the Earth and Moon in place by some extraneous force. True, the picture would not be at all the same as in reality, but the same as in figure 1: on the side of the Moon there is high tide, on the opposite side there is low tide. But we know that both there and there are high tides, and the actual picture is probably the same as in figure 2.

The fact is that the Moon watches not only the ocean falling on the Earth or the bodies located on it, but also our planet itself. The Earth falls on the Moon with an acceleration exactly 300 thousand times less than g - the acceleration of the “free” fall of bodies on the Earth. And the okanda will fall to the Moon with the same acceleration, but only on average: those parts of it that are closer to the Moon have a slightly greater “acceleration of free fall to the Moon”; those that are further away fall with less acceleration. The difference between these accelerations and the average is tidal (tide-forming) acceleration.
