⟦folio 6⟧

At which the charged surface is least stable. The quantity $\alpha^*$ and the corresponding wavelength $\lambda^*$ are determined by the correspondences

$$\left(2\pi \alpha^*\right)^4 = \frac{\rho g}{\sigma}, \quad \frac{2\pi}{\lambda^*} = \left(\frac{\rho g}{\sigma}\right)^{1/2}.$$

The wavelength $\lambda^*$ is called capillary. Let us recall in this regard that in the case of a liquid cylinder and a spherical drop, “dangerous” fluctuations developed at a wavelength comparable to the dimensions of the cylinder or sphere.

Experiments performed recently on the charged surface of liquid helium fully confirm the predictions following from formula (**). figure 6 shows the results of such experiments.

What is the further behavior of the charged liquid surface? If in the previously discussed cases, violation of critical conditions led to non-standard phenomena - the disintegration of the liquid structure, the rupture of a charged drop - then in the “flat” version of the problem, with a careful transition to the supercritical region, a new, “reconstructed” state of the charged surface of the liquid will arise - the so-called dimple crystal. A standing deformation wave is established on the surface of the liquid, which has a periodic structure with a characteristic period equal to the capillary wavelength $\lambda^*$. The view of the free surface of the liquid in this case is presented in figure 7.

Naturally, with a further increase in $\alpha$, the stationary reconstructed state of the charged liquid surface also becomes unstable. The description of this stage develops its own practical absence due to the complexity of the control determined by the dynamics of the surface in the area $\alpha \gg \alpha^*$. As for the real behavior of the liquid surface, a general idea of ​​this is given by figure 8 - this is a photograph of the free surface of liquid copper in a strong electric field normal to the boundary.
