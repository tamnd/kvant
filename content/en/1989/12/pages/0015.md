⟦folio 13⟧

Two surprising facts

Let us clarify the question posed in the title of the article: how many real roots can a polynomial of degree $n$ with real coefficients have? Most readers will immediately answer: a polynomial has no more roots than its degree, i.e. no more than $n$ (the idea of ​​the proof: if $a$ is the root of the polynomial $f(x)$, then $f(x)$ is divisible by $(x-a)$...). The reader adds, in a big artificial way, that if we count all such roots and complex roots, and even with their multiplicity, then there will be exactly $n$ roots (this is a statement of real importance, which is called the “fundamental theorem of algebra”). Finally, some remember that general formulas in radials for the roots of a polynomial exist only for $n \leq 4$; and for large $n$ the roots are expressed only (as they said back) in “higher transcendental functions.”

But this is not what this article is about. I'll tell you about two lesser-known and rather unexpected facts:

1) the number of roots of a polynomial depends not so much on its degree as on the number and signs of its non-zero coefficients;
2) Although it is generally impossible to determine the values ​​of the roots of a polynomial, there is a simple way to find out how many roots any polynomial has on any segment of the number line.

By "roots" in this article we mean only real roots (so the polynomial $x^2+1$ has no roots at all, and the polynomial $x^3+x^2+x-3$ has a single root $x=1$).

Rolle's theorem

Bolshano's theorem on intermediate values ​​was mentioned more than once in «Kvant»:

if a non-prime function has values ​​of proto-opposite signs at the ends of a segment, then at some points of the segment it becomes zero

(cm. Fig. 1). Less fortunate on pages «Kvant» of the following, equally obvious, Rolle's theorem:

if at the ends of the segment the differentiable function has equal values, then at some points of the segment its derivative is equal to zero

(see Fig. 2). The proof is very simple: if the derivative were positive throughout the entire segment, then the function would increase (and if negative, then it would decrease), and the values ​​at the ends of the segment would not be equal*).

Rolle's theorem, of course, does not state that the derivative is equal to zero only at one point of the segment - for example, on figure 2 there are two such points. From Rolle's theorem it follows that if a polynomial $f(x)$ has $k$ roots, then its derivative $f'(x)$ has no less than $k-1$ roots (although it may have more). In particular, if a polynomial of degree $n$ has a maximum number of roots $n$, then its derivative also has a maximum number of roots.

Exercises

1. Let $f(x)=x(x-1)(x-2)...(x-n)$. How many roots does the polynomial $f'(x)$ have?

*) This argument uses the non-prime nature of the derivative, but Rolle’s theorem is true even without this assumption.
