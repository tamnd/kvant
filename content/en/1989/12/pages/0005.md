For $n=1$ this condition is satisfied for $l > 4,5$ $r_\pi$.

A more precise consideration, taking the logical Rayleigh, shows that the events in this problem develop as follows. On the surface of a smooth liquid cylinder falling down from the pipette hole, weak vibrations arise for various random reasons. The wavelengths $\lambda$ corresponding to these vibrations can be different. If $\lambda$ is less than a certain critical value $\lambda^*$ ($\lambda < \lambda^*$), the oscillations turn out to be damped - they arise spontaneously and quickly die out. However, “long-wave” oscillations - from $\lambda > \lambda^*$ - to arise radially, begin to increase their amplitude. The critical wavelength, dividing the entire set of possible oscillations into two qualitatively different classes - stable and unstable, turns out to be equal to $\lambda^* = 2\pi r_\pi$.

This is slightly larger than the value $\lambda^* = 4,5r_\pi$ obtained with the help of our elementary calculations, but it follows the same physical principles of the occurrence of instability. At the moment when the amplitude of increasing oscillations becomes comparable to the radius of the liquid cylinder, the cylinder begins to disintegrate into individual drops. The time for the development of instability can be easily estimated by knowing the rate of liquid flow out of the pipette and measuring the length of the unreplaced part of the liquid column.

II. The disintegration of a liquid jet can be significantly accelerated by introducing additional forces of electrical origin into the problem.

If there are identically charged particles in the liquid, they, as a result of Coulomb repulsion, will accumulate at the “tops” of the hough, leaving its “valleys” (Fig. 2). Such a non-uniform distribution of the surface charge further destabilizes the situation, accelerating the process of instability development.
