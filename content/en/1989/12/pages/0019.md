⟦folio 17⟧

Dit through the root of the original polynomial. The Sturm row is the counter we need! To find the number of roots $f(x)$ on the interval $(a; b)$, we write out two sequences:

$$p_0(a), p_1(a), p_2(a), \dots, p_n(a);$$
$$p_0(b), p_1(b), p_2(b), \dots, p_n(b);$$

We calculate the number of sign changes in each of them and find the difference between the first and second numbers. This is the required number of roots. (Of course, if $a$ or $b$ equals $\pm\infty$, then $p(a)$ or $p(b)$ also means $\pm\infty$. If $p(x)$ is of even power, then $p(\pm\infty)=+\infty$, and if $p(x)$ is of odd power, then $p(\pm\infty)=\pm\infty$.)

It remains for us to construct the Sturm series based on the polynomial $f(x)$. To do this, you need to be able to divide polynomials with a remainder. If you know how to do this, very good, but if not, I will say that it is nothing more difficult than dividing numbers with a “corner”. How this is done is shown in the example at figure 9. Please note that the degree of the remainder is always less than the degree of the divisor.

The Sturm series $p_0(x), p_1(x), p_2(x), \dots$ is constructed sequentially. The first two terms are $-f(x)$ and $f'(x)$. To construct its next term $p_{k+1}(x)$, we divide the two previous ones by each other:

$$p_{k+1}(x)=q(x)p_k(x)-p_{k+1}(x).$$

The next term $p_{k+1}(x)$ is the remainder of the division, taken with the opposite sign (and $q(x)$ is a quotient that we won’t need). Since the powers of the polynomials $p_k(x)$ are decreasing all the time, the division cannot continue indefinitely, and at some step the remainder $p_{n+1}(x)$ becomes zero.

The way the sequence $p_0, p_1, p_2, \dots$ is constructed is very similar to finding the greatest common divisor of two numbers using Euclidean's algorithm. And the result is the same: the last of the polynomials $p_n(x)$ is the most general divisor of the polynomials $f(x)$ and $f'(x)$. This is proven in exactly the same way as in the case of the general Euclidean algorithm (try to prove the reasoning yourself!). From here, by the way, it follows that $p_n(x)$ has no roots at all - after all, every root $p_n(x)$ would be a common root of the polynomials $f(x)$ and $f'(x)$, and by assumption there are no such roots.

It remains to verify that if $p_k(x)=0$, then the numbers $p_{k-1}(x)$ and $p_{k+1}(x)$ are non-zero and have opposite signs. If $p_{k+1}(x)=0$, then from equation (4) it follows that $p_{k-1}(x)=0$. Applying this reasoning to the same equation, but with $k$ replaced by $k-1$, we see that $p_{k-2}(x)=0$, $p_{k-3}(x)=0$, etc. So, $p_1(x)=p_0(x)$, i.e. $f'(x)=f(x)=0$, and this should not be the case.

Therefore, $p_{k-1}(x)$ and $p_{k+1}(x)$ are non-zero. But then from equation (4) it follows that they have different signs - that’s why we put a minus sign in front of the remainder in this equation!

So, all three properties of the Sturm series are satisfied, and the root counter is constructed. Its design seems to be specifically intended for calculations using a computer, for which dividing polynomials with a remainder does not pose a problem.

If you want to work with the Sturm series, perhaps using the polynomial $f(x)=x^3-3x+1$.
