Let us consider the “projections” of polygons onto a straight line l, perpendicular to the direction d, and when depicting the projections we will indicate which of the polygons is located above (we consider d to be the direction “up”, Fig. 1); there is convexity of polygons here.

Now it is enough to prove the following - the lemma about carpets: if a segment-"corridor" is covered by several segments-"carpets", then there will definitely be a carpet, no part of which is covered by another - the top carpet.

Obviously, we can assume that there is no carpet that has two open areas separated by a closed one: otherwise we could consider only those carpets that lie on top of this closed area, and among them look for the top carpet. In the same way, you can exclude (remove) entirely closed carpets from consideration.

Assuming that there is no “top” carpet, i.e. that each carpet has both open and closed sections, we immediately come to a contradiction: adjacent to the left end of the corridor is a carpet whose open section is on the left; adjacent to this area on this carpet lies another “open on the left” carpet, and so on. This chain cannot be broken (meanwhile, at the right end of the corridor there is, of course, a carpet “open on the right”).

The carpet lemma is proven. It is clear that the polygon corresponding to the “upper” carpet can be moved in direction d without interference.

Let's make a few remarks about this problem.

1. The analogue of the “carpet lemma” for figures on the plane is obviously incorrect. An analogous statement for convex polygons is also incorrect: G. A. Galperin indicated an example of several “slabs”, none of which can be completed without bending the others (in this example, the “outer” faces of 12 slabs form a dodecadron, and the “seams” - the faces that they “almost meet” - are chosen so that for each slab at least two non-adjacent “seams” form a large angle with the outer edge 90°).

2. In the original formulation of the authors of the problem, V. G. Ilchev and D. A. Tereshin, there was an additional clause: the n-pleaser is allowed to move only in the direction perpendicular to its side; at the same time, the answer also changes: for example, it is not difficult to invent a family of 5-pleasers, none of which can be done in this way (Fig. 2).
