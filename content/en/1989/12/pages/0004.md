CHARGED
LIQUID SURFACE

Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
V. V. SHIKIN

Who among us has not observed ripples on the surface of a lake or “waves” in a glass... These excitations, which arise accidentally (from a blow of wind, from a weak push), gradually fade away - the free surface of the liquid returns to an equilibrium state. In other words, the equilibrium state of the free surface turns out to be stable. However, this is not always the case. In a number of interesting cases, the amplitude of randomly occurring small oscillations spontaneously increases with time. Such a state of a dynamic system (the free surface of a liquid is a special case of the system) is called unstable. Studying the details of the development of instability is very interesting from the point of view of understanding the physics of this fundamental phenomenon. It turns out that the processes occurring in different systems are largely similar. In this article we will consider the most well-known types of instability of the free surface of a liquid.

I. The simplest opportunity to monitor the development of instability of the free surface of a liquid is provided by observing a stream of water flowing from a water tap or, better yet, from a thin pipette. The instability here manifests itself in the fact that the initially smooth cylindrical surface of the jet at some distance from the edge of the pipette becomes “corrugated”, and even lower it breaks up into separate drops. An example of such jet behavior is at figure 1.

The essence of this phenomenon was understood at the end of the century thanks to the work of the famous English physicist Lord Rayleigh. The disintegration of the scab occurs when the energy of the tension surface of the liquid cylinder $W_\pi$ turns out to be greater than the energy $W_\kappa$ of drops that can form from it:

$$W_\pi > W_\kappa.$$ (*)

In the process of “dividing” the jet into droplets, surface tension forces play a decisive role. In addition to these forces, gravity forces are involved in the problem of a jet flowing out of a hole. However, in this case they are directed along the surface of the jet and therefore do not actively participate in the development of instability (the situation changes if the boundary is normal to the direction of the gravity force; we will talk about this case later).

Let's see under what conditions instability (*) corresponds.

So, the void of the unrecompensed cylinder is $l$, and its radius is $r_\pi$. In this case, the surface energy of the cylinder is

$$W_\pi = 2\pi r_\pi l\sigma,$$

where $\sigma$ is the tension surface coefficient. If the cylinder breaks up into $n$ approximately identical drops with a radius $r_\kappa$, then the surface energy of the entire volume of these drops is equal to

$$W_\kappa = 4\pi r_\kappa^2 n\sigma.$$

The total volume of the drops is equal to the volume of the cylinder (the full volume of liquid will be preserved). From this condition we can find the connection between the drop and the radius of the cylinder:

$$\pi r_\pi^2 l = \frac{4}{3} \pi r_\pi^3 n,$$

thus, inequality (*) is satisfied when

$$r_\pi l > 2n \left(\frac{3}{4} r_\pi^2 \frac{l}{n}\right)^{2/3},$$
