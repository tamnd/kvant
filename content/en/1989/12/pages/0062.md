Problem 1

Liquid $A$ and $B$ are mutually insoluble. Their saturated vapor pressures $p_i$ ($i=A$ or $B$) obey the law with good accuracy

$$\ln \left( \frac{p_0}{p_1} \right) = a_i/T + b_i,$$

where $p_0$ is the normal atmospheric pressure, $T$ is the abscilotic temperature of the vapor, $a_i$ and $b_i$ are non-octorian constants, depending on the liquid (the symbol ln denotes the natural logarithm, i.e. the logarithm to the base $e=2,7182818...$). The values ​​of the (exact) ratios $p_i/p_0$ for liquid $A$ and $B$ at temperatures $40^{\circ}\text{C}$ and $90^{\circ}\text{C}$ are given in the table:

| $t,^{\circ}\text{C}$ | $p_i/p_0$ |
| :--- | :--- |
| $i=A$ | $i=B$ |
| 40 | 0,284 | 0,07278 |
| 90 | 1,476 | 0,6918 |

a) Find the boiling point of liquid $A$ in liquid $B$ at pressure $p_0$.

b) Liquid $A$ and $B$ are poured into a vessel, where they spread out as shown in figure 1. The surface of liquid $B$ is covered with a thin layer of non-coalescing liquid $C$, which does not spread out in liquid $A$ and $B$ and prevents free evaporation from the surface of the liquid $B$. The ratio of the molar masses of substances $A$ and $B$ in the gaseous state is $\gamma=M_A/M_B=8$. The masses of liquid $A$ and $B$ would initially be the same and equal to $m=100$ g each. The height of the liquid columns in the vessel and the density of the liquid also mean that at any point in the vessel the pressure does not differ from normal atmospheric pressure $p_0$. A body is slowly and evenly supplied to the liquid system under consideration. It turns out that the temperature $t$ of the liquid changes over time $t$ as it is schematically presented in figure 2. Determine the temperatures $t_1$ and $t_2$ (accurate to an integer number of boundaries), corresponding to the horizontal segments of the diagram, and the masses of the liquid $A$ and $B$ (with accurate to tenths of a gram) at time $\tau_1$.

Applicable It is assumed that the vapors of the liquids under consideration with a good approximation: 1) obey Dalton’s law, according to which the pressure of a mixture of gases is equal to the sum of the partial pressures of the gases making up the mixture; 2) up to a pressure corresponding to the pressure of saturated vapors, it can be considered an ideal gas.
