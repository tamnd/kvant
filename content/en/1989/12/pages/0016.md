Geometric meaning of Rolle's theorem

Rolle's theorem deals with a function and its arbitrary function. Let us depict these “characters” in one picture. To do this, we draw on the plane $xOy$ a curve defined parametrically: $x = f(t), y = f'(t), t$ runs through the number line (Fig. 3). The intersection points of the curve with the $Oy$ axis correspond to the roots of the $f(t) = 0$ equation, and with the $Ox$ axis - to the roots of the $f'(t) = 0$ equation.

Suppose that $f$ and $f'$ do not have common roots. If $f$ is a polynomial, then this means that there are no $y$ multiple roots (proof idea: if $a$ is a multiple root of the polynomial $f(x)$, then $f$ is divisible by $(x-a)^2$; then $f'$ is divisible by $(x-a)$...). Since $f(t)$ and $f'(t)$ do not vanish simultaneously, the curve at figure 3 does not pass through the origin $O$. Therefore, on the curve from point $O$.

If at the moment when $f(t) = 0$, an arbitrary $f'(t) > 0$ (Fig. 4), then the curve intersects the ray $Oy$ from left to right (see Fig. 3). If at $f(t) = 0$ there is an arbitrary $f'(t) < 0$, then the curve intersects the negative semi-axis from right to left. In both cases, the movement occurs clockwise relative to the point $O$. Therefore, between two intersections of the $Oy$ axis, the curve will necessarily intersect the $Ox$ axis. That is, between the two roots of the equation $f(t) = 0$ there is a root of the equation $f'(t) = 0$.
