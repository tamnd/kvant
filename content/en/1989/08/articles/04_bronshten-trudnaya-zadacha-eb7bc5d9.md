The traditional task has a long history, spanning three and a half centuries. You can form it like this:

Where will a projectile fired from an ordium vertically fall? The initial velocity of the projectile and the geographic latitude of the location are known. You have already agreed that the movement of the projectile should be influenced by the rotation of the Earth. Those who know physics better will also remember Coriolis forces. But let's return to the history of the problem.

Half a century before the publication of Isaac Newton's Principia, the French scientist M. Mersenne (1588-1648) decided to solve this problem experimentally. It must be said that for Mersenne such an experience was not “sports” fun, but an attempt to test a “worldview” experiment. Mersenne was a zealous admirer of Galileo, spreading his ideas in France. The results of the cannon experiment should, according to Mersenne, confirm the rotation of the Earth.

To carry out the planned experiment, Mersenne recruited the military engineer M. Petit. They mounted a vertical cannon and fired several shots. The graphic shown here, taken from P. Varignon’s book “Discourses on the Causes of Heaviness” (1690), depicts Mersenne (in the garb of a monk) and Petit, waiting, as the inscription at the top says, “will they fall back?”

The experiments of Mersenne and Petit ended unsuccessfully: they could not even find the shells they fired, although according to calculations the latter should have fallen several meters away from the cannon.

At the end of the 19th century, this problem attracted the attention of the famous French astronomer and popularizer C. Flammarin (1842-1925). Having become acquainted with the book of P. Varignon and having reproduced in his “Astropomy” what is already known to us

The reason is obvious. The core, rising upward, loses nothing of the speed imparted to it by the movement of the Earth. Downward, under the influence of gravity, it will move along a different diagonal (or rather, along a curve, due to the fact that the movement is accelerated) and will just fall again into the muzzle of the cannon, which still remains in a vertical position.”

Further Flammarin writes about the difficulties of setting up such an experiment, recalls the unsuccessful attempts of Mersenne and Petit...

Three decades later, Soviet readers learned about this problem from the book of our remarkable science popularizer Ya. I. Perelman, “Entertaining Astronomy.” The first edition of the book was published in 1929. Here's what it says: “Where would a projectile fired upward from a cannon mounted on the equator fall? Such a problem was recently discussed in a German journal in relation to an imaginary projectile fired vertically at a speed of 8000 m/s in the first second; This projectile should reach an altitude of 6400 km (Earth radius) in 70 minutes *). This is what the magazine wrote: “If a projectile is fired in response upward at the equator, then when it leaves the gun it also has a circular velocity of the equator points towards the east (465 m/s). At this speed the projectile will be transported parallel to the equator. A point at an altitude of 6400 km, located at the moment of meeting the book.” Perelman was absolutely right, but he was simply unlucky in trying to give his own, correct solution.

In the first edition of the book, Perelman solves the problem this way. Due to the rotation of the Earth, when fired, a projectile has a velocity component relative to the center of the Earth, directed tangentially to the Earth’s surface and equal to 465 m/s (remember that, according to the conditions of the problem, the matter will occur at the equator). In 70 minutes, for this reason, the projectile will move east along the tangent by (70 × 60 × 465) m = 1950 000 m = 1950 km. From the center of the Earth, this segment will be visible at an angle whose tangent is equal to the ratio of the length of 1950 km to the radius of the Earth. It is easy to calculate that this angle is 16°57′. During the same time, the cannon will move east by an angle of (360·70/27·60)° = 17°30′,

After the death of Ya. I. Perelman, one of the “meticulous” readers decided to check his calculations and wrote to the editor of the book to P. G. Kulikovsky. Starting from the eighth edition of the book (1956), both numbers were corrected: the first by 520 km, the second by 18 m. The text remained unchanged.

Formulas of type (1) and (2) are found in the classical works of Gauss, in the works of Academician A. N. Krylov and in a number of courses on theoretical mechanics. They are derived by solving differential equations of motion of materials of a point in the Earth's gravitational field.

So, can we put an end to this? Have both solutions been found? For example, with the experiments of Mersenne and Petit, yes: the actual deflection of the projectile should be approximately 18 meters to the west. But with regard to the first example (with an ordinem firing a projectile at a speed of 8000 m/s), this cannot be said. And here's why.

Formulas (1) and (2) are derived under the condition that the ratio of the height of the projectile to the radius of the Earth $h/R$ is small. This was true in the experiments of Mersenne and Petit. In fact, they have

$$h = \frac{v^2}{2g} \approx \frac{9 \cdot 10^4}{20} m=4,5 km,$$

which is 1400 times less than the Earth's radius. But in the problem from the German magazine $h=R$, i.e. $h/R=1$, and formula (1) is not suitable here. We have to look for a solution to the problem again.

The flight trajectory of any body in the Earth's gravitational field (if we neglect the resistance of the atmosphere) is one of the finite sections - an ellipse, parabolic or hyperbolic. The shape of the orbit depends only on the initial speed of the body. If, as in our example, the initial speed is equal to the first cosmic speed (8000 m/s), then the orbit is an ellipse, the greater of which is equal to the radius of the Earth. In conclusion, we will analyze the reasons for the errors of previous solutions. Let's start with the unsuccessful experimental solution of Mersenne and Petit. The simplest explanation for their failure would be to assume that the gun barrel was not strictly vertical. Perhaps it was so. But Mersenne was a fairly experienced experimenter, and it is unlikely that the deviation of the trunks was large. And calculated estimates give, with a deviation of $1^\circ$, the dispersion of a further projectile is no more than 300 m. The rotation of the projectile, some asymmetry of its shape and mass distribution and, of course, azodynamic forces acting on the flying projectile can significantly affect the flight path. So the failure of Mersenne and Petit was quite natural.

Flamataron's solution would be correct if the Earth were flat, the gravitational field was uniform, and the cannon moved forward along the Earth. Then the core of the real would fall straight into the mouth of the ordium. This solution does not take into account either the sphericity of the Earth or the change in the acceleration of its straightness with distance.

In the decision of the German magazine, only the flight time of the projectile was correctly calculated. The time of its complete revolution around the center of the Earth (with an initial speed equal to the first cosmic speed) is $T = 84$ min 20 s, and the time from launch to fall, according to Kepler’s second law, is equal to

$$T_1 = T \left( \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{\pi} \right) = 69 \text{ мин}$$

(the area of the ellipse sector, described by the radius vector of the projectile in time $T_1$, is the sum of the flat triangle $OAB$, equal to $2ab/2$, and the area of the semi-ellipse $ACB$, equal to $lab/2$ (see figure on p. 30); from the ratio of this area to the area of the ellipse $lab$ we get the formula written above). The remaining calculations in the German journal contain a gross computational error. In 70 minutes, the cannon will move 1950 km east, and for the shell to fall 4000 km west of it, it must move west from the very beginning, which cannot be the case.

Ya. I. Perelman's first calculation was purely kinematic; it did not take into account the change in the acceleration of free fall with distance from the center of the Earth.
