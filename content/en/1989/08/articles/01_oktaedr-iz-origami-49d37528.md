⟦folio 3⟧

In the last issue we told you how to make a regular tetrahedron from a paper square, and now let’s get acquainted with the octahedron model. Both of these models were thought up by biology professor K. Haga from the University of Tsukuba and were borrowed from the book “Orizami for Signs” (Tokyo, 1985).
Again, it all starts with marking the square (Fig. 1), or more precisely, with the construction of a regular triangle $A_1B_1A_3$, placed in a square (this is explained in the “Answers” ​​section of this issue). The red lines in the figures are ridge piles, blue lines are ridge piles. Points $A_1$ - $A_4$ at the end of the assembly will fall into one vertex A of the octahedron, $B_1$ and $B_2$ - into vertex B, etc. As folding progresses, the directions of the ssybs may differ from those indicated at figure 1, although new ssyb lines are not needed. Be careful: at the end of assembly, the joints and combined points must correspond to figure 1 - this will ensure the correctness of the model! Figure 1-3 please speak for themselves. Next comes the most subtle moment of assembly - the re-calling of the “language” $B_2F_2A_2MQ$ (Fig. 3), which at figure 4 appears, as it were, in a cover of two sheets, along the line $F_2E_1$ (blue dotted line at figure 4) inside this cover. In this case, points $B_1$ are combined with $B_2$, an internal fold is automatically formed along the FA line (Fig. 5) and a pocket is formed on the bottom sheet of the cover, where the clan 1 is inserted. Folding along the lines $D_1A$, $D_1A_4$ and $D_1E_3$ creates another internal fold and a pocket for the clan 2. The resulting figure (Fig.5) is still flat. But here a small difference occurs: when we bring points A together, this figure inflates by itself and turns into an octahedron with a flat appendage (Fig. 6). It remains to identify the process along the line AE and insert clan 3 into the slot AD.
