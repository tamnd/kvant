Ten experiences from the “golden background” of hydrodynamics

Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
S. K. BETYAEV

It is impossible to calculate which achievements in hydrodynamics are greater: theoretical or experimental. It is difficult to further divide them into these two categories.

Of course, this applies to any other area of ​​physics. A theory often represents an experiment leading to the discovery of a new phenomenon. Thus, the French astronomer Lever, who discovered the planet Neptune “at the tip of a pen” in 1846, took advantage of the data on changes in disturbances in the movement of the planet Uranus. Conversely, experimentally accelerated preliminary theoretical developments can provide a significant service. For example, the very recent experimental discovery of the phenomenon of superconductivity at “almost room” temperature became possible only thanks to a theoretical analysis of this effect.

There are several types of scientific experiments. If there are still no hypotheses or versions about the nature of the phenomenon being studied, then they talk about a search experiment. If there is one hypothesis, then the experiment conducted to test it is called a control experiment. Finally, if there are several hypotheses, then the experiment conducted to select one of them is called decisive.

In addition to dividing experiments according to their intended purpose, a formal division into quantitative and qualitative experiments is also used. In hydrodynamics, as a result of the first, digital data is added, and as a result of the second, the “geometry” theory is determined - for example, the trajectory of a part of the fluid. A quantitative experiment is usually a “black box”: recording only an eco-signal of some impact on the object under study, the nature of which remains unknown. A high-quality experiment is of great importance for hydrodynamics as a science.

All experiments discussed below are qualitative. Most of them can be tested in the school physics room or at home.

2. Boundary layer

Anyone who has ever dealt with a computer certainly knows that a computer has construction graphics. But it turns out that it is possible to obtain a graph of some physics without any measurements, but immediately during the experiment.

An interesting example on this topic was demonstrated in 1977 by the American scientist F. Wortman. At figure 3 a photograph of the fluid flow in the so-called boundary layer is reproduced. Below you see a plate that was flown around by water moving from left to right at a speed of 9 cm/s. A thin telur wire on the left side of the image, stretched perpendicular to the plate, was heated by a short-term pulse of electric current lasting several milliseconds. A chemical reaction with water generated a thin cloud of kolyud particles, which then drifted along with the flow of water. The photograph shows the position of the colloid particles at some point in time $t$, when each of them passed a path $u(y)t$ parallel to the plate. Here $u(y)$ is the particle velocity depending on the coordinate $y$ perpendicular to the plate.

*) You can read about numerous experiments with a ping-pong ball in the article by A.V. Vyalko “Ping-pong... in the washbasin” («Kvant», 1985, No. 3).

As can be seen from the resulting “graph”, directly on the plate the fluid velocity is zero, and at a further distance from the plate the velocity tends to a constant value equal to 9 cm/s. All the described events occur in a thin layer only 5 mm thick. Such a near-wall layer, formed in a not very fluid liquid, is called the boundary layer. The so-called fluid velocity profile in the boundary layer, obtained experimentally, surprisingly coincides with the calculated one. In hydrodynamics, such coincidences are very rare.
