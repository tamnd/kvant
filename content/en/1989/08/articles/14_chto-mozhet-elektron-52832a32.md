Rocky footprint and great resistance to body movement. In the photo on figure 5 you see a picture of a ball flowing around a ball in water. Visualization of the flow was achieved using air bubbles. The boundary layer is laminar; its separation occurs in front of the equator of the ball. If the boundary layer is made turbulent, the flow around the ball will be fundamentally different. For these purposes, air that is small in size or localized in a small area is sufficient.

The famous German hydrodynamicist L. Prandtl (1875-1953) proposed the simplest solution for turbulizing the flow - putting a thin wire hoop on the ball in front of its equator. A photograph of the flow around such a ball is presented in figure 6. The boundary layer becomes turbulent and breaks away much lower downstream than in the case of laminar flow. In this case, the resistance of the ball decreases sharply (the cross-sectional area on the left decreases).

5. Nu resistance of smoke chips from sigret

Turbulent currents are very photogenic; they captivate the eye, like a bonfire. By the way, they say that smoke from a fire or from a sigret is an example of turbulent movement. A photo of smoke shavings from a sigret (Fig. 7) shows how a laminar column of smoke first turns into a regular network of loops, a staircase to help, and then, having lost stability, becomes turbulent.

6. Turbulent water jet

Another example of the development of turbulence is a general stream of water pouring from a faucet into a blanket of water. The photoskim shown on the screensaver of the article was made using glow - fluorescence caused by a laser. It is clearly visible how the initial symmetry of the shavings is quickly destroyed.
