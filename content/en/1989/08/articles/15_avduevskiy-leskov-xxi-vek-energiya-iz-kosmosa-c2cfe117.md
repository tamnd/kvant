Experts from the Hydrometeorological Center warn us in the Vremya program: it seems that the climate is changing - the “greenhouse effect” is affecting, earthly industry is releasing too much carbon dioxide into the atmosphere. In the gases they write about an environmental crisis, which has either already arrived or is about to occur...

So, maybe it’s true that excessively wasteful humanity has turned into a threat to our not-so-large planet, and its industrial activities are able to upset the climatic balance established on it millions of years ago?

Let's look at the numbers. In developed countries today, primary energy consumption is such that it is about 10 kW per capita. In other regions it is significantly less: in China, for example, 10 times, in the countries of black Africa even 50. It is absolutely clear that this situation is not STRAINAET JENTELEN ETHX STRAN. The only question is how many years it will take them to achieve dramatic changes for the better. It should be thought that in the 21st century the situation will change: most likely, 10 kW can be attributed to the average residential planet.

Let's open the demographic information: experts predict that in the middle of the 21st century, approximately 10 billion people will live on Earth. Let's cross the last two numbers and get 10^14 W - approximately this amount of primary energy will be consumed by humanity every day in the middle of the next century. Is it a lot or a little?

Let's reveal the information again: the power of solar radiation absorbed by the atmosphere and surface of the Earth is 10^17 W. This means that in the 21st century, “anthropogenic” energy - energy associated with the technical activities of mankind - will add only 0.1% to this value. Each, not very many. And today this additive is 12 times less. But will this make you aware that, having looked at the numbers, you can breathe easy and no longer listen to the alarmists who raise the alarm every now and then?

The energy balance of the earth's biosphere has been worked out over millions of years of evolution. A significant increase in energy without the use of solar energy reaching the Earth, through services, has consequences for the biosphere and for human life.

Several years ago, at the Computing Center of the USSR Academy of Sciences, under the leadership of Academician N. N. Monseev, a system of mathematical models (biogeochemical*) was developed. It was called the “Gay system”. This system of mathematical models makes it possible to calculate processes in the atmosphere and ocean, as well as the behavior of biota (all living nature on Earth).

The calculation results allowed us to draw important conclusions. An increase in the average temperature of the planet by 4-5 degrees causes an environmental disaster. But even if this temperature changes by only 0.5°, we should expect significant changes in the climate in the middle latitudes and changes in the productivity of biota. And in order to prevent the atmosphere from overheating by 2-3°, the arbitrary addition of energy on Earth should not exceed 10^14 W. It seems that in the 21st century humanity will come close to a dangerous point...

But perhaps we should not attach too much importance to these calculations? After all, even the weather for the next day is not predicted very accurately. Let us give the word to specialists in the history of the climate and atmosphere of our planet. Thanks to the research of Academician A.L. Yanishin, M.I. Budyko, N.A. Yasamanov and others, it became clear that there were eras in the history of the Earth that perfect humanity would perceive as extremely unfavorable. The occurrence of corresponding conditions was limited to small - about 0.1% -

But that's not all. Energy accounts for 60% of overall air pollution. The rate of decrease in biomass due to human economic activity is that the annual decrease in biota is 2 billion tons. The forest area on the planet is decreasing by 1% every year. Anti-pollution of the world's oceans is increasing (harmful waste, films of oil and oils, etc.). Emissions into the atmosphere of large quantities of smoke, dust, sulfate aerosol, exhaust gases from aircraft and vehicles leads to an increase in the global albedo*) of the Earth and to the depletion of the protective ozone layer of the atmosphere. The result of all these processes may be a decrease in oxygen concentration in the Earth's atmosphere. Will our streams not face a paradoxical situation when they will have to create special plant farms to replenish oxygen reserves in the atmosphere?

But that's not all. Improving the quality of life of people requires an increase in material production, and this, in turn, requires an increase in energy consumption. However, the removed weight of the useful product in relation to the used mineral resources does not exceed 10%, more than 90% goes to waste. As a result, as energy consumption increases, environmental pollution increases disproportionately quickly.

So, the numbers confirm: there are reasons for concern. Where to look for a way out? They also write a lot about this now: it is necessary to transfer industrial production to energy- and resource-saving processes, to make greater use of waste-free technologies, to build and strictly carry out complex production activities ourselves.

In the energy sector, there is such an indicator - the elasticity of energy demand by gross national product (BHII). It reflects the connections between the growth of BNII and the increase in energy production. For many years in industrialized countries this figure was close to unity, in other words, to increase GNII by 1%, it was necessary to increase energy production by 1%. In the 80s, due to the introduction of energy-saving technologies, the elasticity coefficient was reduced by approximately half. It is unlikely, however, that one can count on further significant progress in this direction: the cost of the first energy sources is not decreasing, but rather increasing.

Here we are faced with another problem, and a very complicated one. Currently, 87% of all primary energy reserves consumed are provided by burning organic energy sources - oil, gas, coal, and wood. According to the forecast of the World Energy Conference, this structure of energy consumption will generally remain the same in the first decades of the 21st century. It is expected that the contribution of limited fuels to the global energy balance by 2020 will increase by 1.6-2.0 times, but its share will decrease from 87% to 70%. It is here that the indicated problem will arise: given the scale of development of new energy reserves, adding oil at the current level will need to be maintained for another 30 years, suitable gas - 50 years, coal - 240 years.

Optimists say that the solution is nuclear and then thermonuclear energy. They have grounds for such statements, especially in terms of thermonuclear energy: it will require a minimum amount of energy reserves (the heavy isotope of hydrogen contained in the waters of the World Ocean) and is relatively safe. As for nuclear energy, here after Chernova one should probably listen more guiltily to the voices of skeptics rather than enthusiasts.

However, with the “thermonuclear Eldorado” everything is not so simple yet. Research into the thermonuclear fusion control program has been going on for about 35 years, but is still far from being realized. The cost of experimental thermonuclear installations of the current generation exceeds 300 million dollars, and at the next stage it will increase by an order of magnitude. According to American experts, the re-commercial thermonuclear reactor will begin powering up no earlier than 2020. At the same time, despite the long period of research, there is still no convincing evidence of the economic competitiveness of thermonuclear power plants of the future.

So where can we look for a way out of this tangle of complex energy and economic problems? There is one promising direction that, for some reason, is undeservedly little talked about. This direction is space power systems. Even at the beginning of this century, the founder of astronautics, K. E. Tsiolkovsky, dreamed about them, who wrote that people would be able to master “solar energy, two billion times greater than that which humanity has on Earth.” His work will develop a detailed scenario for mankind’s industrial exploration of outer space, including the construction of orbital power systems of various types and purposes.

To transmit concentrated energy flows to the Earth's surface, reflective solar radiation located in near-Earth orbits can be used. For the first time, Yu. V. Kondratok, one of the pioneers of rocket technology in the USSR, drew attention to this possibility in his handwritten work (1918-1919).

In 1943, the famous Soviet electrical engineer Professor G.I. Babat proposed and substantiated a method of transmitting energy on board an aircraft using a good fused path of microwave radiation.

In 1960, another step was taken: engineer N.A. Varvarov came up with a project to use an orbital gel power station to supply energy to the Earth. And in 1968, the American engineer P. Glaser, working independently, combined the ideas of Babat and Varvarov - he put forward a project for the construction of a solar power station in geostationary near-Earth orbit to supply the Earth with electricity.

Since then, a lot of research has been devoted to this project. The authors of this article also came to this area, making several specific proposals. In particular, it was shown that with the help of such stations it is possible to launch cargo into space. What are the features and advantages of the space solar power plant (SSP) project?

To put it very briefly, the following is proposed. A satellite with a mass of 5·10⁴ tons is placed in a geostationary orbit at an altitude of 36,000 km. Solar panels with an area of ​​100 km² are installed on the satellite and convert the energy of solar radiation into electric current, which, in turn, is converted into microwave radiation. A well-focused transmitting antenna beam of this radiation is directed to Earth, caught there by a receiving antenna, the diameter of which is 20 km, and then again converted into electric current for transmission to the consumer. The parameters of the microwave radiation beam - frequency 2.45 Hz, energy flux density 10 mW/cm² - are selected based on safety requirements and minimal absorption in the atmosphere. These are the features of the project in the most general form. Of course, there are numerous options, but let’s not dwell on specific issues here.

Now about the benefits. There are at least four of them: economic purity, safety, ensuring savings in mineral resources, autonomy (including the absence of the need for nasty power lines - receiving antennas -

Firstly, this is the high cost of launching columnar mass structures into space. Secondly, the high cost of the energy converters themselves. Thirdly, there is the danger of harmful effects on the atmosphere from emissions from propulsion systems of space transport systems. Undoubtedly, all these are serious problems, but they are of a technical nature. And therefore, in principle, solvable.

In what ways can solutions to these problems be found? There is reason to expect that with the development of the package-space and electronics industry, by the beginning of the 21st century, new space transport systems will be created and highly efficient low-cost photoconverters will be developed. According to American experts, in the future, the cost of launching payloads weighing 300-500 tons into low-Earth orbit will drop to hundreds and ten dollars per 1 kg, and the cost of solar panels will drop to 100 dollars per 1 kW of produced electrical power. Taking these figures into account, CSPs with a capacity of 5-10 GW will acquire a sixth advantage - economic competitiveness.

What about the danger of air pollution, which arises from the launch of a large fleet of package carriers? There is a way out here too: Professor of Princeton University in the USA J. O’Neill proposed using mineral resources added on the Moon to build a network of near-Earth CSPs. O’Neill figuratively described his idea as the “boot-lacing” method: the first CSP is built based on earthly resources, and then uses it as an orbital base to create a mining and industrial complex on the satellite of our planet.

Have we moved too far from modernity? — perhaps an attentive reader will ask us. “Isn’t it better to leave these questions for consideration by our descendants, who will live in the 21st century?” And we have enough of our own problems...

Let us remind the skeptics of a few numbers. There are 30 years of oil reserves left, and 50 years of gas reserves. The time for restructuring the energy sector to new energy resources, as experience shows, is quite significant - about 50 years. Think about these numbers and judge which decisions can be left to posterity and which we have to make ourselves.
