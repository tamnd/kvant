Proof. Let $n$ be the number of digits of the number $N$. When changing $k$ characters from the end of the number $N$ to the beginning, it turns into the number $\frac{N-A}{10^k} + A \cdot 10^{n-k}$. Thus,

$$\frac{N-A}{10^k} + A \cdot 10^{n-k} = lN,$$
$$N-A + A \cdot 10^n = lN \cdot 10^k,$$
$$\frac{N(10^{n-k}l-1)}{A} = 10^n - 1,$$

where

$$0,NNN \dots \cdot \frac{10^{n-k}l-1}{A} = 0,999 \dots = 1,$$

which is what we need.

Without wanting it, we learned to solve one type of Olympic problems. Here's an example.

Task. Find all six-digit numbers that increase by an integer factor when the last digit is changed from the end to the beginning.

(We will assume, as is usually done, that the number does not start from zero; we can solve the problem without this assumption, but the answer will be confusing: it will include the numbers 000001, 000002, ..., 000009, 000011, 000013... We will also understand the word “increases” literally, i.e. we will exclude the case when the number remains when the digit changes unchanged; otherwise the answer would include the numbers 111111, 222222, ..., 999999.)

Solution. Let $A$ be the last digit of our number, and let it increase by $l$ times when it is changed to the beginning. Thus, $1 \leq A \leq 9$, $2 \leq l \leq 9$. By virtue of Theorem 4, our number is the six-digit period of the (possibly reducible) fraction $A/(10l-1)$. The denominator of this fraction before reduction can be one of the numbers 19, 29, 39, ..., 89; after reduction by a single digit, the denominator can turn into 39:3=13, 49:7=7, 69:3=23. Since the period of the fraction is six-digit, the denominator must be divided by the divisor of the number $999999=3^3 \cdot 7 \cdot 11 \cdot 13 \cdot 37$ (see Theorem 1). This leaves him with only three possibilities: 7, 13, 39. Thus, $l$ is equal to 4 or 5. With $l=4$, our fraction is $A/39$, where $A=4, 5, ..., 9$ (the fraction must be greater than 0.1, since the period must not start from zero). The period of such a fraction is $A \cdot 25641$ (the period of the fraction 1/39 is 025641). At $l=5$ the fraction is equal to $A/49$ and must cancel by 7, leaving its only option: 1/7; the period is 142857. So,

Answer: 102564, 128205, 142857, 153846, 179487, 205128, 230769.

The effect of nines

Theorem 5. Let $q$ be a prime number greater than 5, and let $1 \leq p \leq q$. Suppose that the period of the fraction $p/q$ is the $2n$-digit number $N$. Next, let us denote by $N_1$ the number formed by the first $n$ digits of the period, and by $N_2$ the number formed by its last $n$ digits. Then $N_1+N_2 = 999 \dots 999$ (singing current).

Proof. According to the conditions,

$$N = 10^n N_1 + N_2,$$
$$\frac{p}{q} = \frac{N}{10^{2n}-1},$$

and therefore

$$p(10^{2n}-1) = Nq = (10^n-1)N_1q + (N_1+N_2)q,$$
$$(N_1+N_2)q = (10^n-1)((10^n+1)p-Nq).$$

Since $2n$ is the smallest $k$ for which $10^k-1$ is divisible by $q$, $10^n-1$ is not divisible by $q$, and since $q$ is prime, then $10^n-1$ is prime with $q$. So $N_1+N_2$ is divisible by $10^n-1$. But at the same time, $N_1$ and $N_2$ are $n$-digit numbers, which are not both composed of nines. This means $N_1+N_2 < 2(10^n-1)$, and thus $N_1+N_2 = 10^n-1$, which is what was required.
