However, the concept of significant interest among the competition participants was the so-called

Denogardus's Great Theorem: The Great One can be found by observing a comet.

The Sturmgard competition submitted a request to the European history-archaeological association “Archeologie” and received all the meager information available about Denogardus. Almost no reliable evidence has survived about his life. The chronicle mentions a case when one of the townspeople, while watching a solar eclipse from the base of the town hall, was bitten by a rat. However, no convincing evidence was found that this townsman was Denogardus. Much is known about the views of Denogardus. The outstanding author (quite naturally for his time) believed that the planets revolve around the Sun, connected to it by invisible hands. In addition to direct instructions found in the manuscript, this is evidenced by the drawing contained in the manuscript.

The competition participants were faced with a difficult task: to translate Denogardus’s statement into the language of modern mathematics and establish the mathematical meaning of his scholae. As a result of the intense joint work of mathematicians, physicists and historians, this problem was successfully solved.

According to Denogardus, every celestial body is connected to the Sun by a bridge. Therefore, the force of its attraction to the Sun is proportional to the distance to it (we know this statement, which describes the forces arising in the hands, as Hooke’s law; however, Denogardus knew it long before Hooke). The force of attraction can be either positive - rukina

tends to shrink, and negative - the rukina tends to straighten. Moreover, according to the views of Denogardus, the rigidity of the invisible rukina on which the planets are suspended changes over time. Most researchers agree that this rigidity is the Great Number that controls the movement of celestial bodies.

Denogardus did not use mathematical symbols - its introduction into scientific use remained a matter of further study of the formulation of Denogardus's discovery in the mathematical language familiar to us.
