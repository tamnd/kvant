⟦folio 24⟧

ka. We are seeing a strange picture: the master mathematician Juhann III Bernoulli, a representative of the famous mathematical Bernoulli family, which, like royal dynasties, was honored with adding serial numbers to names, is engaged, one might say, in child's play! He decomposes numbers written in units alone into prime factors: 11=11, 111=3·37, 1111=11·101, etc. In 1773, Bernoulli published in the proceedings of the Berlin Academy a table of prime factors of numbers composed of $n$ units - up to $n=31$ (see Fig. 1). Despite the fact that he failed to find divisors for some numbers of this type ($n=11, 17, 29$), and for three numbers ($n=20, 25, 27$) the expansion was not brought to prime factors, despite the mistakes he made (for $n=22, 24, 26$), today we can only bow to Ghirant’s work in calculating the prime factors of these huge numbers. It can be assumed that the author of the table was motivated not only by the scientist’s research spirit, but also by the genuine aesthetic passion of the artist, who was drawn into the amazingly attractive world of this mysterious string of units. I. Bernoulli reflects his doubts about the correctness of the expansion in some cases with asterisks.

During the first hundred years that have passed since the publication of I. Bernoulli's table, no particular clarity was introduced into it. In 1838, Westerberg factorized a number of 11 units - and that was all. In 1879, French mathematician Édouard Lucas found prime factors for $n=17$ and recognized that the 19-unit integer could not be decomposed. In 1895, his book “Entertaining Arithmetic” was published in Paris, containing the table below.

Interest in numbers composed of units, which had been waning, has increased again in recent years, especially in connection with the development of the theory of arithmetic codes, which serves as the basis for the implementation of noise-immune coding methods in computer technology (see, for example, the book by Yu. G. Dadaev “The Theory of Arithmetic Codes,” published in Moscow in 1981). Our mysterious numbers, almost two centuries after the publication of the first table of their divisors, are finally acquiring their own name. In “Entertaining Number Theory” (New York, 1964), its author A. Beiler, devoting an entire chapter to this number entitled “111...1111”, introduces the term “repunit” for them (abbreviation of English repeated unit - repeated unit). The Russian word “repunit” is not yet found in dictionaries, but it is already appearing in abstracts of foreign articles, acquiring the force of a new international term.
