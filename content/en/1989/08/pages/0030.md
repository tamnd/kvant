The case of a difficult denominator

Since every natural number is a product of a power of primes that are mutually prime, the last statement applies the problem of calculating the length of a period to the case when the denominator is a power of a prime number. Cool shift effect

Let us remind you what it consists of. We have seen that the six-digit period of 1/7, when multiplied by 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, undergoes a drastic rearrangement: a number of digits from the end of the number move to the beginning. The six-digit period dobi 1/13 behaves somewhat differently when multiplied by various numbers; namely, ... However, what exactly happens to him, the reader can remember by looking at the beginning of the article, and we will now prove a theorem that more or less explains this phenomenon.

Theorem 3. Let N be the period of 1/m (written as a number, possibly starting with one or more zeros), where m is coprime to 10, and let l be the remainder when dividing the number 10^k

on m. Then the number lN is obtained from the number N by rearranging k digits from the beginning of the number to the end.

Proof. Let M be the integer part of the number 10^k/m, i.e. 10^k = Mm+l. Multiply the decimal fraction 1/m by 10^k; in this case the comma will move k positions to the left. The integer part of the resulting number is M. Let's discard the integer part. The result is a number

$$10^k \frac{1}{m} - M = \frac{10^k - Mm}{m} = \frac{l}{m}.$$

This is a periodic decimal fraction, the period of which is obtained from the period dobi 1/m by abrupt rearrangement of digits: k digits move from beginning to end; but at the same time, this number is l times greater than the number 1/m, and therefore its period is l times greater than the period of the number 1/m, i.e. N. The theorem is proven.

If the number 1/m has an (m-1)-digit period, then the proven theorem is all explained. Indeed, by abrupt permutations of numbers from a period, you can get m-1 numbers (including himself), and all these numbers are different. On the other hand, multiplying period by 1, 2, ..., m-1, we also get m-1 numbers; this means they are exactly the same numbers. If the period is shorter, then abrupt permutations of the numbers of the period N do not exhaust all numbers of the form lN with 1≤l≤m-1. All that can be said in this case is that a sharp rearrangement of the digits of the number lN always leads to a number of the form l'N - this is proven in exactly the same way as Theorem 3.

Interestingly, Theorem 3 is explained in some areas:

Suppose that when crossing k digits from the end of the number N to the beginning, it turns into the number lN, where l is an integer.
