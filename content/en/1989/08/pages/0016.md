Dear readers, I believe you have long agreed that almost everything said is a joke. The great alchemist and astrologer Denogardus is a fictional person. The founder of the theory of differential equations is Isaac Newton*), who, by the way, also defended his discovery in the form of anagram signs: “It is useful to solve differential equations.” True, he did this for a different reason than Denogardus - Newoton was afraid of competition.

The gravity between the planet and the Sun is inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. To be fair, we note that the motion of each planet separately can be described by Hooke’s law $F = mk(t)\vec{r}(t)$, but the coefficient $k(t)$ is different for each planet. In general, Newton's and Hooke's laws are, in some senses, dual to each other. For example, the trajectories of bodies that obey Hooke's law with a constant coefficient $k$ will be second-order curves (ellipses, hyperbolas or parabolas), as well as the trajectories of ordinary celestial bodies that obey Newoton's law. Moreover, if the falling body continues to move inside the Earth, obeying only the gravity force, its movement will be described by Hooke’s law.

The relationship between Newton and Hooke was as complicated as between the corresponding laws. Hooke was the first to announce the existence of the “inverse square” law of the treaty, which he reported in a letter to Newton, who was able to use this law to explain Kepler’s observations. For some time, priority disputes continued between Hooke and Newot. You can read about this and many other interesting things in the book by V. I.
