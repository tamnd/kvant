Exercise 2. Solve the equation $x'' = x$. Hint: Consider the function $x = e^t$ and $x = e^{-t}$.

Possessing primitive means of observation, Denogardus could only change the angles between celestial bodies, but not their distance to the Sun. Nevertheless, the astrologer managed to find the Great Number $k(t)$ based only on the direction of the ray connecting the Sun and the comet. The answer to the question of how this can be done is contained in the modern proof of Denogardus's Great Theorem.

This proof is based on simple observation.

Exercise 3. Prove that for any two solutions of the equation $x''(t) = k(t)x(t)$ the quantity $W(x_1, x_2) = x_1x_2' - x_2x_1'$ does not depend on the time $t$. Hint: find an arbitrary $W$ in time and using equation (3).

To those who have worked out this equation, it may seem that success is the result of simple taking. However, the equation about the constancy of $W(x_1, x_2)$ has a physical meaning. Let us consider the motion of a body on a plane with acceleration $\vec{a}(t) = k(t)\vec{r}(t)$. The moment of its implus is constructed since the force acting on the body is always directed towards the center. The moment of the implus is equal to the area of ​​the parallelogram constructed on the vectors $\vec{r}$ and $m\vec{v}$. Show that this area is $m(x_1v_2 - x_2v_1)$. Since $x_2' = v_2$ and $x_1' = v_1$, the constancy of the moment of implus is equivalent to the constancy of the magnitude of $W(x_1, x_2)$.

So, the Great Theorem states that from the direction of the radius vector $\vec{r}(t)$ one can determine the coefficient $k(t)$. We will set the direction $\vec{r}(t)$ of the value tg $\varphi(t)$, where $\varphi(t)$ is the angle between the vector $\vec{r}(t)$ and the abscissa axis. Let us denote the function Тg $\varphi(t)$ by $f(t)$.

Exercise 4. Prove that $k(t) = -\frac{1}{2}\left(f'''/f' - \frac{3}{2}(f'''/f')^2\right)$ (Great Formula of Denogardus).

Hint: By definition of tangent $x_2(t) = f(t)x_1(t)$. Let's substitute the value of $x_2(t)$ into the formula for $W(x_1, x_2)$. After the equation we get the following formula: $W = x_1^2f'$. This means that the first coordinate of the body $x_1(t) = \sqrt{W/f}$ is determined up to a constant factor $W$. From equation $x_1'' = k(t)x_1$ we find $k(t) = x_1''/x_1$. What follows is a useful equation for taking an arbitrary function of a complex function (see «Kvant» No. 4 for 1988, p. 36).

It remains a mystery how Denogardus, without knowing differential calculus, could determine his Great Number.
