The Amazing Adventures of Periodic Processes

1. Proof of the lemma. For each natural number $n$ not divided by $q$, we calculate the remainder when dividing the number $np$ by $q$. Let $r$ be the smallest of these remainders; in part, $Ap = Bq + r$ with some $A$ and $B$. We need to show that $r = 1$. Assume $r > 1$, and divide $q$ by $r$ with remainder: $q = mr + r'$, $r' < r$ (and $r' > 0$, or otherwise $p$ and $q$ would both be divisible by $r$ and would not be coprime). Then $A(m+1)p = B(m+1)q + mr + r = B(m+1)q + q + (r - r')$, which shows that the remainder when dividing the number $A(m+1)p$ by $q$ is $r - r' < r$, which contradicts the minimality of the remainder $r$. The lemma is proven.

Note that in the proven lemma, $A$ is necessarily coprime with $q$, and $B$ is coprime with $p$.

Derivation of the statement from the lemma. Let $2^{a}5^{b}A = Bm' + 1$. Then

$$\frac{1}{m} = \frac{2^{a}5^{b}A - Bm'}{2^{a}5^{b}m'} = \frac{A}{m'} - \frac{B}{2^{a}5^{b}}.$$

In the last difference, the minuend is a decimal fraction having a period the same length as $1/m'$ (see Exercise 2), and the subtrahend is a finite decimal with $c$ decimal places (it is equal to $2^{c-a}B/10^c$ or $5^{c-b}B/10^c$). The statement immediately follows from this.

2. By multiplying the decimal fraction $1/q$ by $p$ its period would only decrease. Let's assume it has decreased. Let $A$ and $B$ be such that $Ap = Bq + 1$. When multiplying the fraction $p/q$ by $A$ the period can only decrease further. But in this case, the multiplication results in $(Bq+1)/q = B+(1/q)$ - the period has the same length as that of the original fraction. Contradiction.

3. The fraction can be considered irreducible. If $q$ is not divisible by 2 or 5, then this directly follows from the corollary proved in the article (and, if it is more specific, exercise 2). If it is divisible, then divide $q$ into all possible twos and fives; let it turn out $q'$. By virtue of the formula proven above in solving Exercise 1, our fraction has the same period as some fraction with the value $q'$.

4. Let $n_1$ be the smallest number of plots that make up a number divisible by $p_1$, and $n_2$ be the smallest number of plots that make up a number divisible by $p_2$. Let, further, $n$ be the least common multiple of the numbers $n_1$ and $n_2$. We must prove that, firstly, a number of $n$ plots is divisible by $p_1p_2$ and, secondly, no number composed of a smaller number of plots is divisible by $p_1p_2$. But the first is very clear, and the second is proven in exactly the same way as Theorem 2 of the article.

6. Let $p$ be a half-period prime. The period of the fraction $1/p$ is equal to $(10^{p-1}-1)/p$. Let's square it and separate the last $p-1$ digits:

$$\left(\frac{10^{p-1}-1}{p}\right)^2 = A \cdot 10^{p-1} + B, B < 10^{p-1}.$$

We have

$$A + B = \frac{10^{p-1}-1}{p} \left(\frac{10^{p-1}-1}{p} - Ap\right).$$

Thus, $A + B$ is divided by the period of the fraction $1/p$. It is easy to understand that the quotient does not exceed $p$ (we leave the proof to the reader), and it is equal to $p$ if and only if the period of the fraction $1/p$ is itself divisible by $p$; whether this happens for half-period $p$, we don’t know. In this case, $A + B$ is equal to LOB with $10^{p-1}-1$. If the quotient is less than $p$, then $A + B$ is obtained from the period by circular rearrangement of digits.

Outfit

The number can be equal to the sum of the geometric progression:

$$n = 1 + 2 + 4 + 8 + ... + 2^{17}.$$

To find $n$, we write:

$$2n = 2 + 4 + 8 + ... + 2^{17} + 2^{18}.$$

Subtracting the first equality from the second, we get

$$n = 2^{18} - 1 = 262143.$$
