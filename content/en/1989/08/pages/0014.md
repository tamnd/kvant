This is a complex mathematical theorem, because it is not even clear why there is at least one solution. However, Denogardus understood it well and almost proved the following by elegant reasoning. Each planet will move somehow, which means its coordinates immediately give a pair of solutions to equation (3) (!). If we assume the existence of a third solution, independently of these two, then we immediately encounter a contradiction. In fact, a point in space, all three coordinates of which change as solutions to equation (3), must move in one plane (!!!).

To get a little clearer with equation (3), we suggest you understand its simplest special case, in which the coefficient $k(t)$ does not depend on time.

Exercise 1. Solve the equation $x''=-x$. Hint: Consider a point on a plane moving uniformly around a circle.

The second and third scholi of Denogardus take on the meaning of satisfaction theorems that describe the properties of solutions to the differential equation (3).

Between two values ​​of time $t_1$ and $t_2$, in which one solution of the equation $x''=k(t)x$ vanishes, any other solution simply must vanish at least once.

In other words, the zeros of the two solutions $x_1(t)$ and $x_2(t)$ are intersected.

To derive this theorem from the second schora, let us consider a point on the plane of motion whose coordinates depend on time and are given by the functions $x_1(t)$ and $x_2(t)$. Then the movement of this point will satisfy equation (3), and therefore (2), i.e., it will obey the second scholi of Denogardus. Let us choose the positive half-subciss as the ray $OA$, and the positive one as the ray $OB$. When a point crosses one axis, its projection onto the other axis (i.e., the second solution) is zero. Since the moment of intersection of the axes must pass through, the zeros of the solutions $x_1(t)$ and $x_2(t)$ also pass through.

But how does the second schola prove it? Its simple geometric proof can be read directly from the drawing from Denogardus's treatise (see Fig. 1). If a comet crosses ray $OA$ twice without intersecting ray $OB$, then at some intermediate instant its trajectory touches some ray $OC$. At this moment, both the speed of the comet and the directional force acting on it are along the straight line $OC$. Consequently, there is no force tending to move the comet off this line—the comet remains on line $OC$. But then it will no longer intersect the ray $OA$, which contradicts the original assumption. This elegant argument shows how fluent Denogardus was in geometry.
