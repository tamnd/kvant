PHYSICS ON A MOUNTAIN RIVER

Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
I. F. GINZBURG

I have long wanted to write an article for «Kvant» about how to do scientific work. However, the description of the subject of one's own research requires too cumbersome mathematical apparatus, not enough for schoolchildren, and it seemed that the event did not exist with my desire. But recently I was faced with two tasks related to my hobby - rafting on mountain rivers. Having solved them, I realized that these are excellent models of real scientific work, and the description of their solution can be interesting. I will try to reconstruct the course of the decision, starting from the formulation of the question and the motives that aroused my interest, and to the stage when I thought about how to present the results so that they would be understandable to a nearby tourist who is familiar with physics in the volume of a crammed high school course.

But first, how we swim.

Nowadays there are almost no tourists swimming on real wooden platforms. Many tourists go by kayaks; the load capacity of a typical double kayak is at least 300 kg. Most tourists float along mountain rivers on platforms or catamarans with inflatable elements - automatic tubes (for rafts) or specially glued gondolas made of rubberized fabric. A wooden (sometimes metal) frame weighing up to 150-200 kg is manufactured on site. Typically, per person there is at least 200 liters of air volume in gondolas or chambers. Thus, the reserve of buoyancy (the ratio of the maximum lifting force to the weight of the vessel with rowers) is never less than 1.5. This allows you to confidently maneuver around water and rocks. A necessary piece of personal equipment is a life jacket with a volume of 16-30 liters.

So, first question.

Average density of water after draining

Tourists are “well aware” that after passing the rapids, immediately after the discharge, the ship “sags.” The fact is that in the threshold the water is mixed with air, and after draining (Fig. 1) air bubbles reduce the average density of the water.

I knew this statement and repeatedly repeated it to beginning “raftsmen.” But one day I wanted to be more specific in this matter, and I tried to estimate the average density of water after draining $\rho_{cp}$. Intuition suggested that in reality the role of bubbles is much less than it is thought. And if so, it is enough to estimate $\rho_{cp}$ in sizu, i.e., obtain a name for the value $\rho_{cp}$ that is compatible with observation. But which observations to use?

I remembered that usually after draining, flashes are actually visible in the water, and at the same time, at not very deep depths, the stones at the bottom are completely absent (if there is no turbidity in the water). This was enough to use one idea well known from the physics of gases and liquids.
