It is natural to assume that inside the film the speed of movement of the liquid is not the same: at the surface of the wing it is zero - here the liquid “sticks” to the body, and as it moves away from the surface the speed increases. This is due to the presence of gravity forces in a moving fluid: on each thin layer of liquid, from the side of the lower adjacent layer, closer to the wing, a force directed against the flow acts, and from the side of the upper layer, a force directed along the flow. If the fluid density is small, then the speed, equal to zero on the surface of the wing, already at a short distance from it reaches a constant value $v_0$. In other words, the gravity forces affect the thin boundary layer adjacent to the surface of the wing. We will assume that this is exactly the situation that occurs in our case.

— The answer to this question was given by Isaac Newton in his landmark book “Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy”: the friction stress is determined by the derivative of the speed $v(y)$ along the normal to the surface $y$, i.e. $dv/dy$. And this dependence is directly proportional:

$$\tau = \mu \frac{dv}{dy},$$

where the coefficient of proportional $\mu$ is called the diametrical coefficient of thickness. Newton's law turned out to be “viable” in a wide range of variable quantities. Environments subordinate to it are called Newotnovian. These include those media that interest us: air and water.
