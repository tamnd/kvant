Indeed, if the tension of a person on the ship’s rope is $F_\gamma$, then the tension of the transverse rope is $F_\pi = F_\gamma/2 \sin \alpha$, where $\alpha$ is the angle between the direction of the stretched rope and its original direction (Fig. 3). Some conclusions

Let us now discuss what was common in both examples and the scheme for their solution.

The first stage is a question that is not very clearly posed. (What is the average density of water after the threshold? What kind of rope can you use?)

The second stage is understanding the situations and setting the problem (with partial guessing of the answer): to show that the change in the average density of water after the threshold is not small; determine which rope should be used for forced mooring. This also includes a preliminary assessment of the error of the subsequent solution.

The third stage is collecting and organizing information; assessment of basic parameters. In our examples, the knowledge I already had was enough. In general, at this stage additional, auxiliary experiments may be required. A very important step at this stage was to generate using excess data (for example, $S$ and $\alpha$ in the formula for drag force).

This allowed, by presenting the answer, to get rid of the definition of a large number of unnecessary intermediate quantities, replaced by one easy estimated quantity (in our case, $l_{\alpha}$).

And only after this is the fourth stage performed - solving the problem, i.e. obtaining formulas and then numbers. Without numbers, without knowing the correspondence of the parameters, it is impossible to determine whether the correct solution was obtained, or whether the implementation of the solution is determined in this way by completely different reasons. The question of how to present the results obtained is also important, or the solution to a real problem is directly addressed to some “consumer”.
