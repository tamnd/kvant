At first it hangs vertically on a thread, and as its oscillations end, it turns into a completely regular ring, lying entirely in a horizontal plane. This experiment also provides a clear example of apparent hardness, which imparts rapid movement to a flexible body.

Although the tendency of the top to assume a vertical position has been well known since it was launched for the first time, I still asked everyone in this room if they knew the explanation for this phenomenon, and I even doubt that there are many people anywhere who know it. Any serious mathematician will tell you that the explanation can probably be found in print from Routh (the author of a very famous English textbook on mechanics) or that he at least knows people in Cambridge who, without a doubt, know this explanation. Perhaps he also thinks that he himself once knew it, but that now he has forgotten those difficult mathematical proofs on which he previously refined his mind. This problem was actually solved by Sir William Thomson and Professor Blackburn when they spent a whole year on the seashore preparing for an examination in mathematics. Probably, anyone interested in Thomson's work will be perplexed to learn that this scientist and his friend spent their holidays on the seashore, engaged in making all kinds of round stones that he collected on the shore spin.

Now I will show you an amazing phenomenon that Thomson was puzzling over at that time. Let the ellipsoid (Fig. 9) be a stone polished with water. It lies on the table in a very stable position. I bring him into a fast spinning motion. You see that for a second or two it tends to rotate about the axis AA, but then begins to wobble violently; when these swings stop after some time, then it calmly rotates around the axis BB, which has now assumed a vertical position; but then a series of quickly subdued swings begins again; when they stop, you notice that the ellipsoid finally comes into a state of stable rotation, becomes vertical to the longest of its axes - CC.
