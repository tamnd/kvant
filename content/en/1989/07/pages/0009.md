But outside the concentration of protons was about this time higher, and energy was needed to push them out of the cell. It was this energy that BP began to capture. When a photon of sunlight is absorbed, the retinal tail straightens and “knocks” the proton out of the cell. But since “the holy place is not empty,” instead of $p^+$ a nutrient molecule enters the cell. The BP molecule can be compared to a seesaw, with a proton sitting at one end and a nutritional molecule at the other. These molecular swings operate using solar energy.

A sharp decrease in the intracellular concentration of $p^+$ led to the fact that, according to the laws of diffusion, disruptive protons rushed even more actively inside the cell. And the cell adapted this desire to its advantage: the energy of protons passing through the freedom membrane began to be used for the formation of ATP.

ATP stands for quite simply: Adenosine Triphosphoric acid. This is “its energetic height” of the cell, the accumulator of intracellular energy. All vital processes in living nature are driven by the energy of ATP. When an ATP molecule breaks down, enormous energy (on the scale of the cell) is released, which is used for various needs of the cell. Thus, halobacteria, these living “fossils” on Earth, which arose about three to four billion years ago, were able to successfully “harness” a photon into an energy chariot.

However, the flourishing of microorganisms containing BP in their membrane also predetermined their “decline” - they disappeared from the stage of evolution. After all, in order to build a new cell, carbon is needed - everything in our living world consists of a chain of atoms of this element.
