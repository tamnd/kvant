The volume of volume (1 l) is denoted by $n$. Then their total volume per unit volume is $\frac{4}{3} \pi r^3 n$, and, neglecting the mass of air, the average density of water can be considered equal to

$$\rho_{\text{cp}} = \rho_0 \left( 1 - \frac{4}{3} \pi r^3 n \right) (\text{кг/л}),$$

where $\rho_0$ is the density of “pure” water, without anthers.

Now let's return to the stones visible at the bottom. If anthers are encountered in the path of the light bow coming from the bottom, then through them we cannot see the stone clearly - the light is scattered on the dust. Let us estimate at what depth $l$ there will almost certainly be at least one dustnek on the path of the light bow. Let us select an area of ​​area $S$ at the bottom and consider the columnoids above it. Its volume is $lS$; it contains $N = nlS$ anthers, and they “block” an area of ​​no more than $S_1 = N \pi r^2 = nlS \pi r^2$ to the light. The path of light is completely “blocked” if this area $S_1$ coincides with $S$ (for simplicity, we assume that we are looking almost vertically down, and we neglect random overlaps, taking into account which unexistently changes the answer). Posibly, the depth $l_{\text{max}}$ of a clear form is determined from the condition $S_1 = S$, i.e.

$$l_{\text{max}} = 1 / (n \pi r^2).$$

Substituting this correspondence into the expression for $\rho_{\text{cp}}$, we obtain

$$\rho_{\text{cp}} = \rho_0 \left( 1 - \frac{4}{3} \frac{r}{l_{\text{max}}} \right).$$

This is the answer. Let us now substitute the numbers to understand what is behind this formula.

There are two varieties of plums. After some types of large anthers with $r \lesssim 0,5$ cm, and the depth of vision of the day is $l_{\text{max}} \gtrsim 0,5$ m. After others, the water of each milky white from very small anthers is with $r \lesssim 0,1$ cm, and the depth of vision of the day (more precisely, the object in the water) after such drains is $l_{\text{max}} \approx 5-10$ cm. Substituting these numbers into the formula for $\rho_{\text{cp}}$, we find in both cases $\rho_{\text{cp}} \gtrsim 0,98 \rho_0 = 0,98 \text{ кг/л}.$

From the formula for $\rho_{\text{cp}}$ it is clear that the smaller the size of the dust, the larger $\rho_{\text{cp}}$ to $\rho_0$. Therefore, from the point of view of our task, the assumption that all dust particles have the same large radius is completely reasonable.

So, for modern tourist ships with a large reserve of buoyancy, the difference between $\rho_{\text{cp}}$ and $\rho_0$ is insignificant, and the “folklore” statement with which I began - about the vessel sagging after passing the threshold - is unfair.

And we, using a thinner rope with a diameter of 6-8 mm, confidently rope at a speed of 3-4 m/s. It became clear to me that there was an error in the calculations in .state.
