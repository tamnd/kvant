So we finally get: in conditions of sufficiently heavy rain, the drag force acting on the wing increases by

$$\frac{\tau}{\tau_1}=\left(\frac{\varrho_1}{\varrho}\right)^{1/4}\left(\frac{\mu}{\mu_1}\right)^{1/2}$$ times.

Returning home, I looked in the reference book for the required densities and dynamic viscosity coefficients for air and water, plugged them into the formula and got $\tau/\tau_1\approx 1,5$. Thus, an “all-weather” aircraft must have a fifty percent thrust reserve compared to a conventional aircraft. This is why planes don't fly when it rains heavily.

This estimate was obtained under the assumption that a wing flowing around air has a near-wall boundary layer consisting of water. A tricky question immediately arises: what should be the thrust of the engines to move an aircraft in a continuous stream of water? The reader who has carefully studied the article will solve this problem on his own. The answer stated: to “transform” an aircraft into a submarine, it is necessary to increase the power of its movement

$$\frac{\tau}{\tau_1}=\left(\frac{\varrho_1}{\varrho}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{\mu}{\mu_1}\right)^{1/2}\approx 230$$ times.
