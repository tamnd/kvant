Look at the top that lies on a flat board and which I throw into the air (Fig. 5). You see that it is difficult to follow its movement, and no one could predict in advance, until it falls, in what position it will return back to the board: with the sharp end forward, backward or to the side. But if I wrap it up and now throw it into the air, there can be no doubt as to the position in which it will come back. The axis of rotation remains parallel to itself, and I can throw the top several times in a row without changing its rotational motion.

If I throw up a biscuit (Fig. 6), then I cannot know in advance how it will fall back; but if, before releasing it from my hands, I set it into rotation, then there will be no doubt about this. Here is the hat (Fig. 7); I throw it up and don’t know in what position it will fall back, but if I give it a rotation, then the axis around which it rotates, like the wolf and the biscuit, remains parallel to itself, and you can be sure that in this case the hat will fall to the ground with the brim down.

Let us now examine a careful property of the renewed top. It doesn't spin and you see it tip over immediately; if I stand it vertically on its spine, it turns out to be completely unstable. But now notice that when it spins, it not only maintains its vertical position, but even if I hit it and thus change its state, it begins to spin in a precessional motion, which gradually becomes smaller and smaller until the top reaches its vertical position. *) Here two important remarks should be made first of all.

The first observation we make is that the top will not tilt at the first moment in the direction of impact. If I direct the blow to the south, then the top will tilt to the west, but if I direct the blow to the west, then the top will tilt to the north.
