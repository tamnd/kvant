Let's leave the screwing at this for now and move on to the ship.

Vessel. When moving in water, the resistance force acting on the ship from the water plays an important role. It can be written in the form

$$F_c = \alpha \rho S u^2,$$

where $u$ is the speed of water relative to the vessel, $\rho$ is the density of water, $S$ is the cross-sectional area of ​​the part of the vessel immersed in water (the cross-section is perpendicular to the flow), $\alpha$ is a numerical coefficient depending on the circumference of the vessel ($\alpha \lesssim 0,5$). In reality, this coefficient also depends on speed, but this dependence is significant only on smooth water, and we will not take it into account.

At first I made estimates using this formula. But how reliable are they? After all, both the coefficient $\alpha$ and the area $S$ are known very poorly. And then I said that these two parameters can be combined into one, which I called the effective length of the vessel. According to Archimedes' law, the mass of the vessel is $M = \rho V$, where $V$ is the volume of the part of the vessel immersed in water, approximately equal to the product of $S$ and the length of the vessel along the waterline $l$. I called the effective length of the vessel $l_{\alpha}$ the values ​​$l / \alpha$. Then

$$F_c = \frac{M u^2}{l_{\alpha}}.$$

At first I rated $l_{\alpha}$ quite harshly. Since $\alpha \lesssim 0,5$ and the length of the vessel $l$ are usually at least 4 m, then $l_{\alpha} \gtrsim 8$ m. But then I realized that $l_{\alpha}$ can be changed.

Let's accelerate the ship in still water to a certain speed $v_0$, and then lower it. Its further movement under the influence of resistance force is described by Newton’s second law:

$$Ma = - \frac{M v^2}{l_{\alpha}}.$$

In this case, the change in the kinetic energy of the vessel along the path $\Delta x$ is equal to the work of the drag force on this path, i.e.

$$\Delta \left( \frac{M v^2}{2} \right) = - \left( \frac{M v^2}{l_{\alpha}} \right) \Delta x,$$

or

$$\Delta \left( \frac{v^2}{2} \right) = - \frac{v^2}{l_{\alpha}} \Delta x.$$

We will now look for speed depending not on time, but on the distance traveled $x$. From the energy equation it is clear that the ratio $v / v_0$ depends only on $x / l_{\alpha}$, i.e. $v / v_0 = f(x / l_{\alpha})$. A neat solution gives

$$v / v_0 = e^{-x / l_{\alpha}} \approx (2,7)^{-x / l_{\alpha}}.$$

*) For those familiar with differentiation, note that this relationship is a solution to the energy balance equation, which can be rewritten as $dv / v = -dx / l_{\alpha}$.
