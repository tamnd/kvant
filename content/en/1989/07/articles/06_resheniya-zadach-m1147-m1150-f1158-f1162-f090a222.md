M1148. Prove that for any $a > 1$, $a \neq \sqrt{q}$ ($p$ and $q$ are integers) and natural $n$ the equality $[\log_a 2] + [\log_a 3] + \dots + [\log_a n] + [a] + [a^2] + \dots + [a^k] = nk$ holds, where $k = [\log_a n]$ ($[x]$ is the integer part of $x$).

For any positive $z$, the number $[z]$ is equal to the number of natural numbers not exceeding $z$, so $[\log_a m]$ is the number of points of the form $(m; y)$, where $y$ is a natural number not exceeding $\log_a m$, and the sum $[\log_a 2] + [\log_a 3] + \dots + [\log_a n]$ is the number of points with natural coordinates lying below straight $y = k$ and to the left of the graph $y = \log_a x$. These two sets of points do not intersect, since by condition there are no integer points on the graph itself (with the exception of $(1; 0)$). And the union of these sets obviously gives all the integer points $(x; y)$ in the right angle $1 \leqslant x \leqslant n$, $1 \leqslant y \leqslant k$, the number of which is $kn$.

R. V. Alekseev, L. D. Kurlyandchik

F1159. Imagine that you are in a train moving in a direction and walking at a constant relative speed forward along the train. A very significant force is pushing you back, and you are undoubtedly doing work against this force. What is your work spent on? Apparently, she cannot go to an additional stop on the train - after all, you are pushing her back. What's the matter here? Is the law of conservation of energy violated?

The energy costs of an electric train, when a person sits on a seat or when he walks at a constant relative speed, are the same. Indeed, in both cases the human accelerations are the same, therefore the forces acting on the electric train from the human side are also the same. This means that a person works “for himself”. Let's check this assumption.

Let $a$ be the acceleration of the train. During the time $\Delta t$, during which the speed of the train changes from $v$ to $v + a \Delta t$, energy is imparted to a sitting person with mass $m$

$$E_1 = \frac{m(v + a \Delta t)^2}{2} - \frac{mv^2}{2} = \frac{m}{2}((v + a \Delta t)^2 - v^2).$$

If a person moves forward with a speed $u$ relative to the base, then during the same time his kinetic energy should increase by amounts

$$E_2 = \frac{m(v + a \Delta t + u)^2}{2} - \frac{m(v + u)^2}{2} = \frac{m}{2}((v + a \Delta t)^2 - v^2) + mua \Delta t.$$

The first term in this expression “provides” the work of the electric train’s engines (the same as in the first case), and the additional energy $mu \Delta t$ arises precisely due to the work of human muscles. In fact, the force against which a person performs work is equal to $ma$; in time $\Delta t$ a person will travel $u \Delta t$ along the carriage; therefore, it will do work equal to $ma \Delta t$.

Thus, the work of the muscle in the cold actually provides greater changes in a person’s kinetic energy than when he is sitting. Therefore, this time everything is in order with the law of conservation of energy.

A. I. Korotkov

F1160. A cylinder of radius R and height H contains N electrons. The constructed field is applied parallel to the cylinder axis by induction B. Assuming that all electrons have the same velocity v, lying in a plane perpendicular to the magnetic field, and the impacts of electrons on the walls of the cylinder are absolutely more elastic, estimate what is equal to and how the pressure on the walls of the cylinder, which creates such an “electron gas”, depends on the magnetic field (the pressure can be found accurate to the constructed coefficient, does not depend on the magnetic field). Electron charge e, mass m. Assume that (mv/eB)≪R (“strong” field B). Neglect the interaction of electrons with each other.

In a magnetic field, all electrons will move along arcs of circles with a radius

$$r = \frac{mv}{eB} \ll R.$$

Only those electrons whose orbital center is located from the walls at a distance h<r (see figure) will collide with the walls of the cylinder. With each collision, the electron transfers an impulse to the wall

$$\Delta P = 2mv \sin \alpha,$$

and these collisions will occur with an interval in time

$$\Delta t = \frac{2(\pi - \alpha)r}{v}.$$

To estimate the pressure of the “electron gas” on the cylinder walls, we will assume that each electron acts on the wall with a force

$$f = \frac{\Delta P}{\Delta t} = \frac{mv^2 \sin \alpha}{(\pi - \alpha)r}.$$

The number of electrons colliding with a small section of the wall with area S is equal to

$$z = 2rSn = 2rS \frac{N}{\pi R^2H}$$

(here n is the electron density in the cylinder).

Then the desired pressure of the “electron gas” can be represented in the form

$$p = \frac{zf_{cp}}{S} = 2rnf_{cp} = 2rn \frac{mv^2}{r} \left( \frac{\sin \alpha}{\pi - \alpha} \right)_{cp} = 2nmv^2C,$$

where f_{cp} is the force with which the electron acts on the wall, accelerated by the position of the center of its orbit, and C=(sin α/(π-α))_{cp} is some constant number that does not depend on the magnetic field.

The final pressure of the “electron gas” on the cylinder walls (up to the construction coefficient) is equal to

$$p = 2Cnmv^2 = \frac{2CNmv^2}{\pi R^2H}$$

and does not depend on the magnetic field.

If at point A there is a non-directional emitter of a sufficiently loud sound, then this sound can get to point B in many ways. The shortest of them is straight from A to B. The signal traveling this way will arrive first, and it will take time

F1162. Underwater at a depth h there is an accurate light source. Where is the image of this source for an observer looking at the surface of the water (Fig. 1)? Refractive index of water n.

An observer looking at the surface of water with water examines the image of the source in rays that, after refraction at the water-air boundary, form very small angles from the surface of the water.

Let's consider a ray that makes a small angle $\Delta\varphi$ with the horizon and bounds the chosen ray of rays (Fig. 2). Let us denote by $A$ the point of exit from the water of this boundary ray, and by $B$ the point of contact with the surface of the water of the ray that has experienced complete reflection, and find the distance between these points:

$$AB = h(tg\alpha_0 - tg(\alpha_0 - \Delta\alpha)).$$

Here $\alpha_0$ is the limiting angle of total reflection $(\sin\alpha_0 = 1/n)$, and $(\alpha_0 - \Delta\alpha)$ is the angle of incidence on the surface of the water of the ray, which, after refraction, forms an angle $\Delta\varphi$ with it.

According to the law of light refraction,

$$\frac{\sin(\alpha_0 - \Delta\alpha)}{\sin(\pi/2 - \Delta\varphi)} = \frac{1}{n},$$

or, given that $\sin\alpha_0 = 1/n$,

$$\cos\Delta\alpha - \cos\Delta\varphi = n \cos\alpha_0 \cdot \sin\Delta\alpha.$$

For small angles $\Delta\alpha$ and $\Delta\varphi$ the following relation is valid:

$$\cos\Delta\alpha = 1 - 2\sin^2(\Delta\alpha/2) = 1 - (\Delta\alpha)^2/2,$$

$$\cos\Delta\varphi = 1 - (\Delta\varphi)^2/2.$$

From here we obtain a quadratic equation for the angle $\Delta\alpha$:

$$(\Delta\alpha)^2 + 2n \cos\alpha_0 \cdot \Delta\alpha - (\Delta\varphi)^2 = 0.$$

Considering that $(\Delta\alpha)^2 \ll \Delta\alpha$, we find

$$\Delta\alpha = \frac{(\Delta\varphi)^2}{2n \cos\alpha_0} = \frac{(\Delta\varphi)^2}{2\sqrt{n^2 - 1}}.$$

Then

$$AB = h \frac{\sin\Delta\alpha}{\cos\alpha_0 \cdot \cos(\alpha_0 - \Delta\alpha)} \approx h \frac{\Delta\alpha}{\cos^2\alpha_0} \ll h \tg\alpha_0.$$

Thus, for parastrong rays (i.e. rays for which $\Delta\varphi \ll 1$), the image of the source appears at the point of incidence of the ray that experienced total reflection (at point $B$).
