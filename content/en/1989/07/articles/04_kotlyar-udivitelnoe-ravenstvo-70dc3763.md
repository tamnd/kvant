AMAZING EQUALITY

V. D. KOTLYAR

Perhaps a coincidence... What is the value of l - the ratio of the circumference of a circle to its diameter? Reader «Kvant» has the right to be offended by such a question. Of course l ≈ 3.14; it is possible and more precise: l ≈ 3.14159; it can be even more precise... This means π² ≈ 9.87. What is g, the acceleration due to gravity, equal to? Who doesn’t know this - whoever studied physics will say that g ≈ 9.81 m/s². Means:

"So what? - the reader will shrug his shoulders. - You never know how many coincidences there are in the world! And it’s not a real coincidence: the difference between π² and g is not so small - about 0.06.”

“There’s nothing to talk about here at all,” another reader will enter into the conversation, “the equality π² ≈ g itself does not make any sense, because on the left side there is a number, and on the right side there are quantities that have dimension. If you change the distance not in meters, but, for example, in centimeters, then g ≈ 981 cm/s². And in England at all: g ≈ 32.2 ft/s². So there is nothing promised between π² and g!”

And yet, let's try to figure out what the nature of the guessing equality π² ≈ g is. To do this, as the second reader usually noted, you need to understand in what units physical quantities are measured.

How to change the length? From ancient times to the present day, people have had to make measurements - determine length, time, weight, speed and other quantities. And the question of uniform measurements has long been raised. Everyone knows, of course, that in the International System of Units (SI) the main units are length - meter, mass - kilogram, time - seconds. The people of Edine are good, we are used to them, we cannot imagine our daily life without them. When we come to the store, we ask you to answer 2 kilograms of steepness, when we go out onto the path of the herd, we run 100 meters, from bell to bell in the lesson 45 minutes pass, or 2700 seconds. We don’t have time; we need a scale to determine the mass; we measure the distance with a graduated ruler or tape measure. What if you don’t have a tape measure at hand? How to change the length in this case? It would be nice if the unit of measurement was always at hand; I wanted to and made my own “ethailon”. That's how it goes with measuring angles! To open an angle of 42°, we use a transor, but the standard of “1 degree” angles is not needed. If tomorrow all the angles and transorts disappeared on Earth, we could easily restore this unit by simply dividing a full angle into 360 equal parts. It is desirable for other quantities to have reproducible units of measurement. When the first systems of measures spontaneously arose, this reproducibility was built into them from the very beginning - units of length were associated, for example, with the sizes of individual parts of the human body. This is how “fathom” arose - the distance between the ends of the outstretched arms of an adult (“measured fathom” ≈ 176 cm) and “elbow”, the name of which speaks for itself - it is equal to the distance from the elbow to the outstretched fingers. From the English word “foot” - “step” - the unit of length “foot”, which we have already mentioned, originates

When traveling to India around Africa, an important task was to determine the location of the ship at sea as accurately as possible. Some scientists had a wonderful idea - to create a unit of length without an eton! Find such a unity for all quantities that can be reproduced anywhere and at any time with the smallest possible number of simple materials and devices! Make this unit common to different countries and peoples! One of the scientists who proposed a new unit of length was the great Dutch physicist, mechanic and mathematician Christ IAN Huygens.

His proposal is this: Let's make a mathematical device, that is, hang a small heavy body on a thread. Let us select the length of the thread l so that the mathematical machine makes one oscillation every 2 seconds. (This means that if a body moves away from the equilibrium position and releases it, then after 1 second it will take a symmetrical position, and after another second it will return to its original position.) So, Huygens proposed taking the length of the thread l as a unit of length.

Digression on mathematical mathematics and ChristIAN Huygens. A mathematical mathematics machine is a material point suspended on a non-weighty, inextensible thread. Mathematical mathematics is an abstraction, because in

in the real world there are neither material points nor weightless threads. But this abstraction sufficiently describes the properties of real objects.

In the 10th grade physics course, a wonderful formula is derived

$$T = 2\pi \sqrt{l/g},$$

expressing the period of small oscillations of the mathematical matrix $T$ through the length of the thread $l$. This formula cannot but be connected with our mysterious equality $\pi^2 \approx g$, because the same characters participate in it: $\pi$ and $g$...

Suppose that Goygenas's proposal to choose a unit of length were accepted. Then for length $l=1$ the period is $T=2$. Substituting these values ​​into the formula, we get: $2 = 2\pi \sqrt{1/g}$, or $\pi^2=g$. Our mysterious equality turns out to be exactly true, and true by definition of units of length! But Goygenas's proposal was answered and the unit of length was established differently. How this happened and how to explain the equality $\pi^2 \approx g$, we will discuss in the next section. And now a few words about Christin Geugens himself.

He was born on April 14, 1629 in the family of the famous Dutch politician Constantine Goygenas. The times were stormy - only recently the Netherlands had freed themselves from the rule of the Catholic Church and the Spanish king. Young Christan received a good education at home, and in 1645 he entered Leiden University. After completing his education, he engaged in scientific work, first in Holland and then at the Paris Academy of Sciences. At the end of his life, Geugens returned to his native Holland. He dies on July 8, 1695.

And during these decades there has been a real downpour of work, results, and discoveries. Many of them are genuine masterpieces that have become great science. Geugens discovered the ring of Saturn, developed the theory of impact of elastic bodies (see «Kvant» No. 9 for 1988), deducing the lens formula, proposed the wave theory of light, introduced and studied the development of curves - involutes. Geugens was not only a scientist, but also a remarkable craftsman. He polished lenses, manufactured and designed the largest telexcopes of his time, and a planetarium was built under his leadership. And his entire life was interested in watches. In 1673, Goygenas's book “Pendulum Clocks” was published. This work contains the results obtained by the scientist over many years. The idea of ​​a matrix clock belonged to Galileo, but Geugens brought this idea to practical implementation - he made a clock in which successive equal periods of time were measured by vibrations of the matrix. It was Heugens who discovered the formula for the period of oscillations of a matrix with a small amplitude (for oscillations with a large size, the period $T$ already depends on the amplitude).

And finally the meter appeared... But let's return to the unit of length proposed by Heugens. Its disadvantage turned out to be that the acceleration of gravity $g$ varies on Earth from place to place. While still in Paris, Geugens met Richet, a young employee of the Academy of Sciences, hired to help academicians in experiments and observations. It was Richet who subsequently discovered the dependence of the length $l$ of the second matrix on geographic latitude—in fact, this means that $g$ depends on the width. There are several reasons for the change $g$. Firstly, $g$ changes with latitude due to the fact that the distance from a point on the Earth’s surface to its axis of rotation changes and, therefore, the magnitude of the centripetal acceleration of the body changes. Secondly, the Earth is not a perfect sphere, but a body slightly flattened at the stripes. Because of this, the force of attraction of the body by the Earth changes. Thirdly, the globe is under-exposed; in some places there are layers of more massive rocks. Therefore, a universal unit of length cannot be created on this basis. Still, in the 18th century, the length of the second matrix (for some

What was to be done? In 1789, the Great French Revolution took place. In the same year, the Estates General—the name of the French parliament at that time—received several draft reforms of the system of measures. Before the National Assembly - the revolutionary parliament - there is only one project, presented by Bishop Prince Talleyrand, a future important French diplomat. Talleyrand proposed choosing the length of the second matrix at latitude $45^\circ$ as the unit of length. The final draft of the reform should be developed by two special commissions. One of them - led by the great mathematician and mechanic Lagrange - proposed decimal ratios for units of measurement. The other - led by the remarkable mathematician, mechanic and astronomer Laplace - began to search for a reliable and reproducible unit of length, as well as other units of measurement. Two projects were put before the commission. One of them is the Talleyrand project. The second is the project of Laplace himself, who, based on his interest in the structure of the Earth and cosmology, wanted the size of our planet to be expressed in round numbers (i.e., to be multiples of large powers of ten).

The commission decided to accept Laplace's proposal, but at the same time, as it arose, to take into account Talleyrand's proposal. To do this, 1/10,000,000 parts of the four wig meridian were taken as units of length - this value is approximately equal to the length of the second matrix, differing from it by only 0.6%. Thus, the decision was made, as it were, not to fully respond to the Huygen-Talleyrand project, but only to somewhat “duck” it. The decision made - to take $10^{-7}$ of the four meridians as “1 meter” - was apparently influenced by one more circumstance. It was assumed (and it was decided, but, unfortunately, it did not take root) a broken right angle of 100 degrees - instead of the usual 90 degree division. In this case, 1 degree on the earth’s surface along the meridian would be $10^5$ m=100 km. On March 26, 1791, the National Assembly approved the proposals of the commission, and on April 7, 1799, the very value of the “meter” (for now, up to a more accurate measurement of the length of the meridian).

The idea of ​​​​creating a unit of length, for which an etapon is not needed, would be realized: an accurate “meter” is needed - a measured and manufactured model of the “meter”. At the same time, the length of the meter was equal to the length of the second matrix only approximately, so the equality $\pi^2 \approx g$ also became approximate. Here is the answer to the question we posed at the beginning of the article!

In conclusion, two words about further developments. Of course, the length of the four meridians turned out to be equal to not exactly $10^7$ meters (according to measurements in 1976 - 10,001,970 m). And measuring the meridian is an extremely long and expensive matter. One way or another, it became clear that we couldn’t do without this meter. Such an etapone was made of a platinum-plate alloy and was stored at the International Bureau of Weights and Measures in Paris.

Finally, in 1960, the General Conference on Metas and Libras defined the meter as 1,650,763.73 wavelengths of orge light emitted in the vacuum of krypton. So we returned to Christin Huygen’s idea - to take from nature itself an unchangeable and reproducible unit of length.
