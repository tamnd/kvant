" FOR THE SAKE OF THE FATHERLAND,
SCIENCES AND GLORY"
Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences
Y. P. SOLOVIEV

In the very center of the Latin Quarter in Paris, next to the Pantheon, the Sorbonne and the Louis the Great Museum, there is a small quiet street called Descartes. The house at number 5 on this street is famous all over the world - for more than a century and a half it has housed the famous Polytechnic School. Its history goes back to 1794 - the most heroic and tragic period of the Great French Revolution. The revolutionary upheaval affected all spheres of life in French society. Please, for the first time in history, political and social activities have become truly aware of the role of science, its influence on politics, industry and commerce. The revolutionary wars and the island's rivalry with England forced the government to take up the problem of centralized training of scientific personnel necessary to quickly and competently solve complex government problems. THERE ARE SEVERAL FAMOUS SCIENTISTS IN THE Governing Bodies of the REVOLUTIONARY STATE. The great geometer Gaspard Monge served as minister of the sea, the talented mathematician and mechanic Lazare Carnot was one of the founders of the armed forces of the republic and the military industry. They were the initiators of the creation of a new type of higher educational completion that glorified France.

In old France, higher education was in its basic state in 22 universities - outdated institutions, suppressed by a group of medieval traditions and scholasticism. The mathematical and physical sciences were in a particularly deplorable state. The only exceptions were a few privileged military engineering schools (School of Places and Roads, Mézières School of Military Engineers, School of Artillery Students), in which, in essence, the training of specialties in the exact sciences was concentrated. The most famous secondary school was the Mézières school, where G. Monge taught for many years and where L. Carnot was a graduate.

In the first years of the revolution, many higher educational institutions and secondary specialized schools were closed. At the end of 1793, the highest ortholegal authority - the National Convent - began to reorganize the education system in the country. By decree of the 29th Frimer of the Second Year of the Republic (December 19, 1793), free and compulsory primary education was introduced in the country.

Around the same time, Gaspard Monge made a report to the Committee of Public Safety with a proposal to create a new higher educational institution that would train specialists in mathematics and the exact sciences. In contrast to the ideal of the all-round harmonious development of the individual, which shimmered in the 18th century, it was assumed that the educational completion created should be specialized, focused on achieving possible results in mathematics, exact natural and technical sciences. Every ounce of influence on ambition, inspired by the prospect of lectricalizhevanny, must be brought into play in order to force students to work with maximum effort. The committee fully supported Monge and entrusted him, as well as Lambardi, Fourcroix, Carnot and Prieur, with the development of its project.
