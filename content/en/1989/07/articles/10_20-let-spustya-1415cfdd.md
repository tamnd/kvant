20 years later
(on the anniversary of the first lunar expedition)

Most of our readers were born when flights to the Moon had already become history, and they would probably find the incident described a century and a half ago in the newspaper Moskovskie Gubernie Vedomosti unjustified: for “seditious speeches about a flight to the Moon,” it was decided to exile the philistine Nikifor Nikitin to... the settlement of Baikonur!

But here is another, more recent, but also curious fact: just two years before the launch of the first artificial Earth satellite, the then president of the USSR Academy of Sciences asked several scientists to report on the possibility of implementing an artificial satellite. “I’m not interested in science fiction,” “I assume that the spacewalk will take place no earlier than the year 2000,” “I can’t imagine what practical significance artificial satellites can have,” these were the scientists’ answers.

Life, as always happens, not only refuted the doubts of skeptics, but also outstripped the hopes of optimists: just three and a half years later, Yuri Gagarin went into space after the first satellite, and eight years later, American astronauts landed on the Moon.

Here are just a few of the most important things on the way to the lunar expedition:

On July 16, 1969, the decisive assault began. On this day, the giant Saturn 5 rocket ensured the launch of the Lunar Odyssey. The Apollo 11 pod was commanded by N. Armstrong, the main block raft was M. Collins, and the lunar cabin raft (it had the name “Eagle”) was commanded by E. Aldrin. “According to the script,” Armstrong and Aldrin were supposed to land on the Moon, and Collins was supposed to wait for his goods in low-Earth orbit.

It took almost four days for the astronauts to “get” to the vicinity of the Moon. ...On July 19 at 16.00 Washington time at the Mission Control Center (Houston, USA), the flight director made the decision to begin landing on the Moon. At this time, neither he nor the astronauts themselves had any idea

how dramatic the next four hours would be. The radio was the first to fail - it went silent for 51 seconds. A little later, dispatchers discovered a new problem - in the Orel comploter. Was the computer making a mistake or was it receiving incorrect information?

At 16.07 Armstrong already noticed something unusual: craters in the porthole appeared 2-3 seconds behind schedule. However, the Control Center decided to continue the flight.

Another minute later, when Aldrin entered the landing radar data into the comploter, “the devil began”: the comploter, due to overload, did not accept the new input data.

After another 2 minutes, the second alarm sounded. The astronauts began to analyze the signals, but they were reassured and allowed to continue landing.

Less than four minutes remained until the program was completed when the flight director, asking the dispatchers for the last time, commanded: “Eagle, go for landing.” “I understand,” Aldrin answered calmly. “We’re going to land.” Height is 1000 meters." In the last minute, the comploter gave alarm signals three more times.

The Eagle was descending quickly. Aldrin recorded the altitude and rate of descent. At an altitude of about 125 meters, the descent introduced slowdowns - Armstrong finally smoothed out the surface under the cockpit, and what he saw scared even him (and he was an experienced test pilot, and his composure was well known to everyone): the “Eagle” was not descending to a level surface, as had been previously foreseen, but to the bottom of the crater, strewn with huge boulders. Slowing down, Armstrong moved the cabin forward, looking for a landing site. Time passed, but nothing suitable came across.

When there were thirty meters left to the surface, a new obstacle appeared: due to a strong jet of gas from a running engine, lunar dust rose. Unlike the earthly one, it did not settle. It seemed like there was a dense moving fog all around.

“60 seconds,” Earth warned, referring to the fuel supply (the minimum limit is 20 seconds). “Come on, come on!” - Aldrin encouraged his comrade.

"30 seconds." There were 10 seconds left before the command to stop the descent was accepted. And then the “Eagle” touched the lunar surface with its noises.

“There is lunar contact!” - Aldrin closed.

“Houston, this is Tranquility Base speaking. "Eagle" has landed on the moon! - Armstrong said excitedly...

Over the next few years, 5 more expeditions visited the Moon, and one attempt did not end tragically.

The USSR continued to explore the Moon using unmanned vehicles. In 1970, Luna-16, and later Luna-20 and Luna-24, delivered lunar soil to Earth. Two automatic self-propelled vehicles, Lunokhod-1 and Lunokhod-2, also worked on the Moon.

Over the past thirty-odd years of the space age, the “social status” of space flight has changed markedly. Today, the problems solved in these flights are wide and varied - issues of fundamental science and new technologies, the study of space objects and the study of specific physical and biological phenomena, and purely “applied” research...

How can we evaluate the first lunar expedition from these positions? The editors addressed this question to the coshonaut pilot of the USSR, Doctor of Technical Sciences K. P. Feoktistov. Let’s say right away that K.P.’s answer was somewhat unexpected for us. Moreover, it seems to us, the recording of this conversation will be of interest to our reader, because this is the view of a person who is directly related to the development of practical astronautics, in a sense, a person’s reflection on his work.
