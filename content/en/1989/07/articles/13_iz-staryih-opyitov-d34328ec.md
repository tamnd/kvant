From old experiences

Under this heading more than once we have been publicists about the occupied and at the same time revered old experiments (see «Kvant»: 1982, No. 10; 1983, No. 5; 1984, No. 8; 1985, No. 7).

For our readers' seasonal inspiration, we offer excerpts from J. Perry's book "The Rotating Drawbar", published in Russian in 1935. The book is based on a lecture given by the author, English professor of physics John Perry, to the public, containing a popular presentation of the Voluk theory and accompanied by beautiful demonstrations. The material is published with minor editorial cuts and changes.

Think carefully - and you will admit that the behavior of the common wolf is extremely surprising. If he doesn’t believe it, then, as you see, he immediately topples, and I am not able to keep him balanced on the tip. But it has completely different properties when rotating: not only does it not fall, but on the contrary, when I push it, it shows amazing resistance and even takes on a more and more vertical position.

As soon as we awaken our interest in scientific observation, we will notice that nature will overcome phenomena of this kind for us in large numbers.

Those of you who have had to spin a rope quickly know that fast movement imparts a kind of “stiffness” to flexible and even liquid bodies. Here, for example, is a circle made of very thin paper (Fig. 1); if I bring it into rapid rotation, it resists behind my hand or the blow of my fist, as if it were a circle of steel. You can hear what it sounds like when I hit it with a stick. Where did his flexibility go?

Here is another ring-shaped target, completely flexible (Fig. 2). Everyone thinks that it can be made to stand like a rigid hoop, but meanwhile, if

Every body has three axes around which it can rotate in a balanced state, without revealing tendencies to pack in different directions, but in two of these three cases this equilibrium is still unstable, and there is only one axis around which a perfect stable and balanced rotation can take place. In addition, a rotating body, left to itself, after a more or less long time, eventually begins to rotate around this axis, if only there is friction to calm its swing.

To make what was said above clear, I will bring the body into rotation in such a way that allows it to take as the axis of rotation that main axis, the top whose rotation turns out to be the most stable. Various bodies can be suspended on a thread, and I can impart rotation to the wheel to which this thread is attached (Fig. 8). You see that circle (a) first rotates quite calmly around the axis AA; but soon you notice how he begins to pack a little. The swings are calming, and the circle rotates calmly and steadily around the BB, since this is the most important of its three main axes.

Cone (b) also rotates at first quietly around axis AA; but then a swing begins, which becomes stronger and stronger, and finally, the cone begins to rotate calmly around around around BB in the same way - the most important of its three main axes.

Although the tendency of the top to assume a vertical position has been well known since it was launched for the first time, I still asked everyone in this room if they knew the explanation for this phenomenon, and I even doubt that there are many people anywhere who know it. Any serious mathematician will tell you that the explanation can probably be found in print from Routh (the author of a very famous English textbook on mechanics) or that he at least knows people in Cambridge who, without a doubt, know this explanation. Perhaps he also thinks that he himself once knew it, but that now he has forgotten those difficult mathematical proofs on which he previously refined his mind. This problem was actually solved by Sir William Thomson and Professor Blackburn when they spent a whole year on the seashore preparing for an examination in mathematics. Probably, anyone interested in Thomson's work will be perplexed to learn that this scientist and his friend spent their holidays on the seashore, engaged in making all kinds of round stones that he collected on the shore spin.

Now I will show you an amazing phenomenon that Thomson was puzzling over at that time. Let the ellipsoid (Fig. 9) be a stone polished with water. It lies on the table in a very stable position. I bring him into a fast spinning motion. You see that for a second or two it tends to rotate about the axis AA, but then begins to wobble violently; when these swings stop after some time, then it calmly rotates around the axis BB, which has now assumed a vertical position; but then a series of quickly subdued swings begins again; when they stop, you notice that the ellipsoid finally comes into a state of stable rotation, becomes vertical to the longest of its axes - CC. I hung several glasses here (see the intro to the article): one (a) filled with sand, another (b) with syrup, a third (c) with oil, four (d) with water, and a fifth (e) let. Look now: I twist the wires on which the glasses are, and then I leave them to their own devices - an oscillatory movement begins, similar to the swinging pendulum of a pocket watch. Notice now that the glass of water moves very quickly and the duration of the oscillation is almost the same for this glass and for the empty one, that is, the water does not seem to move with the glass. You will also notice that this glass continues to oscillate for a significant period of time.

On the contrary, a glass filled with sand oscillates slowly; in this case, the pitch and the glass together represent a kind of solid body, and the vibration lasts a long time.

In glasses filled with oil and syrup, the periods of oscillation are longer than in a glass with water or empty, but shorter than would be the case if the oscillating bodies were completely solid, since the oil or syrup only partially participates in the movement. But since the liquid only partially participates in oscillations, tremors appear inside the liquid itself, which lead to the fact that the oscillations die out faster than if the glass is empty or filled with sand.

A boiled egg (g) and an unboiled egg (f), which are suspended in the same way on a wire, will show the same difference in the properties of oscillatory motion, as do two bodies, one of which is solid inside and the other liquid; you see how much slower the vibrations of a boiled egg are than those of an unboiled egg.

Further here, on this table, you can easily detect the difference between a boiled and unboiled egg. If you twist both eggs, you see that the unboiled egg stops much earlier than the boiled one, since the first one comes to a state of rest earlier due to internal tremors.

Now watch carefully and understand the completely missing sign by which you can tell whether an egg is boiled or not. I twist the egg or rotate it around a vertical axis, and then hold it with my finger for just one moment, just long enough to stop the movement of the shell. You see that the boiled egg has only made the movements of the shell, but the liquid contents not only continue to move, but even resume the movements of the shell as soon as I remove my finger from it.

At the top of the column, we want the mortar to absorb moisture from the air, which may be as low as 20% relative humidity. The solution must therefore be quite saturated: the relative increase in saturated vapor pressure $p$ above the solution (in our case 80%) is proportional to its concentration (Raoult’s law). That's why

$$\frac{p}{p_0} = \frac{80}{100} \approx \frac{n}{(N+n)},$$

where $p_0$ is the saturated vapor pressure above pure water. This condition is satisfied starting from $n=4N$, whence the minimum height of the liquid column is

$$h = \frac{RT}{g} \frac{4N}{(NM+4N\mu)} = \frac{4RT}{(M+4\mu)g}.$$

Take $T=300$ K and find $h$ for the three above-mentioned compositions. The molecular weight of water is 18, i.e. $M = 0,018$ kg/mol. For sulfuric acid $\mu = 0,098$ kg/mol, for sugar syrup $\mu = 0,342$ kg/mol, for liquid polyethylene oxide $\mu = 5$ kg/mol. Corresponding height of the liquid column is 2430 m (sulfuric acid); 720 m (sugar syrup), 50 m (polyethylene oxide). It is clear that the choice should be polyethylene oxide pastes.
